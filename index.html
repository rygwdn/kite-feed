  • AI researchers target agent reliability and robotics failures

    AI 2026-07-02
    A set of AI research papers and developer reports published around July 2 identify a common failure pattern across agentic systems, reinforcement learning and robotics: models often break down when they must operate over long horizons, use tools, adapt to changing environments or act with incomplete information. The work spans language-model agents, GUI agents, robot policies, multi-agent systems, reward models and autonomous driving, with several papers proposing constrained execution, structured memory, recovery loops or verifiable rewards rather than larger models alone [10][18][28][31][58][60]. Several studies reported concrete evaluations on reliability and efficiency. ChainCaps, a tool-use safety framework, reduced attack success from 25%-68% to 0%-4.8% across 82 tasks while preserving 96%-100% benign completion under its stated assumptions [58]. HydraCollab reported using 41% of the bandwidth on V2X-R and 26% on V2X-Radar compared with Where2comm while slightly improving performance [14]. FAR, a robot retry framework, reported average success-rate gains of 17.6% in simulation and 11.7% in real-world manipulation tasks over a standard diffusion policy [18]. The papers and reports also described limits in current evaluation. GUI-Perturbed found GUI grounding models that report more than 85% accuracy on standard benchmarks can drop 27-56 percentage points when instructions require spatial reasoning rather than direct element naming [22].   • Webb detects atmosphere around white dwarf planet WD 1856 b

    Science 2026-07-02
    Astronomers using the NASA/ESA/CSA James Webb Space Telescope observed an atmosphere around WD 1856 b, a Jupiter-sized exoplanet orbiting the white dwarf WD 1856+534 about 80 light-years from Earth, according to results published in Nature on July 1 [1][8][7][10]. The observations mark the first atmospheric detection for a giant planet orbiting a white dwarf and were made as the planet transited its host star [1][8][18]. WD 1856 b orbits every 34 hours at a separation of less than 3 million kilometers, about 50 times closer than Earth is to the Sun. Its host star had previously expanded into a red giant before becoming a white dwarf [8][9][11][14]. Researchers reported methane, aerosols and hydrocarbons in the atmosphere, estimated the planet’s mass at roughly 4.3 to 10.9 times Jupiter’s mass, and found temperatures of about 117°C to 139°C, hotter than expected for a planet of its size [3][2][11]. The team concluded that WD 1856 b likely could not have occupied its present close orbit before the star’s red-giant phase because it would have been destroyed. Webb observations helped constrain how the planet migrated inward after the star became a white dwarf [8][9][12][13]. The system gives researchers a case study for what may happen to outer planets in Sun-like systems after their stars become white dwarfs billions of years from now [3][7][16].

  • Researchers identify Antarctica's first dinosaur fossil as a titanosaur

    Science 2026-07-02
    Researchers have identified a fossil collected on Antarctica's James Ross Island in 1985 as a tail vertebra from a titanosaurian sauropod, making it the first known dinosaur bone found on the continent [1][2][10][11]. The specimen, cataloged as BAS D.8621.25, came from the Santa Marta Formation and dates to the Late Cretaceous Campanian; sources place its age at roughly 78 million to 83 million years [2][8][1]. British Antarctic Survey geologist Michael Thomson and German paleontologist Reinhard Förster collected the vertebra on December 9, 1985, while mapping rock layers and gathering fossils on the Antarctic Peninsula, but researchers at the time treated it as a large reptile or marine reptile specimen rather than a dinosaur [2][3][4][10]. The new analysis, published June 29 in Acta Palaeontologica Polonica, compared the bone's shape with better-known dinosaur remains and assigned it to Titanosauria, a group of long-necked, plant-eating sauropods that included some of the largest known land animals [1][2][7][12]. The fossil is only the second sauropod body fossil reported from Antarctica, where dinosaur remains are scarce because much of the continent is covered by ice [2][8][4]. Researchers said the animal lived when Antarctica supported temperate forests rather than today's ice sheet, and Paul Barrett of London's Natural History Museum noted the fossil's significance for the history of Antarctic exploration [1][2].
