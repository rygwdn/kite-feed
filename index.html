  • Triassic armored amphibian fossils point to life entirely at sea

    Science 2026-10-27
    Researchers have described Gerrothorax maritimus, an armored amphibian that may have lived its entire life in the sea about 237 million years ago. The claim rests on two nearly complete skeletons found in marine deposits in Guizhou, China, more than 30 kilometers from nearby islands. Their preserved limbs and strontium isotope signatures are consistent with the animals having lived and grown in seawater [4][5][8]. The study appeared in Nature Ecology & Evolution on Oct. 26 [1]. The roughly 74-centimeter-long animal had a flattened body covered in bony armor. Researchers propose that the armor limited water exchange through its skin, while its gills and digestive tract may have helped it cope with salt. Its skull shape suggests it waited on the seafloor and sucked in prey [2][1]. No living amphibian is known to live exclusively in the ocean, and fewer than 2% of modern amphibian species show any salt tolerance [4][6].

  • Lumina simulation traces early universe’s hydrogen and helium reionization

    Science 2026-10-27
    Researchers using Lumina, a supercomputer simulation of the universe’s first 2 billion years, report that early stars and galaxies drove hydrogen reionization, while energetic radiation from active galactic nuclei helped reionize helium [3][1][10]. The model places the start of helium reionization around redshift 6, its midpoint near redshift 4 and near-completion by redshift 3, when singly ionized helium occupied less than 1% of the simulated volume [1]. Stars’ ultraviolet light could strip electrons from hydrogen, while higher-energy radiation from actively feeding supermassive black holes could drive helium’s later transition [3][1]. Lumina follows both processes in one model, allowing their effects to shape the simulated universe as it evolves [6][13]. The team says researchers can compare Lumina’s predictions with observations from the James Webb Space Telescope and other instruments. It plans to use the simulation to investigate gas flows between galaxies and the evolution of large-scale cosmic structure [3][6].

  • Animal blood colors reflect distinct oxygen-carrying proteins and adaptations

    Science 2026-10-27
    Animal blood can be blue, violet, green or white because species use different oxygen-transport proteins or pigments, according to a Knowable Magazine feature republished by Nautilus and ZME Science [1][2]. Octopuses, squid and crustaceans use copper-containing hemocyanin. Some marine worms and brachiopods use purple hemerythrin, while New Guinea lizards accumulate green biliverdin [1][2]. Antarctic blackfin icefish lack hemoglobin and red blood cells, relying instead on oxygen dissolved in their blood and enlarged hearts and capillaries [1][2]. Vampire bats are the only known mammals that live exclusively on blood; specialized kidneys and gut microbes help them handle the diet [3].
