  • Researchers advance guardrails for autonomous AI agents

    AI 2026-08-19
    New research papers, developer posts and open-source projects published Aug. 18-19 focused on securing AI agents that can call tools, change files, send messages, modify databases, execute code and initiate financial transactions [3][4][5][7]. The sources frame agent safety as an execution-control problem, not just a prompt-writing problem: Google said an agent connected to live databases and internal APIs is “mutating production state,” while an arXiv paper on Aegis said prompt-level governance can shape model behavior but does not create an execution boundary [4][3]. Several proposals put runtime mediation between model outputs and real-world actions. Aegis treats model outputs as action proposals and routes them through a trusted decision layer before tool execution; PACE applies transaction-level authorization to DeFi agents using typed intents, deterministic policy verification and signed Policy Decision Records; TRUSS evaluates generated agent skills with static checks plus shadow-agent execution traces before deployment [3][5][10]. Community projects described similar containment patterns, including AgentZ’s default-deny sandbox with kernel-level network blocking and a credential proxy that avoids exposing API keys to agents [7].

  • X-ray study explains narwhal tusk’s straight spiral

    Science 2026-08-19
    An international research team reported in Nature Communications that the narwhal’s tusk, long known for its straight, spiraling form, is made of two structures that twist in opposite directions rather than a single spiral [7][9][8]. The work examined the dental tissues of the tusk, a modified canine tooth that in male narwhals grows through the jaw and upper lip and can exceed two meters in length [6][9][8]. The researchers used 3D X-ray methods, including synchrotron imaging at three European particle accelerators, to study the tusk from microscopic to molecular scales [8][17]. They found that the inner dentin forms a right-handed spiral while the outer cementum forms a left-handed spiral. The team said the counter-rotating arrangement helps explain how the tusk stays straight while displaying its distinctive left-handed external twist [6][8][17]. Henrik Birkedal, a chemist at Aarhus University and senior author of the study, said the consistent handedness pattern in narwhal tusks appears unusual among biological structures [7]. The tusk’s function remains debated, with proposed roles including sexual signaling, sensing water conditions and fighting, but Popular Science reported that evidence remains limited for several of those explanations [7].
