  • Vienna and Beijing teams demonstrate first working thorium nuclear clocks

    Science 2026-10-08
    Teams in Vienna and Beijing have built the first working nuclear clocks, using lasers to drive an energy transition in thorium-229 nuclei embedded in calcium fluoride crystals [7][9]. The devices use nuclear transitions as a timing reference rather than the electron transitions used by conventional atomic clocks. Both are prototypes and neither yet surpasses the best atomic clocks, which can lose or gain only about one second over billions of years [7][9]. The Tsinghua University clock in Beijing showed about six times lower frequency instability than the Vienna system. Measurements from two independently produced Chinese crystals agreed at the 10^-13 level [9][15]. Vienna researchers also used their clock to search for ultralight dark matter. The experiment found no evidence of it, but constrained possible dark-matter interactions at a level comparable to results from leading atomic clocks [9]. Researchers say their next steps are to improve laser power, crystal quality and signal-to-noise performance. The teams’ studies appeared in Nature on Oct. 7. Proposed future uses include navigation, communications and precision tests of fundamental physics [7][9].
