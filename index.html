  • OpenAI adds invisible text watermarks to ChatGPT and Codex in EU

    Technology 2026-10-06

API customers worldwide can opt in for selected models now, but the feature will remain off by default outside the EU [2][15]. OpenAI says the detector is imperfect: in 400-token passages, replacing 10% of words with synonyms reduced the detection rate from about 92% to 66%, and replacing 25% reduced it to 17%; shorter passages and mathematics are also harder to identify [2][4]. A detected watermark does not identify a user, establish how much a person contributed or verify accuracy, while failure to detect one does not prove human authorship [15][6]. OpenAI will initially limit detector access to approved researchers and expert organizations as it evaluates reliability [15][2].

  • Three scientists win Nobel for light-based control of brain cells

    Science 2026-10-06
    Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine on Oct. 5 for discoveries behind optogenetics, which lets researchers switch selected nerve cells on or off with light [1][2][7]. The award recognizes a method that lets scientists test whether particular neural circuits cause behaviors, rather than simply observing which brain areas are active during those behaviors [10][27]. Hegemann and Nagel identified channelrhodopsins, light-sensitive proteins first studied in green algae; Deisseroth then used the proteins’ genetic instructions to make mammalian neurons respond to light, demonstrating control in living mice [10][26][38]. Researchers now use the technique to study circuits involved in memory, behavior and disease, while clinical uses remain limited: an experimental approach has partially restored sight in a patient with retinitis pigmentosa [10][27]. The three laureates will share 12 million Swedish kronor, and the award ceremony is scheduled for Dec. 10 in Stockholm [14][45][46].

  • Astronomers identify candidate planet that may have formed from white dwarf’s stellar remains

    Science 2026-10-06
    Astronomers have found evidence of a possible second-generation planet around the white dwarf HS 0209+0832 that may have formed from material expelled when the star died [6][1]. The candidate appears to be a Jupiter-sized gas giant in a close orbit. Researchers say this is the first indication of such a planet around a white dwarf, though it has not been confirmed [6][4]. The clue emerged when researchers re-examined Hubble observations from 1999. They found unusually high levels of heavy elements, including niobium, in the white dwarf’s atmosphere. Niobium had not previously been reported in a white dwarf, and its presence points to matter produced during the star’s final stages [6][12]. NASA’s TESS satellite detected a faint variation in brightness repeating every 4.4 days, consistent with a planet orbiting about 6 million kilometres from the star. The team thinks radiation from the still-hot white dwarf is stripping the planet’s atmosphere, with some material falling onto the star. Researchers plan to use Hubble to study how such planets form and how common they are [6][8].
