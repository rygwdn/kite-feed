  • Stanford researchers create 16 AI-designed bacterial viruses

    AI 2026-08-07
    Researchers at Stanford University and the Arc Institute used genome language models to design complete viral genomes, then synthesized and tested them in laboratory bacteria, producing 16 viable viruses not found in nature [2][16][18][24][50].   • Researchers target LLM costs, benchmarks and security risks

    AI 2026-08-07
    A wave of AI papers and engineering posts published or surfaced this week focuses on reducing large language model costs and latency and improving evaluation, rather than only increasing model size. The material includes an engineering breakdown of vLLM’s high-throughput inference stack, a first-principles model for LLM inference performance, and an LLM harness design that replaces always-pasted context with indexed, on-demand payloads to reduce repeated prompt costs [1][2][6]. Several papers examine how budgets and orchestration shape model performance. Token-budget-aware reasoning dynamically adjusts reasoning length by problem complexity, CostAda allocates inference-time search effort by cost-calibrated frontier utility, TIPEX coordinates two kinds of multi-agent parallelism, and HarnessOpt-Bench evaluates LLMs as optimizers of the prompts, tools, control flow, memory and orchestration code around other agents [3][5][10][7]. Other studies focus on measurement problems: SciCode-Verified reports that defects in an existing scientific-coding benchmark suppressed scores, GB/T-Bench measures rule-intensive review of national standards, and LangChoiceBench tests programming-language choice in project-level code generation [9][12][16]. Other papers cover security and reliability. SparSEEty describes a token-extraction attack against sparsity-exploiting LLM serving systems, including an instantiation inside an Intel TDX confidential virtual machine, while a self-consistency position paper argues that failures such as sycophancy, incomplete logical generalization and confident incorrect answers cannot be fully detected through isolated single-output evaluation [4][17].

  • Study links dietary sugars to human brain evolution

    Science 2026-08-07
    Researchers from the University of Sydney and the University of Glasgow reported in Science that natural dietary sugars from fruit, honey and other sweet foods may have helped fuel human brain expansion over roughly four million years of evolution [7][13][5][8][11]. The paper says sugar, along with animal foods, belongs in explanations of hominin brain growth [3][6][2][12]. The study modeled glucose demands in humans and their ancestors and compared them with likely carbohydrate content in diets ranging from chimpanzee-like, plant-heavy diets to those of modern warm-climate hunter-gatherers [3]. Sci.News said the authors described hominin brain mass as increasing nearly fivefold over the past 4 million years, from about 300 grams in Australopithecus afarensis to roughly 1,500 grams in modern Homo sapiens [3]. The human brain accounts for about 2% of adult body weight but uses about one fifth of resting energy, and a five-year-old child devotes 66% of survival energy needs to the brain [3][14]. The researchers said glucose is the brain’s preferred fuel and that the body can make some glucose from other sources, but only through a limited, inefficient process [3][1]. Before cooking made starch-rich foods such as tubers and grains easier to digest, natural sugars may have supplied accessible carbohydrate energy [5][15].
