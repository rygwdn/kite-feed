Study links Egyptian princesses’ bones to weapon training

Category: Science

A study published July 17 in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology reexamined skeletal remains from six royal individuals, including five royal women, buried at Dahshur during Egypt’s Middle Kingdom roughly 3,800 to 4,000 years ago [3][5][7][8]. Researchers led by Zeinab Hashesh of Egypt’s Beni-Suef University reported pronounced muscle-attachment sites and changes in arm and hand bones, which they interpreted as consistent with repeated archery, hunting and weapon handling [2][3][4][7][10]. The findings address a long-running question over whether bows, arrows, maces and daggers placed in some royal women’s tombs were ceremonial objects or tools used in life [2][4][5][7]. The interpretation remains disputed: Live Science reported that bioarchaeologists not involved in the study urged caution about linking muscle-attachment patterns directly to archery or military training [3]. The paper was published on July 17, 2026 [2][3][7][10].

They carried their bows and daggers into the afterlife not just as ornaments, but as testaments to the active, resilient and powerful lives they led

— Zeinab Hashesh (New Scientist)

Did you know: Some of the rediscovered bones were still wrapped in 1890s newspaper when researchers found them in museum storage [11].

Key Points

  • Princess Ita: Princess Ita, who died at about age 28 to 34, was buried with an elaborate dagger decorated with gold, lapis lazuli and turquoise [5][8].
  • Royal identities: Four of the women are identified or provisionally identified as Princess Ita, Princess Khenmet, Princess Itaweret and Sathathormeryt, daughters of pharaoh Amenemhat II [10][2][5].
  • Health evidence: The study also reported healed fractures, signs of persistent infection and possible malnutrition, indicating that elite status did not prevent injury or disease [7][11].

Perspectives

Zeinab Hashesh: Hashesh said the remains had been poorly cataloged after earlier museum handling, telling ScienceAlert that early curators gave the entire box one number and described it only as human remains.

Sources: ScienceAlert

Outside bioarchaeologists: Bioarchaeologist Siân Halcrow of the University of Otago told Live Science, 'You cannot go from muscle marking to specific activity,' and said bone changes can have many causes beyond a single activity [3].

Sources: Live Science

The Conversation analysis: The article described the popular image of armed Egyptian royal women as more dramatic than historical, while saying the new skeletal work suggests there may be an element of truth to it.

Sources: The Conversation

Timeline

  • 1890s: Jacques de Morgan excavated the Dahshur royal remains [1][2][10].
  • 1895: Investigators examined King Hor and Princess Noub-Hotep [1].
  • 1915: The Dahshur bodies reached the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir [1].
  • 2020: Hashesh rediscovered the boxed remains during a curation project [1][2][10].
  • 17 July 2026: The team published its findings in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology [3][7].

Scientific Significance

  • Study interpretation: The researchers said the findings challenge traditional assumptions that royal women led idle, luxurious lives, instead suggesting they trained and took part in physically demanding activities [9].
  • Uncertainty: Outside experts caution that bone changes can result from factors such as genetics and nutrition, so the claimed link to archery should be viewed as suggestive rather than conclusive [3].
  • Limitations: Because the group includes only six royal individuals from one burial context, broader claims about women’s training across ancient Egypt would require comparison with more female and male skeletons from different sites and social ranks [3][common].

Historical Background

Dahshur is a royal necropolis south of modern Cairo associated with major pyramid complexes from Egypt’s Middle Kingdom [8]. Amenemhat II’s White Pyramid was named for its bright Tura limestone casing, much of which was later removed and reused as building material [8][12]. The royal women in the study belonged to the Twelfth Dynasty, a powerful Middle Kingdom ruling line [8].
