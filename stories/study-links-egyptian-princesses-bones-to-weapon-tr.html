Study links Egyptian princesses’ bones to weapon training

Category: Science

A new study in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology examined six royal individuals from Egypt’s Middle Kingdom burial complex at Dahshur, including five female mummies tied to royal tombs dating to roughly 1850–1700 BC, nearly 4,000 years ago [1][3][7][10]. Researchers led by Zeinab Hashesh of Beni-Suef University reported upper-body skeletal changes and pronounced muscle-attachment sites, which they linked to repeated archery and possible handling of weapons such as daggers or maces [3][7][5][14]. The findings address a long-running question about whether bows, arrows, maces and daggers found in some royal women’s tombs were symbolic grave goods or items used in life [5][8][10][12]. Princess Ita, who was buried with an ornate gold-and-lapis-lazuli dagger, showed upper-body bone features that the researchers interpreted as consistent with weapon use [1][14].

Primary Source: View original article

Members of the royal family, especially the women, were active participants in skilled, physically demanding activities such as archery and hunting

— Zeinab Hashesh (Live Science)

Did you know: Some rediscovered bones still had 19th-century handwriting and wrapping papers from their early excavation history [11].

Key Points

  • Royal group: Sources identify the women as including Princesses Ita, Khenmet, Itaweret and Noub-Hotep, with four described as daughters or sisters linked to Pharaoh Amenemhat II [1][8][12][16].
  • Health evidence: The skeletons also showed signs of cared-for trauma, persistent infection and possible malnutrition, suggesting royal status did not eliminate physical hardship [7][13][19].

Perspectives

Zeinab Hashesh and research team: Hashesh said royal women took part in physically demanding activities including archery and hunting, and the team argued the tomb weapons were not merely ceremonial.

Sources: Live Science

Outside bioarchaeologists: Researchers who were not involved in the study cautioned that muscle-attachment changes can have more than one explanation and should not be treated as direct proof of combat or military roles.

Sources: Live Science

The Conversation analysis: The analysis said skeletal data can be used to test interpretations of richly furnished burials, rather than relying only on the symbolism of grave goods.

Sources: The Conversation

Timeline

  • 1894: Jacques de Morgan excavated Dahshur royal remains [16]
  • 1895: Early examinations studied King Hor and Princess Noub-Hotep [11]
  • 1915: Dahshur bodies reached the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir [11]
  • 2020: Hashesh rediscovered boxed remains during museum curation work [1][11]
  • 17 July 2026: Study appeared in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology [3][8]

Historical Background

The Dahshur burials belong to Egypt’s Middle Kingdom, a period that included the 12th Dynasty and the reign of Pharaoh Amenemhat II [12][14]. French archaeologist Jacques de Morgan excavated the royal remains in 1894 and 1895. Later museum handling separated some skulls from bodies for study before the skulls were lost [11][16].
