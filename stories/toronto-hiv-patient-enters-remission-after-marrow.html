Toronto HIV patient enters remission after marrow transplant

Category: Canada

Doctors in Toronto say a man who had lived with HIV for 27 years has no detectable virus after receiving a bone marrow transplant from a donor with a natural resistance to HIV. The procedure was done to treat his cancer, not the virus itself [1][2][3]. Clinicians described the patient as being in remission and said he could be considered cured if he remains free of the virus through 2027 [1][3]. The case adds another hopeful chapter to a very small number of similar HIV remission cases worldwide. Doctors also stressed that bone marrow transplants are not a standard HIV treatment because they are high-risk procedures usually reserved for serious blood cancers [2][3]. For readers in Canada, the development stands out as an encouraging step in care and research, even if it remains a highly specialized outcome rather than a broadly available cure [1][2].

  • Cancer treatment: Doctors performed the transplant to treat the patient's cancer, and the HIV remission followed after they used stem cells from a donor naturally resistant to the virus [3][1].

Historical Background

The first widely reported HIV remission after a stem-cell transplant involved the 'Berlin patient' [common]. Since then, doctors have documented only a small number of comparable cases worldwide, usually involving patients who needed transplants for blood cancers and received donor cells that carried resistance to HIV [common]. That history matters here because Toronto clinicians are placing this Canadian case on the same rare and closely watched medical path [2][3].
