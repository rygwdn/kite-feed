COP31 science dispute threatens timing of 2028 global climate assessment

Category: Science

A dispute over when to publish the next assessment from the UN’s Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which reviews climate science for policymakers, could leave its findings unavailable for the 2028 global review of emissions-cutting efforts. The European Union, Brazil, the United Kingdom and vulnerable island states want the report published in 2028; Russia, Iran, India and Saudi Arabia have argued for 2029, after that review. The IPCC’s latest plenary was put on hold in October, and delegates at COP31 in Antalya have also been divided over its budget and schedule [8][2][23]. Nearly 200 countries missed Friday’s scheduled close of the UN climate talks and continued negotiating in Turkey. A revised draft agreement omitted fossil fuels and included support for carbon capture and storage. Some governments and climate specialists objected that the technology could prolong fossil-fuel use; a new draft was expected Saturday [17][6][13]. Researchers say steep emissions cuts remain essential as warming is expected to exceed 1.5C, while carbon dioxide removal could be needed to bring temperatures down later. The draft’s references to capture targets were still under negotiation, and scientists and analysts cited at the summit warned that current removal capacity and project delivery fall far short of proposed levels [25][6][28].

Key Points

  • Assessment schedule: The IPCC publishes assessments every six to seven years. An October open letter signed by more than 100 researchers from 36 countries said they were committed to delivering a rigorous, timely and inclusive seventh report; several countries have called for publication in 2028 [8].
  • Research funding: IPCC budget disagreements include travel support for hundreds of volunteer scientists, particularly researchers from poorer countries; a delegation member warned, “No budget means no more IPCC” [8].
  • Removal scale: The UN Environment Programme’s Inger Andersen said the world needs to remove 320 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide this century to bring warming back down, equivalent to eight years of current global emissions [28].
  • Technology distinction: Atmospheric carbon dioxide removal takes CO₂ from the open air, while carbon capture, utilization and storage (CCUS) captures emissions at facilities such as power plants and factories; critics note that CCUS has also been used to inject CO₂ into oil reservoirs to boost production [25].
  • Electrification context: The International Energy Agency estimated that developing countries could save about $100 billion on fossil-fuel imports by 2035 through electrification, while the disputed summit target remains voluntary and does not require renewable electricity [8][17].

Perspectives

European Union and countries seeking a 2028 IPCC report: They say the panel’s role as an independent source of current climate evidence must be protected. EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said safeguarding its role as the source of the best available science was fundamental [8].

Timeline

  • 2015: Paris Agreement established the 1.5C warming goal [8][17]
  • 2023: IPCC published its most recent full climate assessment [8]
  • October 2026: IPCC plenary was put on hold at several countries’ urging [8]
  • 2028: Global stocktake is scheduled to review emissions-cutting efforts [8][2]

Technical Details

  • CCUS capacity gap: Climate Analytics expert Neil Grant said existing global projects could capture about 60 million tonnes of CO₂ annually if all ran at capacity, but in practice operate at roughly half that level [6].
  • Carbon removal cost: Berkeley Earth scientist Zeke Hausfather estimated that removing enough carbon to lower global temperatures by 0.1C would cost about $22 trillion. Returning below 1.5C after a 0.2C–0.3C overshoot would cost an estimated $44 trillion to $66 trillion by 2100 [28].

Scientific Significance

  • Political impact: The timetable dispute does not change the scientific findings themselves, but researchers warned that it could delay their influence on negotiations and policy [8].
  • Overshoot response: Bill Hare cautioned that without policy changes, no available carbon-removal approach could offset a pathway to at least 2.7C of warming by 2100 [28].
  • Evidence and uncertainty: Researchers cautioned that carbon dioxide removal’s climate effectiveness and impacts on nature and people remain uncertain [6][25].

Historical Background

At COP28 in Dubai in 2023, countries agreed to transition away from fossil fuels without setting a timetable or targets [1][17].
