Spain’s snap election pits Sánchez against conservatives as Vox seeks kingmaker role

Category: World

Nearly 39 million Spaniards are set to vote on Nov. 29 in an early general election that could end Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s eight years in office and bring the far-right Vox party into national government for the first time since Spain’s return to democracy [2][8][13]. Voters will elect the 350-member Congress and part of the Senate; parliament chooses the prime minister [8]. The conservative People’s Party (PP), led by Alberto Núñez Feijóo, leads most polls, followed closely by Sánchez’s Spanish Socialist Workers’ Party (PSOE), with Vox, led by Santiago Abascal, in third place. Poll averages put the PP at about 31.5%, the Socialists at 29% and Vox at 17.9%; projections suggest the PP may need Vox’s support to reach a majority [1][6][20]. Sánchez called the election after parliament rejected two housing measures on Oct. 2. His minority government has faced pressure over housing costs, living expenses, immigration and corruption investigations involving people close to him; the Bank of Spain estimates a shortage of about 700,000 homes [2][11][3]. After the vote count, parties must negotiate to form a government. A candidate for prime minister needs 176 votes in the first investiture ballot or more votes for than against in a second [8].

Key Points

  • Cost pressures: Spain’s inflation rate reached 4.9% in October, while average real wages fell 0.3% between 2019 and 2025; consumer confidence has dropped from 107 when Sánchez took office to 84.1 in the latest cited survey [7].
  • Youth discontent: Vox leads among voters under 35 in recent surveys, while just over one-third of households headed by someone under 35 owned a home in 2024, down from nearly 70% in 2011 [3].
  • Migration debate: Foreign nationals accounted for 3.7 million Social Security contributors in October, 16.4% of the total workforce, with substantial shares in hospitality, agriculture, construction and domestic work [22].
  • Seat mechanics: Spain allocates Congress seats across 50 provinces and Ceuta and Melilla using closed party lists and the D’Hondt method; smaller parties generally need more than the formal 3% threshold to win seats in low-seat districts [8][13].
  • Foreign policy: The PP favors stronger ties with Washington and higher defense investment, while Sánchez has resisted U.S. use of Spanish bases for offensive operations against Iran [1].

Perspectives

Pedro Sánchez and the PSOE: Sánchez has made housing reform central to his campaign, calling for more homes—especially social housing—and regulation; he has also framed the choice as whether Spain should remain open and socially progressive or move toward the far right [13][8].

Timeline

  • June 2018: Pedro Sánchez became prime minister after a no-confidence vote ousted Mariano Rajoy [13][12]
  • 23 July 2023: PP won most seats but failed to form a government [1][2]
  • 2 October 2026: Parliament rejected two emergency housing decrees [20][1]
  • 5 October 2026: Sánchez called an early election after the housing defeat [13][20]
  • 11 November 2026: Unions staged Spain’s first general strike since 2012 [13][2]
  • 29 November 2026: Voters are scheduled to choose a new parliament [2][8]

Industry Impact

  • Banking: The outgoing government extended the banking tax for one year, a measure expected to raise €1.4 billion for housing policies [23].
  • Utilities: The PP has proposed extending nuclear-reactor lifespans without setting a timetable, which analysts identified as a potential positive for nuclear plants and grid operators Iberdrola and Endesa [23].
  • Equities: The IBEX 35 entered the election among Europe’s stronger-performing markets and was on track for a fourth consecutive annual gain, while domestic banks and utilities face exposure to possible tax and regulatory changes [15].

Historical Background

The PSOE’s agreement with the Catalan separatist party Junts included an amnesty law for people facing legal action over the 2017 independence bid [11]. Junts later withdrew its backing after accusing Sánchez’s Socialists of breaking promises [6].

Business Angle

  • The PP has signaled possible gradual sales of the state’s 18% CaixaBank stake and 10% Telefónica holding, creating a potential ownership and governance change for both companies [23].
  • A Vox role in government could make policy less predictable for companies exposed to immigration, trade or European Union rules [10][23].

International Reactions

  • 🇧🇷 Brazil: President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva publicly said a Sánchez victory would be important during a Madrid visit on Nov. 16 [12].
  • 🇺🇸 United States: President Donald Trump has threatened tariffs against Spain and suggested the country could be expelled from NATO after Sánchez rejected the alliance’s proposed higher defense-spending target [1].

Q&A

Q: When will the newly elected chambers first meet?

A: Spain’s newly elected Congress and Senate are scheduled to hold their constitutive session on Dec. 23 [13].

Q: How quickly could a new prime minister take office?

A: Government formation can take months. The candidate tasked by the king must win an investiture vote; if no candidate secures sufficient support after the second ballot, another election is called [8].

Q: Could Vox enter a national government directly?

A: Feijóo has not ruled out working with Vox, but whether the parties form a cabinet coalition or agree to support each other in parliament depends on the seat count and negotiations after election day [2][8].
