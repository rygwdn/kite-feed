Carney says Trump has not raised Pentagon's Canada defence complaints

Category: Canada

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said U.S. President Donald Trump has not raised the Pentagon’s criticism of Canada’s defence spending in their conversations, as Ottawa and Washington dispute Canada’s military readiness and procurement plans [1][2]. In a fact sheet released Monday, the Pentagon said Canada lacked a credible, fully costed plan to meet NATO’s spending goal and was limiting its contribution to North American defence [5][2]. Carney said Canada is taking responsibility for its defence, citing its NATO deployment in Latvia and work with allies in the Arctic and Europe [3][2]. Stephen Fuhr, secretary of state for defence procurement, acknowledged readiness shortfalls but said purchases and recruitment were accelerating [2][1]. Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre said the U.S. defence secretary should not dictate Canadian policy, while arguing that government spending has not adequately reached military personnel [2]. NATO members are due to review their spending commitment in 2029 [5].

Primary Source: View original article

He has not raised issues such as has been reported recently

— Prime Minister Mark Carney (The Toronto Star)

Key Points

  • Budget targets: Canada’s latest budget projects combined defence and security spending of 4.1% of GDP in 2027-28, including 2.6% for core defence [5][9].
  • Cooperation limits: A Pentagon directive restricts some military engagement with Canadians at think-tank events and social functions; a senior U.S. official said it does not affect military operations [1][3].
  • Fighter review: Canada has committed to 16 F-35s, while the rest of its planned 88-aircraft purchase remains under review; the Pentagon says a mixed fleet could put additional pressure on personnel and NORAD [3][6].
  • Procurement choices: Fuhr cited U.S.-made P-8 surveillance aircraft and HIMARS launchers as equipment Canada continues to buy, alongside Ottawa’s effort to buy more at home and from other partners [2].

Perspectives

U.S. Department of Defense: A senior department official said the engagement restrictions aim to ensure a unified objective in dealings with Canada and argued that Ottawa needs a realistic approach focused on military efficiency [3].

Sources: The Globe and Mail

Stephen Fuhr, Secretary of State for Defence Procurement: Fuhr said U.S. officials may not have up-to-date information about the pace of Canadian changes and defended a strategy of diversifying procurement while continuing to buy American equipment [1][2].

Sources: CBC News

Stephen Saideman, Carleton University defence expert: Saideman said U.S. Defence Secretary Hegseth was trying to bully Canada into abandoning its plan to diversify defence spending and reduce reliance on U.S. suppliers [2].

  • May 2026: Pentagon suspended a bilateral defence forum; Canada selected Saab for early-warning aircraft [4][9]
  • 23 November 2026: Pentagon released a fact sheet criticizing Canada’s defence spending and readiness [2][5]
  • 25 November 2026: Carney said Trump had not raised the Pentagon’s concerns with him [1][2]

Industry Impact

  • Canadian defence procurement: Canada’s selection of Saab’s GlobalEye could support thousands of Canadian jobs. The aircraft is based on a Bombardier Global 6000/6500, and Saab proposed work in Canada on aircraft conversion, systems integration, engineering and maintenance [9].

Historical Background

Canada reached NATO’s previous defence spending target of 2% of GDP earlier in 2026, after years of falling short. NATO members agreed in 2025 to a 5% goal by 2035, split between core defence spending and wider security investments [5][4].
