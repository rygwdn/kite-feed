SIU researchers 3D-print cookies from plastic waste

Category: Science

Southern Illinois University Carbondale researchers developed µBites, a NASA-backed food technology that uses engineered yeasts to convert PET plastic and agricultural waste into edible ingredients for 3D-printed cookies, according to work presented at the American Chemical Society fall meeting [1][4][7][14]. The project began through NASA’s Deep Space Food Challenge, which sought low-waste ways to make food for long-duration space missions [1][7][8][12]. The team processes PET, corn stalks, leaves and other biomass into carbon-rich feedstock. Designed yeast strains then produce proteins, fats, acids, vitamins and flavor molecules before researchers mix the material into a dough, extrude it through a 3D food printer and bake it in a microwave [4][11][12][15]. Researchers say they have not eaten the cookies because they are awaiting institutional approval for human testing; lab analyses found no toxic chemicals, heavy metals or food pathogens [7][11][12][13].

We thought, why not look into food? Plastic is carbon and food is carbon.

— Dr Lahiru Jayakody (The Guardian)

Did you know: The cookies are shaped as the Greek letter µ, so the project name µBites is pronounced “microbites” [1][2].

Key Points

  • Conversion rate: Turkish outlets reported that the system can convert more than 50% of waste carbon into food material in one to two days [11][12].
  • Flavor chemistry: Researchers engineered one yeast strain to produce vanillin and another to turn PET-derived ethylene glycol into beta-carotene, which the body can convert into vitamin A [4][11][15].
  • Possible deployment: Jayakody told ScienceAlert the portable system could be used in submarines, disaster relief vehicles, deserts, polar regions and future lunar or Martian settings [1].

Technical Details

  • Oxidative hydrothermal dissolution: The process uses high-temperature, high-pressure water and oxygen to break down PET and biomass into smaller carbon fragments that engineered yeasts can metabolize [11][12][15].
  • PET carbon routing: PET-derived compounds provide carbon that yeast strains redirect into nutritional or sensory molecules, with different strains engineered for specific outputs [4][15].

Scientific Significance

  • Proof of concept: The work shows a route for turning PET and crop residues into food ingredients through microbial metabolism, rather than treating plastic as a direct edible material [4][14].
  • Engineering challenge: The team wants microbes to produce more of the cookie’s major ingredients, including starches, fibers and sweeteners, instead of adding them after fermentation [15].
