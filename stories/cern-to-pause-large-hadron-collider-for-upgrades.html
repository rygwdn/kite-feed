CERN to pause Large Hadron Collider for upgrades

Category: Science

CERN’s Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the 27 km underground particle accelerator near the Swiss-French border, is scheduled to shut down starting in June for a major upgrade program rather than a permanent closure [2]. The planned “high-luminosity LHC” work is expected to take about five years and aims to enable roughly ten times more particle collisions, increasing the amount of experimental data available to scientists [2][3]. Researchers say the current run is producing large amounts of data to analyze during the pause, keeping scientific progress moving even while the machine is offline [2].

Did you know: The proposed Future Circular Collider would require a 91 km tunnel, compared with the LHC’s 27 km ring [3].

Key Points

  • Hardware overhaul: Plans include upgrading superconducting magnets to increase beam luminosity, alongside detector upgrades to handle the higher collision rates [3].
  • Higgs focus: Scientists hope the improved machine will better probe how Higgs bosons behave, including whether and how they interact with each other [3].
  • Open mysteries: One motivation highlighted is that particle physics still cannot explain why elementary particles have the particular range of masses they do, even though they get mass through the Higgs field [3].
  • Next collider: CERN is also planning a potential successor, the Future Circular Collider, which would require a new 91 km tunnel and would target questions such as dark matter, dark energy, gravity’s relative weakness, and why matter dominates over antimatter [3].
  • Leadership note: The forthcoming shutdown and upgrade will be overseen by CERN’s new director general, Mark Thomson, who has emphasized that the current LHC run is performing strongly and generating plenty of data to study during the downtime [2][3].

Technical Details

  • Luminosity (colliders): Luminosity measures how often particles collide; the high-luminosity LHC aims to raise it so experiments can record far more collision events [2][3].
  • Superconducting magnets: The LHC uses superconducting magnets to steer and focus particle beams; the upgrade plan includes improving these magnets to increase beam performance [3].
  • High-luminosity LHC (HL-LHC): HL-LHC is the upgrade project intended to deliver roughly an order-of-magnitude increase in collision opportunities, enabling more measurements and searches [2].

Industry Impact

  • Detector technology: Detector upgrades planned for the high-luminosity era could push development of sensors and readout systems designed to cope with much higher collision rates [3].
  • Research workflows: A multi-year shutdown shifts effort toward data analysis and software work, as CERN leadership notes there will be substantial recorded data to study while the accelerator is offline [2].
  • Big-science planning: Discussion of a potential Future Circular Collider illustrates how major facilities rely on long-term international funding decisions, with uncertainty highlighted around the next machine’s financing [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: A higher-luminosity LHC should let physicists make more precise measurements and run a wider set of analyses, including deeper studies of Higgs-boson properties that could test the Standard Model in new ways [3].
  • What could change: The upgrade is framed as a way to potentially uncover “completely unexpected” results—by collecting much larger datasets, scientists improve their chances of spotting rare processes that current collision totals might miss [3][2].
  • Limitations and next steps: Even with improved performance, the articles note that some of the biggest open questions (dark matter, dark energy, matter–antimatter asymmetry) may require an even more powerful successor machine like the proposed 91 km Future Circular Collider, which still faces funding and feasibility hurdles [3].

Historical Background

The LHC was built to collide particles at extreme energies to study fundamental physics and conditions resembling the early universe [2]. In 2012, experiments at the LHC discovered the Higgs boson, a milestone that confirmed a key missing piece of the Standard Model [2][3]. The newly planned high-luminosity upgrade continues a pattern in big science: periodic long shutdowns to install more capable components and extend a facility’s scientific reach [common].
