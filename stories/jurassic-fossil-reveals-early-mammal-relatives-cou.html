Jurassic fossil reveals early mammal relatives could suckle milk

Category: Science

Researchers have described Megacauda sungei, a semiaquatic mammal relative from Jurassic China whose exceptionally preserved throat anatomy indicates that structures associated with suckling and swallowing had evolved at least 165 million years ago [2][3][6]. The fossil, found in Inner Mongolia, dates to roughly 168 million to 163 million years ago. At an estimated 1 to 2 kilograms, the animal was unusually large among Jurassic mammal relatives [3]. The throat bones and palate provide evidence that the capacity for milk feeding and coordinated swallowing evolved before modern marsupials and placental mammals diverged, researchers said [3][6]. The anatomy indicates that capability but does not directly establish how the animal fed its young [6]. The study was published Oct. 7 in Nature [3][6].

Key Points

  • Predator evidence: Robust canines and shearing cheek teeth point to a meat-based diet; vertebrate bones found near the fossil and in the animal’s stomach area suggest salamanders may have been among its prey [3][6].
  • Swimming anatomy: Using CT scans, researchers inferred that the animal could paddle with its forelimbs, propel itself with its broad tail and steer with its hind limbs [6].
  • New classification: The research places the animal in a newly named docodont family, Megacaudidae, adding a distinct body form and ecological niche to the known diversity of the group [6].

Perspectives

Zhe-Xi Luo, study co-author: Luo said the suckling-and-swallowing apparatus was an important innovation that helped early mammals diversify into different dietary niches [3].

Sources: Live Science

Tim Flannery, mammalogist: Flannery said aquatic lifestyles appear to have arisen repeatedly among early mammal-like animals [3].

Sources: Live Science

Historical Background

Docodonts were an extinct lineage of mammal relatives that lived from about 200 million to 120 million years ago [3]. Fossil finds from this group include species adapted to tree climbing and burrowing, as well as the semiaquatic Castorocauda lutrasimilis [3].
