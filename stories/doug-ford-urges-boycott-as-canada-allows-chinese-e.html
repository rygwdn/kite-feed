Doug Ford urges boycott as Canada allows Chinese EVs

Category: Canada

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government has struck a trade arrangement with China that would allow up to 49,000 Chinese-made electric vehicles to enter Canada at a reduced tariff rate, while China would lower its levies on Canadian canola and other products [1][4]. Ontario Premier Doug Ford says Canadians should boycott those vehicles and argues the deal risks harming Ontario’s auto sector and could raise security concerns, including potential issues for cross-border relations with President Trump’s United States [4][1][2]. Policy analysts and industry researchers say the move may help address near-term affordability and slowing EV adoption in Canada, but they warn it could create longer-run vulnerabilities if it isn’t paired with a broader industrial strategy [3][1].

  • US tariff backdrop: The Walrus reports the United States imposed a 25% levy on Canadian automobiles in 2025, tightening conditions for a sector that exports most vehicles south of the border [1].

Technical Details

  • Tariff-rate quota: The deal creates a capped quota of Chinese EV imports that face a reduced tariff rate, rather than applying the same tariff treatment to all imports [1][3].
  • EV availability standard: IRPP describes this as a regulation intended to require automakers to ensure a minimum supply of EVs in the Canadian market; it was paused amid shifting federal policy [3].
  • Cross-border auto exposure: Canada exports most vehicles to the U.S., making Canadian production sensitive to U.S. trade measures such as the 2025 U.S. levy on Canadian automobiles [1].

Industry Impact

  • Ontario manufacturing: Ontario political and industry voices warn the deal could add competitive stress to a province already concerned about auto-sector weakness and seeking stronger federal supports [4][2].
  • Consumer EV market: IRPP says the agreement is presented as a way to counter rising vehicle costs and a slowdown in EV uptake by expanding access to lower-cost models [3].
  • Agricultural exports: The arrangement includes China lowering levies on canola and other Canadian products, potentially improving market access for Canadian exporters [1].
  • Canada–U.S. trade ties: Ford argues the deal could complicate Canada’s relationship with the U.S., pointing to concerns about how President Trump’s administration views Chinese vehicles crossing the border [1].

Historical Background

Canada’s auto industry has long been deeply integrated with the United States market through cross-border supply chains [common]. The Walrus notes that in 2025 the U.S. imposed a 25% levy on Canadian automobiles, adding pressure on Canada’s export-oriented auto sector and shaping Ottawa’s search for new trade options [1]. IRPP adds that Canada’s EV momentum cooled in 2025 after consumer incentives were rolled back and an EV availability regulation was paused, which helps explain why affordability and availability have become central to the policy debate [3].
