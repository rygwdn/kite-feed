Scientists recover ancient human DNA from cave art

Category: Science

An international research team reported the first recovery of ancient human DNA directly from cave walls and rock art, using samples from sites in Spain and Portugal in a study published in Nature Communications [2][3][4][5]. Researchers examined rock-art panels in Iberian caves, including red marks and hand stencils in Maltravieso Cave [4][5]. The clearest sample came from a red ocher dot on a cave wall in Portugal that had been covered by calcite, a mineral crust that may have helped preserve genetic material [2][4]. The team said rock surfaces can retain traces left when people touched, painted, leaned on or otherwise came into contact with cave walls, making cave walls another source for archaeogenetics alongside bones, sediments and bone tools [2][4].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Some prehistoric rock art was made by blowing or spitting pigment, which gives researchers a plausible route for saliva-borne DNA to end up on cave walls [3].

Key Points

  • Study scope: The First Art project included researchers from Spain, Portugal, the United Kingdom, Germany and China, coordinated from Cáceres, Spain [4][5].
  • Research aim: The project grew from work at Maltravieso Cave and sought to date early artistic expressions in the Iberian Peninsula and analyze their chemical composition [4].
  • Potential attribution: Genevieve von Petzinger of the University of the Witwatersrand said the method could help identify individuals who made prehistoric artworks [3].
  • Sampling caution: Researchers used scalpels, drills and swabs under strict contamination controls and removed only tiny quantities of pigment or calcite [2].

Perspectives

Technical Details

  • Calcite sealing: At Escoural Cave, the sampled red ocher dot is a claviform, a type of abstract sign found in Paleolithic art, and the overlying calcite crust may have acted as a protective seal that helped preserve DNA on the wall surface [2].
  • DNA age and population: Researchers attributed the recovered DNA to Western Hunter-Gatherers, a population that lived in Europe between roughly 16,800 and 5,000 BCE, indicating the genetic traces are from the Upper Paleolithic or Mesolithic periods [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The study expands ancient-DNA research to decorated cave surfaces, which could connect genetic evidence with specific marked locations inside prehistoric sites [2][4].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The articles identify Escoural’s calcite-covered red dot as the strongest example but do not provide DNA coverage, contamination-rate figures or statistical confidence intervals for the genetic assignments [2].
  • Limitations and future work: Because sampling removes physical material from rock art, future work must balance genetic recovery against conservation limits and contamination risk [2].
