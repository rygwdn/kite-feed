November sky offers Mars-Jupiter pairing, Leonids and supermoon

Category: Science

Mars and Jupiter will appear about 1.2 degrees apart before dawn around Nov. 15-16, followed by the Leonid meteor shower’s expected peak around Nov. 17-18 [1][4][11]. The month’s full moon arrives Nov. 24 as a supermoon, while Uranus reaches opposition the following day and is visible throughout the night with binoculars or a telescope [4][8]. Early in the month, the waning moon passes near Mars, Jupiter and the Beehive Cluster; toward month’s end, it returns near the planets and Regulus [3][1]. Mercury also becomes easier to spot before sunrise around its greatest elongation on Nov. 20, though its low position near the horizon can make viewing difficult [5][1].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Taurid meteors: The Southern and Northern Taurids peak around Nov. 5 and 12, respectively; the showers produce only a few meteors an hour but are known for bright fireballs [5][14].
  • Leonid speed: Leonid meteors can travel about 71 kilometers per second, and Taipei’s observatory estimates a zenithal hourly rate of about 15 under ideal conditions [4][11].
  • Comet return: Comet 161P/Hartley–IRAS reaches perihelion on Nov. 27 at an estimated magnitude of 12, making it a telescope target; Popular Science recommends an aperture of at least 20 centimeters [2].
  • Voyager milestone: NASA’s Voyager 1 is expected to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth during November, meaning a radio signal would take 24 hours each way [1].

Timeline

  • 2 November 2026: Jupiter occultation visible in parts of Asia-Pacific; timing depends on location [3]
  • 9 November 2026: New moon darkens the sky for viewing faint stars and deep-sky objects [5][14]
  • 24 November 2026: The Moon occults several stars in the Pleiades across the western U.S. before dawn [8]

Q&A

Q: Will the Leonid peak be easy to see from a city?

A: Watch away from city lights, preferably after midnight, when the shower’s radiant in Leo is higher in the sky [4][14].

Q: Can Uranus be seen without optical aid at opposition?

A: At magnitude 5.6, Uranus may be visible to the unaided eye under very dark skies; look between Aldebaran and the Pleiades [8].

Action Items

  • Choose a dark, open viewing location for faint objects and an unobstructed eastern horizon for predawn planet groupings [4][14].
  • Avoid looking toward the Sun. Stop observing the morning planets before sunrise, especially when using binoculars or a telescope [2][14].
