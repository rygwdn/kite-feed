Lockin unveils infrared-powered smart lock and doorbell

Category: Technology

Lockin used CES 2026 to show new smart locks designed to reduce a classic smart-home hassle: power management, by keeping the lock charged via an optical infrared beam from an indoor transmitter plugged into a standard outlet [2][7][1]. The flagship V7 Max combines a mortise-style smart lock with dual cameras for video doorbell features and multiple biometric unlock options, including palm/vein and 3D face recognition [2][7][8]. Several outlets note the system is intended to be “eye-safe,” but it needs a clear line of sight between the transmitter and the lock to charge [2][7]. Lockin has not disclosed pricing, and Engadget reports units are expected to start shipping in July or August 2026 [2].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: KnowTechie notes the V7 Max’s industrial design is credited to Hartmut Esslinger, described as a former Apple design chief [8].

Key Points

  • Matter support: KnowTechie says the V7 Max supports Matter, aiming for smoother compatibility across Google Home, Apple Home, Amazon Alexa, and Samsung SmartThings setups [8].
  • Install reality: Because it’s a mortise lock rather than a simple deadbolt replacement, Engadget and PCWorld caution it will likely require professional installation for many doors [2][7].
  • Charging behavior: Engadget notes charging stops if something blocks the beam, while KnowTechie adds the lock can keep running for several hours on an internal battery reserve if the transmitter cuts out [2][8].
  • Award nod: PCWorld reports the AuraCharge power system earned a CES 2026 Innovation Awards “Best of Innovation” honor [7].
  • Renter angle: CNET highlights that battery-free operation could help renters or apartment dwellers who can’t run wiring to the door hardware [4].

Perspectives

Engadget: Frames the product as a high-end, do-many-things lock (cameras, speakers, biometrics, AI), while emphasizing practical constraints like line-of-sight charging and likely pro installation for a mortise lock.

Technical Details

  • Optical infrared charging: A dedicated indoor transmitter plugs into a standard outlet and sends an optical infrared beam to a receptor panel on the lock that converts light into usable power, with a reported range of about four meters (around 13 feet) [2][7].
  • Line-of-sight requirement: Both Engadget and PCWorld say the transmitter needs an unobstructed path to the lock; if something interrupts the beam, charging stops until the path clears [2][7].
  • Biometric modalities: The V7 Max supports multiple biometrics—finger-vein, palm-vein, and 3D facial recognition—alongside camera-based doorbell functions [7][8][2].

Industry Impact

  • Smart-lock design: PCWorld positions continuous optical power as an alternative to disposable batteries, rechargeable packs, and solar-topped designs, potentially shifting how future smart locks prioritize “always on” features like cameras and screens [7].
  • Install services: Because the V7 Max is mortise-style and likely needs professional installation, it could increase demand for locksmiths and installer partners compared with simpler retrofit deadbolt locks [2][7].
  • Platform ecosystems: With Matter support and reported compatibility with major smart-home ecosystems for video, Lockin is aligning with the industry trend toward cross-platform interoperability rather than single-vendor setups [8][2].

Scientific Significance

  • Energy transfer approach: These locks apply photonic energy transfer—using light to deliver power to a receiver that converts it to electricity—similar in principle to how small photovoltaic sensors convert light into electrical energy [2][7][common].
  • Safety engineering: Engadget and PCWorld describe the transmitter output as “eye-safe,” and Engadget adds that charging stops when something blocks the beam, reflecting a design that prioritizes limiting exposure while maintaining function [2][7].
  • Real-world constraints: The need for unobstructed line of sight illustrates a key systems tradeoff: wireless power can reduce user maintenance, but it adds environmental dependencies (placement, occlusion, geometry) that can affect performance in daily life [2][7][common].

Historical Background

Battery changes and recharging have been a long-running pain point for smart locks because motors, radios, and sensors draw more power than a traditional mechanical lock [common]. Some models have tried to reduce that burden using solar panels to top up internal batteries, a comparison PCWorld explicitly notes when describing prior approaches [7]. Lockin’s CES pitch builds on that history by shifting the “top up” mechanism indoors via a dedicated optical transmitter rather than relying on ambient sunlight [7][2].

Q&A

Q: Does the lock still work if your home internet goes down?

A: The articles emphasize local biometrics and on-device hardware (e.g., vein and face recognition plus touchscreens), but they do not specify offline behavior for app features or remote video access [2][7][8].

Q: What happens if you install it where sunlight or reflections are common?

A: PCWorld says AuraCharge delivers power regardless of lighting conditions, but the sources do not detail how reflective surfaces or bright sunlight might affect placement in specific homes [7].

Q: Will there be a lower-cost model without all the sensors?

A: Engadget reports a cheaper version will exist and will use the same wireless charging approach, though pricing and full feature differences were not disclosed [2].

Action Items

  • Check your door type before getting excited: Confirm whether your entry door uses a mortise lockset, since Engadget and PCWorld say the V7 Max is mortise-style and may need pro installation [2][7].
  • Plan transmitter placement: Measure whether you can place the AuraCharge transmitter within roughly four meters/13 feet with a clear line of sight to the lock, as described by Engadget and PCWorld [2][7].
