Euclid maps Milky Way core with 60 million stars

Category: Science

The European Space Agency on June 24, 2026, released a Euclid space telescope image of the Milky Way’s central bulge, describing it as the largest and most detailed high-resolution visible-light view yet of the galaxy’s crowded core [1][4][5][10]. Euclid, an ESA mission with NASA contributions, captured more than 60 million stars, along with nebulae and star clusters, in observations made over about 26 hours on March 23 and 24, 2025 [1][2][4][6][8]. Euclid normally studies distant galaxies to investigate dark matter, dark energy and cosmic expansion, but astronomers pointed it at the Milky Way’s bulge to prepare for NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, which NASA says will launch later this summer and repeatedly observe an overlapping region [1][2][5][6]. The Euclid image gives researchers a visible-light baseline for later microlensing work aimed at identifying planets around stars in the dense bulge [1][4][2].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Bulge age: The Milky Way bulge contains about 10 billion stars, mostly older, redder stars from roughly 10 billion to 11 billion years ago [6].

Perspectives

Research team: The image gives astronomers an early look at the same stars Roman will monitor, allowing them to check faint planet signals and estimate the masses of cold, distant worlds that are difficult to find otherwise.

Sources: ZME Science

Dr. Eamonn Kerins: Kerins said Euclid was not built for this exoplanet work but has proved to be a strong facility for it.

Sources: ZME Science

Technical Details

  • Mosaic construction: The image is a mosaic of nine “pointings” from Euclid’s visible-light camera, each covering more sky than the full moon, made over about 26 hours on March 23 and 24, 2025 [2][4].
  • Crowded-field resolution: The bulge is difficult to observe from the ground because Earth’s atmosphere and dense stellar crowding blur or blend stars, while Euclid can separate individual sources from space [2][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Wavelength complementarity: Euclid captured the bulge in visible light, while NASA’s Roman Space Telescope will observe the same region in infrared, giving astronomers a combined multi-wavelength view to study stars and planets [5].
  • Limits: The release describes a snapshot, not completed planet detections; the planet search depends on future alignments and repeated observations over the coming years [1][2].
