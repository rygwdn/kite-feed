Army says detonation of 68 barrels of lead azide in Iowa will proceed Wednesday

Category: Science

The U.S. Army plans to detonate 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at its ammunition plant near Middletown, Iowa, on Wednesday, Nov. 25, after a federal appeals court lifted a pause on the permit. Officials say the decades-old stockpile has become unstable and could explode even if left undisturbed as temperatures fall [16][20][14]. The three-judge panel found that environmental groups had not shown they were likely to succeed in their challenge and said the Environmental Protection Agency permit includes safeguards such as barriers, weather restrictions, advance notice and environmental monitoring [16][18]. President Trump had ordered the Army to wait for agreement from the court and Iowa’s governor-elect, Rob Sand. Sand said the administration should decide, and the Army later announced it would proceed [11][20][9]. The Army’s latest statement schedules the blast for Wednesday morning, citing forecast wind conditions. Farm Progress reported that plume maps are expected six hours before the detonation and hourly afterward [16][21].

Key Points

  • Alternative review: The Army's records included chemical treatment and thermal options; a contracting officer told MuniRem the cancellation was not based on deficiencies in its science [18][3].
  • Public notice: Mediapolis Mayor Charles Massner told WIRED that an Army email in October gave no date, quantity or public-protection instructions, and that the town next heard about the imminent blast through a later Army release [3].

Perspectives

Rob Sand, Iowa governor-elect: Sand said Iowans should have access to the same information he received in federal briefings [20][14].

Timeline

  • March 2026: Army reported degraded lead azide and awarded a chemical-treatment contract [14][3]
  • 13 November 2026: EPA emergency permit authorizing controlled detonation took effect [14]
  • 19 November 2026: Environmental groups sued and appeals court temporarily stayed the permit [14][10]
  • 23 November 2026: Appeals court lifted its stay; Army announced Wednesday operation plans [16][20]

Technical Details

  • Recrystallized material: Army filings say liquid loss left crystals around the inside rims and undersides of drum lids; freezing can increase their sensitivity, and friction may initiate an explosive chain reaction [18][13].
  • Handling risk: The Army says chemical treatment requires opening or manipulating deteriorated containers, exposing personnel to friction and shock hazards over a prolonged period [7][14].

Industry Impact

  • Livestock and farming: Farm guidance recommends sheltering cattle with ventilation fans off and cleaning dust from stored outdoor hay before feeding, reflecting concerns about possible deposition on feed and animals [21].
  • Regional environmental monitoring: Illinois plans to use state air monitors and assess updated Army maps, while Missouri is preparing added air, water, public-health and agriculture monitoring [15][18].

Historical Background

A 2014 Army article said concern about a roughly 40-year-old stockpile of deteriorating lead azide at the Iowa facility had helped spur research into safer explosives in 2004 [5].

Travel Advisory

  • Safety: Officials advise people in potentially affected areas to remain indoors with windows closed during the event; Illinois guidance calls for staying inside for 24 to 48 hours afterward [15][17].
  • Cleanup: Officials recommend washing outdoor surfaces with water after the blast to limit contact with deposited particles [20][18]. Follow state and local cleanup instructions, and avoid disturbing dust until authorities provide updated guidance.

Q&A

Q: Does the governor-elect have legal authority to approve the blast?

A: Rob Sand does not take office until January, and reporting says his approval is not legally required; President Trump nevertheless made the operation subject to Sand’s agreement in his public statement [16][11].

Action Items

  • Check the latest Army plume maps and state or county alerts to determine whether your location is in the projected exposure area [21][20].
