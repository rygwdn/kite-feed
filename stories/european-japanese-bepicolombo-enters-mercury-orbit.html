European-Japanese BepiColombo enters Mercury orbit ahead of two-probe science mission

Category: Science

BepiColombo, a joint mission of the European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, entered orbit around Mercury on Nov. 21, completing an eight-year journey and becoming the second mission to orbit the planet [4][2][13]. The two orbiters remain attached; they are scheduled to separate on Dec. 9–10 and take up different polar orbits, with science operations due to begin in April 2027 [1][2]. The mission will use the European Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, called Mio, to study Mercury from separate vantage points. Their instruments are designed to examine the planet’s surface and interior, its magnetic field and the thin gas envelope around it, including how the magnetic field interacts with the solar wind [2][4]. Controllers in Germany confirmed the spacecraft’s capture several hours after a series of braking thruster firings, with the spacecraft about 152 million kilometers away [4]. A propulsion-system problem delayed BepiColombo’s arrival from December 2025. Its nominal science mission is planned to last one Earth year, with an extension through 2029 [2].

What makes this mission really unique is that for the first time in space exploration, we are bringing two spacecraft to the vicinity of the planet, and we are going to put them in orbit around this mysterious body.

— Santa Martinez, European Space Agency mission manager for BepiColombo (Eos)

Key Points

  • Orbital paths: Mio is headed for a highly elliptical polar orbit, while the European orbiter will use a less elliptical polar orbit [1].
  • Long route: The spacecraft made one Earth, two Venus and six Mercury flybys to shed speed; the looping trajectory covered more than 10 billion kilometers [4][1].
  • Energy challenge: Reaching Mercury is harder than reaching Pluto because a spacecraft must shed enough momentum to match Mercury’s path around the Sun [2].
  • Mission cost: The joint mission is valued at about $2 billion, or 1.7 billion euros [4][2].

Perspectives

Daniel Schmid, research-group leader at the Austrian Academy of Sciences’ Space Research Institute, said measurements of the strength and direction of Mercury’s magnetic field could help explain why the planet has a global field [6].

Timeline

  • October 2018: BepiColombo launched from French Guiana on its Mercury-bound mission [4][13].
  • 2024: Thruster problems prompted a trajectory change and delayed Mercury arrival [2][13].
  • 3 September 2026: The transfer module separated from the still-connected science orbiters [1][7].
  • 21 November 2026: BepiColombo entered Mercury orbit after braking thruster firings [4][1].
  • 9 December 2026: The European and Japanese orbiters are scheduled to separate [2][1].
  • 10 March 2027: The European orbiter is scheduled to reach its nominal science orbit [1][12].
  • April 2027: The mission’s science operations are scheduled to begin [2][13].

Technical Details

  • Gravity and radio measurements: The European orbiter’s altimeter will map surface elevation, while its accelerometer, gravitometer and radio-science experiment will help map Mercury’s gravity and determine the size of its core [2].
  • Magnetic-field observations: Mio will map the magnetosphere, magnetic field and plasma environment. Researchers aim to investigate why Mercury’s field is offset from the planet’s center and how it varies [2].
  • Thermal management: The spacecraft use metallic-coated insulating films and multilayer insulation to manage temperature extremes; plans also call for one orbiter to spin 15 times per minute to reduce overheating [6][14].

Scientific Significance

  • Polar scans will search for water ice, while comparisons with MESSENGER observations may reveal changes on Mercury since that mission visited about a decade earlier [7].

Historical Background

NASA’s Mariner 10 flew past Mercury in the 1970s. NASA’s MESSENGER was the only spacecraft to orbit the planet before BepiColombo, from 2011 until it ran out of fuel and struck the planet in 2015 [4][2].
