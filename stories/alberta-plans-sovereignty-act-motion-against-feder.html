Alberta plans Sovereignty Act motion against federal hate speech law

Category: Canada

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith said Nov. 24 her government will introduce a motion under the province’s Sovereignty Act opposing the removal of a Criminal Code defence for good-faith opinions on religious subjects under the federal Combatting Hate Act. The federal law took effect July 18. Smith says the change could deter lawful religious and political speech; Ottawa says the Charter protects religious expression and the law targets intentional promotion of hatred [1][3][6]. The proposed motion would ask Alberta’s cabinet to consider further lawful steps, rather than directly cancel the federal law. Alberta Justice Minister Mickey Amery already has authority over whether many hate-speech charges proceed in the province. Constitutional law professor Gerard Kennedy said the motion could guide police and prosecutors but not bind them. The federal law also created offences targeting intimidation outside religious or cultural institutions and the public display of certain hate or terrorist symbols [1][2][4].

The federal government, yes, has the constitutional authority to define what areas they believe should be in the Criminal Code

— Mickey Amery, Alberta justice minister (The Globe and Mail)

Key Points

  • Prosecution authority: Alberta said it recorded 14 cases involving hate-crime offences in 2025-26; Amery had no comparable figures for the period since the federal act took effect [2].
  • Provincial guidance: Amery directed Alberta Crown prosecutors in September to give significant weight to freedom of expression and not charge people simply for controversial or unpopular political or religious beliefs [6][1].
  • Party politics: Alberta NDP Leader Naheed Nenshi called the move political theatre ahead of the United Conservative Party’s annual meeting in Calgary, while Smith denied it was aimed at the gathering [1][4].
  • Faith leaders: Edmonton Rabbi Ari Drelich said open disagreement should be protected, but speech that could lead to violence presents a serious problem [1].

Perspectives

Alberta Justice Minister Mickey Amery said the province is not planning a court challenge, describing the motion as Alberta “drawing a constitutional line” [3].

Timeline

  • 2022: Alberta enacted the Sovereignty within a United Canada Act [1][3]
  • 18 July 2026: Federal Combatting Hate Act took effect, removing the religious-opinion defence [6][3]
  • August 2026: Smith and Alberta’s public safety minister criticized an Edmonton police post encouraging people to report hate crimes [4]
  • 24 November 2026: Smith announced a motion challenging the federal law’s effects [1][4]

Historical Background

Smith has previously used the Sovereignty Act to push back against proposed federal environmental regulations and to direct provincial law-enforcement agencies not to enforce Ottawa’s gun buyback program. In a recent provincial referendum, nearly 56 per cent of Albertans rejected giving provincial law priority when it conflicts with federal law [3].

A: The Globe and Mail reported that the motion had not yet been tabled and did not specify a debate date [3].
