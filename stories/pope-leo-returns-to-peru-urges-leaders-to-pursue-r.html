Pope Leo returns to Peru, urges leaders to pursue reconciliation

Category: World

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Lima on Wednesday, Nov. 11, for a six-day visit to Peru, where he spent more than two decades as a missionary priest and bishop. The Vatican estimated that about 3 million people lined his motorcade route. At a welcoming event with President Keiko Fujimori, Leo called Peru his “homeland” and credited its people with forming him as a pastor [1][2][3]. In his first address to Peruvian authorities, Leo urged honesty, dialogue and fairness in addressing the country’s painful past; Fujimori described national reconciliation as an unfinished task [3][6]. Peru also faces rising violent crime and deep economic inequality: 25.7% of the population lived in monetary poverty in 2025 [1][14]. The visit is scheduled to include Chiclayo, where Leo served as bishop from 2015 to 2023, as well as Cusco and Pucallpa in the Amazon. His itinerary includes a stop at an elevation of about 3,400 meters in Cusco. Leo underwent surgery to remove a malignant lung tumor in mid-October and is due back at the Vatican on Nov. 17 [25][3][6].

Did you know: Inmates at Peru’s largest women’s prison stitched velvet dolls depicting Pope Leo to sell during the visit [6][21].

Key Points

  • Tourism boost: Peru expects about 850,000 domestic travelers and 50,000 foreign visitors during the visit; the government-backed “Paths of Pope Leo XIV” route attracted more than 1 million visitors and generated 450 million soles, or $125 million, in its first year [12].
  • Religious change: Catholics accounted for 67% of Peru’s population in 2026, down from 76% in Pew Research Center’s 2013-14 survey [13].
  • Crime figures: Police data cited by AFP show annual homicides rose from 1,000 in 2018 to 2,600 in 2025, while extortion complaints increased from 3,200 to 26,500 [34].
  • Amazon pressures: Illegal gold mining and coca cultivation affect Indigenous communities in the Amazon, where a Peruvian Catholic University professor said environmental damage, exclusion, violence and extreme poverty intersect [1][8].

Perspectives

Political science professor Alonso Cárdenas said he sees “no sign of a reaction, no strategy” from Fujimori’s government as homicide rates have nearly doubled in five years [3].

Atlantic Council analyst Martin Cassinelli said any papal reflection on Peru’s past could clash with the Fujimori government, given the president’s family legacy [21].

Industry Impact

  • Religious tourism: Clothing maker Victoria Quiñones expected a threefold increase in work during October and November as demand rose for pope-themed shirts and souvenirs [12].

Historical Background

The conflict between the state and the Shining Path insurgency lasted from about 1980 to 2000 [3][6]. Alberto Fujimori, Keiko Fujimori’s father, governed Peru from 1990 to 2000 and was convicted of human-rights abuses; he received a controversial pardon in 2017, which then-Bishop Robert Prevost criticized [6][21]. The conflict left about 69,000 people dead or disappeared, according to figures cited by RTÉ [22].
