Astronomers say ED138's dwarf-planet status awaits size measurements

Category: Science

Astronomers say 2026 ED138, an icy object beyond Pluto and a potential dwarf planet, cannot be classified until researchers measure its size more accurately [11][2]. Its estimated diameter ranges from 600 to 1,500 kilometers, and Matthew Holman, an astrophysicist at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, said the measurements may take months [11][1]. The uncertainty arises because astronomers infer size from reflected sunlight: a small, bright object can appear as luminous as a larger, darker one [2]. Infrared observations and longer-wavelength measurements with facilities such as Chile’s Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) could help narrow the size estimate; ALMA can detect light emitted by the object itself [2][7]. If the body is large enough for its gravity to make it nearly round, it could qualify as the sixth dwarf planet recognized by the International Astronomical Union; the current size estimate alone does not establish its shape or status [11][3]. Further observations will refine its size estimate, leaving astronomers and the Union to determine its classification [11][7].

Key Points

  • Search pipeline: Matthew Holman and his colleague Kevin Napier developed software that matches billions of detections to identify objects moving between images [3][8].
  • Long survey: The Rubin Observatory is designed to scan the southern sky repeatedly for 10 years. Researchers expect the survey to uncover thousands of previously unknown outer-solar-system objects [2].
  • Other candidates: Astronomers are also evaluating outer-solar-system dwarf-planet candidates including Gonggong, Quaoar, Sedna and Orcus [1].
  • Sky coverage: Holman said ED138’s position in the southern sky and its path across the Milky Way’s crowded star field put it beyond the reach of many earlier searches [11].

Perspectives

Megan Schwamb, an astrophysics professor at Queen’s University Belfast and a Rubin operations team member, said she was surprised Rubin found an object of this kind so early in its observations. She credited the observatory’s combination of speed, depth and sky coverage for its discovery potential [11].

Sources: CNN

Ralf Jaumann, a planetary researcher at the Free University of Berlin, called ED138 a promising candidate but said many others await consideration. He also said researchers have already determined a substantial part of its orbit [12].

Sources: Die Presse

Michele Bannister, a University of Canterbury associate professor who studies small solar system worlds, said a future spacecraft might find a nitrogen glacier on ED138, as on Pluto. She said that prospect would be exciting [11].

Sources: CNN

Timeline

  • 2016: Archived Dark Energy Camera images later showed ED138 in earlier observations [2][17]
  • March 2026: Rubin observations of ED138 began; the team later counted 30 detections [2][13]
  • 13 October 2026: Holman’s software flagged the moving object in Rubin data [2][1]
  • 27 October 2026: The Minor Planet Center announced ED138 as a candidate object [13][10]

Technical Details

  • Image-field scale: Rubin’s 3.2-gigapixel camera captures a patch of sky equivalent to about 40 full moons in one exposure, allowing repeated searches for moving points of light [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Orbital context: Researchers calculate that ED138 takes about 281 years to orbit the Sun, ranging from 36.86 astronomical units at its closest approach to 49.04 astronomical units at its farthest [2].

Historical Background

In 2006, the International Astronomical Union adopted a formal planet definition and reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet because it had not cleared other bodies from its orbit [11][1]. The Union recognizes five dwarf planets: Ceres, Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake [3].

Q&A

Q: What happens if astronomers confirm ED138 as a dwarf planet?

A: It would first receive a permanent number and later a formal name. The naming process could take time, and Holman said it is not yet clear who would choose the name [11].
