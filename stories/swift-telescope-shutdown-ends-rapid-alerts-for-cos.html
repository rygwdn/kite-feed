Swift telescope shutdown ends rapid alerts for cosmic explosions

Category: Science

NASA shut down its Swift Observatory in late October after it could no longer hold steady enough for observations, ending a 22-year mission to detect gamma-ray bursts, supernovas and other high-energy events [1][5][3]. Swift’s rapid observations helped direct other space telescopes toward eruptions for more detailed study, and astronomers are working out how to replace that alert function [10][9]. The observatory resumed observations after the rescue effort ended and captured additional gamma-ray bursts and other ultrahigh-energy events before NASA turned off its systems [1][7]. Over its mission, Swift recorded more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and 1,400 exploding stars [1][10].

Primary Source: View original article

Our fervent hope is that the lessons learned by the successes of Swift can be applied to new generations of space missions to accelerate our discoveries about the universe

— John Nousek, Pennsylvania State University astronomer who led Swift’s control team (John Nousek)

Key Points

  • Science tally: Swift recorded more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and 1,400 exploding stars, along with comets, asteroids and black holes tearing stars apart [1][5][3].
  • Rescue attempt: NASA hired Katalyst Space for a rescue effort, but its robot Link came no closer than 9 miles to Swift before returning to Earth in September [1].
  • Orbital decline: Intense solar activity in recent years dragged Swift down faster than usual [1].
