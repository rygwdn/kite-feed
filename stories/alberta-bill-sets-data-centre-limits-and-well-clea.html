Alberta bill sets data-centre limits and well cleanup funds

Category: Canada

Alberta introduced Bill 42 on Nov. 18, proposing new siting and water rules for large data centres and mandatory cleanup funding for every new oil and gas well [2][1]. Centres using at least 10 megawatts would generally have to be at least 600 metres from homes and avoid top-quality or irrigated farmland; they would also face a near-zero cooling-water standard. The Alberta Utilities Commission would regulate the larger facilities. The province says about 30% of current proposals could be affected, while projects already far along in approvals or construction would be exempt [2][3]. For new wells, companies would pay up to $50,000 initially and make equal annual payments for up to 10 years. The money would remain attached to the well if ownership changes. If a well becomes orphaned, its funding would go to the Orphan Well Association. The requirements are expected to cover wells drilled after April 1, 2027 [5][4]. The bill would also eliminate Alberta’s oil-sands emissions cap, which the province says is redundant with industrial carbon pricing [8].

We are making sure that when these wells are drilled, we have something set aside for them to be cleaned up so that the taxpayers are not on the hook.

— Brian Jean, Alberta energy and minerals minister (CBC News)

Key Points

  • Meta project: The Sturgeon County complex is a $13-billion, one-gigawatt development expected to come online in two to three years [2].
  • Cleanup backlog: The Orphan Well Association lists 7,328 wells awaiting decommissioning and 9,258 sites needing reclamation; it estimates cleanup costs for its inventory at $1.66 billion [5][4].
  • Emissions cap: Bill 42 would eliminate the oil-sands emissions cap, which set a 100-million-tonne limit and had never been enforced; emissions were about 84 million tonnes in 2023 [8].
  • Public response: A September CBC-commissioned poll found 31% of Albertans strongly opposed data-centre development and another 26% mostly opposed it, with electricity bills and environmental effects among the concerns [2].

Perspectives

Nathan Ip, Alberta NDP technology critic: Ip said the data-centre provisions do not set clear water-use standards and criticized the rules for exempting already approved projects; he called for a pause on projects while standards are developed [3][6].

Mark Daley, Western University chief AI officer: Daley said requiring operators to pay for their own power infrastructure is a substantial expense but can also improve electricity generation and transmission for the surrounding community [2].

Industry Impact

  • Electricity system: Data centres would have to recover waste heat for greenhouses, industry or homes; existing rules also require them to reduce power use before homes or essential services in emergencies [2].
  • Oil and gas producers: The new well-specific fund would supplement, not replace, the existing industry-funded Orphan Well Levy [5].

Business Angle

  • Siting exemptions: Designated industrial zones and brownfield sites could be exempt from minimum-distance rules, and municipalities could reduce the setback with agreement from affected landowners and the Alberta Utilities Commission [2].
  • Alberta’s goal is to attract $100 billion in data-centre investment by 2030 [7].
  • In 2025-26, average costs were $28,800 to decommission a well and $27,700 to reclaim a site. The proposed funding requirement includes an initial payment of up to $50,000 and up to 10 years of further payments [5][4].

Q&A

Q: How will the province determine the data-centre water standard?

A: The bill enables a province-wide standard aimed at near-zero cooling-water use, with detailed requirements to be set later through regulation [3][2].
