Carney government tables three justice bills to tighten crime rules

Category: Canada

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s Liberal government has made tougher crime policy a central focus, introducing three justice bills during Parliament’s fall sitting [2][3][4]. The package includes proposed Criminal Code changes that would make bail harder to obtain in some cases, add new offences, and expand the use of mandatory minimum and consecutive sentences [2][3]. The justice minister says the approach responds to Canadians’ stated worries about public safety and crime, framing it as government acting on what it has heard from the public [4][2].

  • Public messaging: The minister described the push as a direct response to public input, saying “people have been loud and clear” on crime concerns in government discussions about safety and justice priorities [4].
  • Bail direction: One stated aim of the legislative package is to “make bail tougher to obtain,” positioning pre-trial release rules as a key lever the government wants to adjust [2][3].
  • Sentencing approach: The bills propose adding or expanding mandatory minimum penalties and consecutive sentencing in some circumstances, signalling a shift toward more prescriptive sentencing tools [2][3].
  • New offences: The government’s justice agenda also includes creating new Criminal Code offences related to specific conduct (details not fully outlined in the article summaries) [2][3].

Technical Details

  • Bail (pre-trial release): Bail rules govern whether an accused person waits for trial in the community or in custody; the bills aim to make bail harder to obtain in some situations, which could change who is detained before trial [2][3].
  • Mandatory minimum sentence: A mandatory minimum requires judges to impose at least a set minimum penalty for certain offences; the proposed bills would add or expand these requirements in parts of the Criminal Code [2][3].
  • Consecutive sentencing: Consecutive sentences are served one after another rather than at the same time; the government proposes increasing their use for some offences, which can lengthen total time to be served [2][3].

Industry Impact

  • Courts and corrections: If tougher bail and expanded mandatory minimums increase the number of people held pre-trial or sentenced to custody, provincial jails and federal/provincial correctional systems could face added capacity and cost pressures over time [2][3].
  • Legal services: A broad slate of Criminal Code amendments (including new offences) typically means more litigation to interpret new provisions, affecting workloads for police, prosecutors, defence counsel, and courts [2][3].
