Carnegie Mellon study finds monkeys share geometric skills

Category: Science

A Carnegie Mellon University-led study published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences found that olive baboons and rhesus macaques could match two-dimensional geometric shapes in patterns resembling those of young children and adults [4][5][6]. The experiments involved eight adult monkeys, 55 U.S. preschoolers ages 3 to 6 and 77 Tsimane' adults with minimal formal education; researchers also reanalyzed data from an earlier study involving baboons, French preschoolers and first-graders, French adults and Himba adults [1][7]. At Seneca Park Zoo's Primate Portal, monkeys used a touchscreen task in which correct matches earned fruit or cereal rewards; children received stickers in comparable tasks [4][5][7]. Researchers designed the game so participants had to pair shapes by geometry rather than rely on obvious differences in color, size or form, and Jessica Cantlon said the results suggest some geometric intuitions may exist before formal schooling [4][1][5].

Primary Source: View original article

What surprised us wasn’t just that monkeys could do the task. It was that monkeys, children and adults seemed to think about geometric relationships in fundamentally the same way.

— Jessica Cantlon (Popular Science)

Did you know: ScienceAlert reported that one baboon named Kalamata liked the matching game so much that other monkeys argued when Kalamata monopolized it [1].

Key Points

  • Minimal instruction: Participants received limited guidance and had to infer the matching rule through trial and error, with correct answers followed by a positive sound cue [7].
  • Primate portal: The Seneca Park Zoo Primate Portal lets monkeys voluntarily interact with touchscreen cognitive tasks, and the project makes data, code and video publicly available [4].
  • Research context: Popular Science reported that only a handful of nonhuman species are known to understand geometry, which has made primate shape reasoning a disputed question in comparative cognition [4].

Perspectives

Der Standard: Der Standard said the findings could be relevant to children's education because the study suggests monkeys can abstract geometric forms in a way similar to humans.

Sources: Der Standard

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty and confidence: The new experimental sample included only eight monkeys — four rhesus macaques and four olive baboons — so the strongest evidence comes from comparing their patterns with larger human samples and reanalyzed prior datasets [1][7].
  • Limitations and future work: The touchscreen results show shape matching and abstraction, but Index.hr noted that the monkeys lacked symbolic understanding of geometric forms, leaving open how far the ability extends beyond the tested task [7].
