Astronomers model first green white dwarf from planetary debris

Category: Science

Astronomers led by Mark Hollands, an astrophysicist at the University of Warwick, have identified a white dwarf whose light would appear green to human eyes. The object, WD J1330+6435, has a temperature of about 4,000 degrees Celsius and has accumulated rocky material containing sodium, calcium and lithium. Sodium absorbs orange wavelengths, giving the star a greenish appearance in the researchers’ calculations [2][1][7]. The researchers did not observe the star’s color directly. They calculated how its recorded spectrum would appear using a model of human color vision. In a sample of 151 white dwarfs within about 65 light-years of Earth, five had unusual modeled colors. The researchers say forthcoming spectroscopic surveys should provide many more stars to examine, and Hollands plans to continue the analysis [2][11][6].

Primary Source: View original article

Imagine standing on a planet and looking up at a green star. It feels like something out of science fiction.

— Mark Hollands, astrophysicist at the University of Warwick (SciTechDaily)

Key Points

  • Pink counterpart: WD J1011+2845, about 48 light-years away, may be the reddest star recorded. Carbon brought to its surface gives it a deep salmon-pink color in the model [2][10].
  • Planetary evidence: Elements such as iron, magnesium and sodium in a white dwarf’s atmosphere can reveal traces of rocky debris from bodies that were torn apart, helping researchers infer the chemistry of former planets [1][11].
  • Survey bias: Brightness-selected surveys detect hot, blue white dwarfs at greater distances, making them disproportionately common in those samples compared with the representative nearby sample [2][10].
  • Color range: The study associates carbon-rich white dwarfs with pink or purple hues, stars enriched by rocky debris with yellow, and some hydrogen-helium atmospheres with cyan or pale blue [2][7].

Perspectives

Adam Burgasser, professor of astronomy and astrophysics at the University of California San Diego, likened the analysis of material accreted by white dwarfs to reconstructing a destroyed planet from the remaining evidence. He said the material’s elemental proportions can offer clues to how the planet formed [1].

Sources: The Nation

Agnès Kim, an astrophysicist and physics professor at Penn State Scranton, called the translation of starlight data into colors perceived by human vision an interesting link between physics and biology [11].

Sources: Dnews

Technical Details

  • Dense-atmosphere absorption: In the coolest white dwarfs, atmospheric density can reach about 1 gram per cubic centimeter—roughly the density of water and millions of times that at the Sun’s visible surface. Under these conditions, trace elements interact with dense helium plasma and absorb broader ranges of light [2][10].
