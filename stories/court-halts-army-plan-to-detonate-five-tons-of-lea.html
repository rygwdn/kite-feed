Court halts Army plan to detonate five tons of lead azide in Iowa

Category: Science

A federal court temporarily halted the U.S. Army’s plan to detonate 68 drums of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown after environmental groups challenged an emergency permit and raised concerns about lead dust spreading across Iowa and western Illinois [6][4][8]. The Army had rescheduled the blasts for Monday, Nov. 23, after objections from the public and elected officials; the court’s pause leaves it unclear whether they can proceed that day [5][6]. The Army says the material became more sensitive to shock and friction after liquid evaporated during prolonged storage, making transport hazardous and controlled detonation the safest immediate option. The plan calls for wetting the soil and using sand barriers to limit airborne particles, but the amount of lead likely to escape remains uncertain [2][9]. University of Iowa researchers and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency planned to sample air and surfaces. The EPA was expected to respond to the lawsuit by Nov. 22, while the judge set no date for further action [4][6].

Key Points

  • Alternative disposal: The Army hired Georgia-based MuniRem Environmental to chemically neutralize the explosives, then canceled the contract. The company’s chief executive said the Army offered no explanation beyond citing its safety assessment [3][5].
  • Farm concerns: Some dairy farmers worried that cattle could ingest lead that settled on pasture grass. Most crops had already been harvested, reducing the potential for lead to settle on them [4].
  • Exposure testing: University of Iowa researchers planned to collect airborne dust as the plume passed and use gauzelike pads to sample settled material, with laboratory results expected about a week after detonation [4].
  • State response: Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds activated the state Emergency Operations Center, while Cedar Rapids prepared to monitor drinking water drawn from the Mississippi River [5].

Perspectives

U.S. Army: Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said the Army moved the operation to Monday as the latest possible moment before a forecast temperature drop that could further destabilize the material [5].

Sources: CNN via KRDO

Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club’s Iowa chapter: The groups asked a federal appeals court to overturn the emergency permit, arguing the public lacked meaningful advance notice, safeguards were inadequate and safer disposal options had not been sufficiently considered [2].

Illinois Department of Public Health: Director Sameer Vohra advised potentially affected Illinois residents to remain indoors with windows closed for 24 to 48 hours and wash hands and outdoor surfaces to reduce exposure [9].

Technical Details

  • Stockpile composition: The 68 barrels held about 9,700 pounds of lead azide, containing roughly 6,900 pounds of lead by chemical mass [2].
  • Blast effects: The Army warned that noise and ground vibrations from the detonation could cause localized window damage [6].
  • Forecast conditions: For the rescheduled Nov. 23 date, the preliminary forecast called for slower winds toward the south-southeast, potentially affecting a smaller area but bringing higher lead concentrations to those downwind [4].

Scientific Significance

  • Lead persistence: Lead cannot be destroyed by heat; experts said the blast could convert or disperse it into fine particles and compounds that settle into soil and on outdoor surfaces [2].
  • Model estimates: The Army's models predicted elevated airborne lead levels, but below the threshold at which the EPA would require protective actions [2].
  • Health vulnerability: Lead exposure can harm the brain and nervous system, and experts highlighted particular risks for young children and developing fetuses [4][2].

Historical Background

The material had been in storage for more than 20 years before its deterioration was discovered [5][9]. The emergency permit authorized detonation through Feb. 11, 2027, and required the Army to track potential air and dust dispersion and notify the public five days in advance [9][5].

Q&A

Q: What would happen if the court allows the operation to proceed?

A: Fort Madison officials said they had been told the detonation could happen Monday or Tuesday. If it did not take place that week, another date would need to be arranged with additional planning [5].

Q: What cleanup steps are recommended after the plume passes?

A: The Army recommended hosing down windowsills, cars and other horizontal surfaces where dust could collect, and washing outdoor toys and play areas. Washing moves lead from surfaces into soil rather than eliminating it [4].
