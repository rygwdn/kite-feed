Wisconsin advances PFAS limits as studies reveal widespread water contamination

Category: Science

Wisconsin’s Natural Resources Board voted unanimously on Oct. 28 to set groundwater limits for six per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a class of persistent chemicals often called “forever chemicals.” The standards still require approval from the governor and Legislature [5]. Separately, a study of 1.86 million U.S. drinking-water measurements estimated average concentrations of six federally regulated PFAS at 5.2 nanograms per liter when researchers modeled values below laboratory reporting thresholds, compared with 2.6 when they treated those readings as zero [1]. Monitoring reports also show PFAS detections in waterways in the Chesapeake Bay region and along the Rhône River in Europe. In the Chesapeake region, testing found PFAS at every sampled location in Washington, D.C., and Delaware, while a Rhône survey detected the chemicals at 52 sites from the river’s source to its delta [3][4]. Wisconsin’s limits set different thresholds for six chemicals, but the rule remains subject to legislative and gubernatorial review. The state Department of Natural Resources put its highest estimate of two-year compliance costs at $9.9 million; wastewater utilities and industry groups dispute that estimate [5].

When the levels of these chemicals are below our reporting limit, it's often treated as zero when we calculate how widespread they are

Key Points

  • Specific limits: Wisconsin set limits of 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS, 10 parts per trillion for PFNA, PFHxS and GenX, and 2,000 parts per trillion for PFBS [5].
  • Testing gap: In the U.S. study, 98% of Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) drinking-water readings fell below the minimum reporting level; the authors caution that a below-threshold reading proves neither the absence of PFAS nor a health risk [1].
  • Waterway findings: The Rhône study detected 14 PFAS and two tire-derived contaminants; trifluoroacetic acid appeared in 96% of samples, reaching 3,252 nanograms per liter [4].
  • Site concerns: At the former RAF Upper Heyford base in England, investigators identified a firefighting training area as the most significant ongoing PFAS source. A separate report found that all groundwater and surface-water samples exceeded environmental standards [2].

Technical Details

  • Below-limit statistical modeling: Researchers used recorded measurements for 25 PFAS to model values below laboratory reporting thresholds. They also made the PFAS Censoring-Aware Screening Tool (PFAS-CAST) available to other teams and agencies [1].
  • Rhône sampling design: French kayaker Remi Camus collected samples during autumn low-water conditions along the river’s 830-kilometer course [4].

Industry Impact

  • Water utilities and wastewater plants: Wisconsin’s groundwater standards may require utilities to change biosolids disposal or treatment; the Madison Metropolitan Sewerage District projected about $10 million in two-year disposal costs, while a cited Minnesota estimate put treatment costs at $40 million to $110 million per facility [5].
  • Industrial facilities and landfills: The Wisconsin standards apply to these facilities as well as wastewater plants, extending potential compliance obligations beyond municipal utilities [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Benchmark exceedances: Including modeled below-threshold measurements pushed only a few dozen additional sampling locations above EPA benchmarks for PFOA and PFOS [1].
  • Coverage limits: The Chesapeake report drew on testing across 40 states but said its dataset did not include all PFAS testing sources. Many waterways had not been tested, so the detection rates do not describe every waterway [3].

Historical Background

Wisconsin approved PFAS standards for drinking and surface water in 2022 but did not approve groundwater limits at that time [5].

Q&A

Q: How widespread were PFAS detections in the Chesapeake region?

A: The report found PFAS in 95% of tested locations in Pennsylvania, 93% in New York, 71% in West Virginia, 70% in Virginia and 67% in Maryland [3].
