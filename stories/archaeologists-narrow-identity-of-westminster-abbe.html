Archaeologists narrow identity of Westminster Abbey skeleton to two medieval abbots

Category: Science

Archaeologists say a man buried in a Roman sarcophagus at Westminster Abbey was probably one of two medieval abbots, Ælfwig or his successor Wulfnoth. In a study published in the Journal of the British Archaeological Association, Westminster Abbey consultant archaeologist Warwick Rodwell identifies Ælfwig as the likelier candidate because he was already a monk at the abbey, but says the evidence cannot confirm either identity [1][4][8]. Radiocarbon testing dates the bones to 987–1052. An examination of the remains indicates the man was 35 to 49 years old and about 5 feet 9 inches tall [3][4]. A large cross carved into the lid shows the fourth-century Roman coffin was adapted for a later Christian burial. The sarcophagus is now on display in Westminster Abbey’s Queen’s Diamond Jubilee Galleries [1][8].

Primary Source: View original article

We do not know the date of birth of either. But either would fit equally well if we assume the Abbot was in his early twenties when the appointment was made and close to fifty when he died.

— Warwick Rodwell, consultant archaeologist at Westminster Abbey (Scimex)

Key Points

  • Burial evidence: A white lime outline inside the stone coffin indicates that the man was buried in a timber coffin, which later decayed [3][4].
  • Health clues: The bones show osteoarthritis, gout and two healed rib injuries [1][11].
  • Unusual reuse: Rodwell said reusing Roman sarcophagi for medieval burials is extremely unusual, and no comparable example is known [1].
  • Research history: The investigation drew on archaeological work around Henry III’s former sacristy, including a 2009 examination by Channel 4’s Time Team [3][4].

Timeline

  • 1820: Workmen accidentally exposed the sarcophagus and broke its lid [5][10]
  • 1869: The sarcophagus was formally excavated and its damaged lid repaired [4][1]
  • 2016: Conservation work revealed an almost complete skeleton inside [3][4]
  • 2025: Archaeologists completed sacristy excavations and analyzed the remains [4]
  • 21 October 2026: Rodwell’s study on the sarcophagus and burial was published [1]

Historical Background

The sarcophagus bears a Roman inscription to Valerius Amandinus, but archaeologist Warwick Rodwell said it was probably not originally buried at the abbey; later excavations in the area found no Roman artifacts [8]. Rodwell suggested it may once have stood in a mausoleum before being moved [8]. The abbey’s sacristy, built by Henry III, later stood above the sarcophagus site [5][8].

Q&A

Q: What work is planned at the site?

A: A new King Charles III Sacristy is being built at Westminster Abbey and is scheduled to open in 2027 [4].
