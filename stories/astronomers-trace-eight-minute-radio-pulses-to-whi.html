Astronomers trace eight-minute radio pulses to white dwarf and Sun-like star

Category: Science

Astronomers have traced repeating radio pulses from the system GPM J071507−114153 to a white dwarf orbiting a Sun-like star—the first known long-period radio transient with this type of stellar pairing [2][3][4]. The pulses recur every 7.9 minutes but appear only during a roughly six-hour window in each 32-hour orbit. The system lies more than 5,000 light-years away [2][3][4]. Researchers discovered the system using Australia’s Murchison Widefield Array, pinpointed it with South Africa’s MeerKAT telescope and used the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii to identify the companion as a G-type star [3][5]. Measurements of the orbit and the companion’s slight movement indicate that the pulsing object has about two-thirds the Sun’s mass, making it too light to be a neutron star [2]. The study was published in Nature Astronomy on Nov. 12, 2026 [4].

This is the first time the pulses are clear enough to see the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion, so we know that the pulsations are coming from a region close to the white dwarf, or possibly even from the white dwarf itself

— Dr. Sam McSweeney, lead author and commissioning scientist with the Square Kilometre Array Observatory (Dr. Sam McSweeney)

Key Points

  • Orbital evidence: As the pulsing object moved along its orbit, pulses arrived up to four seconds earlier or later than expected because the radio waves traveled different distances to Earth [2].
  • Stellar activity: The radio observations also revealed flares on the Sun-like star and a likely stellar prominence—a large loop of plasma with a strong magnetic field [3][4].
  • Formation hypothesis: Researchers think the star that became the white dwarf may have expanded into a giant and engulfed its companion. The companion then spiraled inward before the giant’s outer layers were ejected [2].

Timeline

  • 2021: The ICRAR research team discovered the first long-period radio transient [3].
  • Late 2024: Researchers spotted regular radio flashes in a Murchison Widefield Array survey [2].
  • 12 November 2026: The study appeared in Nature Astronomy [4].

Technical Details

  • Radio-data processing: The team processed more than 100 hours of radio data at the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre to confirm and measure the white dwarf’s motion [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Possible supernova precursors: The researchers say studying more white-dwarf binaries could clarify whether some are precursors to Type Ia supernovae, which astronomers use as standard candles to measure distances and the universe’s expansion [2].
  • Detection limits and future observations: Bright companions can obscure faint white dwarfs in optical observations, while radio pulses can reveal them. The authors expect the next generation of Square Kilometre Array telescopes to find more systems [2][3].
