Orionid meteor shower from Halley’s Comet to peak under bright Moon

Category: Science

The annual Orionid meteor shower, produced by dust left by Halley’s Comet, is expected to peak overnight on Oct. 21-22, with 10 to 20 meteors an hour under dark skies [2][1]. A bright waxing gibbous Moon will make fainter meteors harder to see, but viewing should improve after it sets. The shower is visible without a telescope in both hemispheres [5][6]. In the UK, the Moon is expected to set at about 3 a.m. BST on Thursday, while cloud and rain could disrupt views in parts of the country [1][2]. Australian astronomers recommend looking before dawn, after the Moon has set. The debris enters Earth’s atmosphere at about 66 kilometres per second [12][9].

“Neither of them on its own is the best shower of the year,” he said. “But one shower [Orionid] has fairly swift meteors … and the other [Taurid] very slow meteors … and so they add up and put on a better show.”

— Jonti Horner, University of Southern Queensland astronomer (ABC News)

Key Points

  • Australian viewing: In Australia, astronomers recommend looking north from about 4 a.m. local time on the east coast and 6 a.m. on the west coast, toward the area below Orion’s belt [12].
  • Weather outlook: The central and eastern United States are forecast to have clearer skies, while clouds could hinder viewing in the Four Corners region and parts of California, Oregon and Washington [4].
  • Next shower: University of Southern Queensland astronomer Jonti Horner described the Orionids as a warm-up for the Geminids, which peak in mid-December [12].

Timeline

  • 2 October 2026: Orionid activity began [9][7]
  • 21 October 2026: The peak viewing window begins overnight into Oct. 22 [2][1]
  • 7 November 2026: The shower’s active period is expected to end [1]

Historical Background

Halley’s Comet produces two annual meteor showers as Earth crosses its debris trail: the Orionids in October and the Eta Aquariids in April or May [5][12]. The comet itself is visible to the naked eye from Earth only every 74 to 79 years, with its next pass expected in 2061 [12].
