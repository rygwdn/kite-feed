EU proposes digital fairness law targeting dark patterns, subscriptions and games

Category: Technology

The European Commission proposed a Digital Fairness Act on Nov. 11 that would ban manipulative online sales designs, let users turn off features such as autoplay and endless scrolling, and make digital subscriptions easier to cancel [18][3][15]. The draft also calls for clearer prices for ticket queues and in-game currencies, disclosure of paid influencer posts, restrictions on personalized ads and prices targeting minors or exploiting sensitive data, and a right to reach a human customer-service representative. For minors, it would restrict game features including loot boxes and pay-to-win mechanics [2][11]. A companion revision to the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation would give the Commission direct authority to investigate large-scale, cross-border cases and impose fines of up to 6% of a company’s worldwide annual turnover. Companies based outside the EU would have to appoint a representative in the bloc [18][2]. The Commission estimates the package could benefit EU consumers by up to €13.7 billion annually [15][3]. The proposal is not law: the European Parliament and Council of the European Union must negotiate its terms, which may change [18][3]. The Commission has not said when the rules would take effect if adopted [1].

Key Points

  • Scale of concern: The Commission cited findings that 97% of popular platforms use dark patterns; a separate Commission figure put annual consumer losses from problematic digital practices at €10.3 billion [6][7].
  • Gaming safeguards: The proposal would require publishers using paid loot boxes to disclose possible item types and odds, show real-money values for virtual currency, and let users turn off spending features. The draft does not propose a blanket ban on adult microtransactions [25][12].
  • Enforcement: Euroconsumers said the rules will matter only if authorities enforce them effectively [7].

Perspectives

European Commission: Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen described the proposal as an effort to close gaps between existing digital laws rather than duplicate them [8].

Sources: Wired

Computer & Communications Industry Association Europe: Policy manager Albane Vannier argued that overlapping authorities applying similar rules under different EU laws could produce inconsistent enforcement and extra compliance costs [5].

Sources: Silicon Republic

Finance Watch: The NGO said the dark-pattern provisions do not go far enough to protect consumers in financial services and warned firms could still pay influencers to promote risky investments [7].

Sources: TechCentral

Timeline

  • 2020: Development of the Digital Fairness Act began in 2020 [25].
  • 2024: The Commission’s Digital Fairness Fitness Check informed the proposal [18].
  • September 2026: The Commission proposed a separate EU Kids Act on children’s online protections [5].
  • 11 November 2026: The Commission presented the Digital Fairness Act and enforcement-reform proposals [18].

Industry Impact

  • Subscription services: Digital merchants would face requirements for easier online cancellation, reminders before free trials end and explicit consent before a trial converts to paid service [13][11].

Business Angle

  • The Commission estimates annual costs to businesses at €678 million; it has not set company-by-company costs [15].
