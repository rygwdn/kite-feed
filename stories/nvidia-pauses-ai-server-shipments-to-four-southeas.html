Nvidia pauses AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian markets

Category: Technology

Nvidia has instructed manufacturing partners Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers containing its advanced artificial-intelligence chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, the Financial Times reported, citing three people familiar with the matter. The duration is unclear. The report described the hold as a compliance step while Nvidia and its partners work through new U.S. export controls [15][30][18]. The U.S. Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security issued a rule on Nov. 16 that took effect Nov. 19. It requires licenses for covered advanced-chip shipments to the four countries and Brazil. It also targets overseas data centers that provide substantial computing capacity to companies in China and other restricted jurisdictions. Existing providers serving those customers have 90 days to apply for licenses to continue those services [3][5][35]. The controls affect projects built around leasing Nvidia-powered computing to Chinese customers. Singapore cloud provider Aolani had planned to serve ByteDance; lenders have stopped extending about $500 million in credit for the project, and ByteDance may abandon the arrangement. Nvidia booked $104 billion through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, a figure that also includes some Taiwan and China revenue [15][42][13].

Key Points

  • Compute threshold: The rule requires a license when a data center supplies more than 12 million aggregate total processing performance units to covered customers; linked facilities count together [3][35][21].
  • U.S. carve-out: The destination-based licensing requirement does not apply to shipments for U.S.-headquartered companies or companies whose ultimate parent is U.S.-based, subject to reporting requirements [5][16][21].
  • Regional investment: Malaysia approved RM123.5 billion in data-center and cloud investments across 86 projects from January through September 2026, although the number relying on affected shipments is unknown [5].
  • Credit exposure: Lenders are also taking longer to review a $6.6 billion loan sought by Bain Capital’s Bridge Data Centres for Malaysian facilities serving ByteDance, according to CoinDesk [28].

Perspectives

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the Commerce Department’s rule is intended to block indirect access to restricted computing, including through smuggling routes and remote use of third-country data centers [38].

Malaysia’s trade ministry said Malaysia does not require a local permit to import AI chips and does not enforce other countries’ unilateral export controls, but expects local businesses to take those rules into account [16].

Technical Details

  • Remote-access threshold: The 12-million-unit trigger is roughly equivalent to the capacity of 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips [35].

Industry Impact

  • Data-center construction: Compliance checks could delay projects in Johor, Jakarta, Batam, Bangkok and Singapore; some regional AI clouds are also looking to AMD and newer suppliers as alternatives [10].

Historical Background

An October 2023 update targeted China-specific Nvidia chips, including the A800 and H800, as Washington sought to limit circumvention through third countries [35]. In May 2025, the Trump administration rescinded the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule before it took effect [3][6].

Q&A

Q: Does the reported shipment pause include Brazil?

A: Brazil is covered by the new licensing rule, but it was not named among the four countries in the reported shipment pause [5][16].
