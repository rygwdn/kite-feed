Gravitational-wave data point to black holes born without supernovas

Category: Science

A team led by California Institute of Technology astrophysicist Katerina Chatziioannou found that black holes about 10 times the Sun’s mass dominate a sample of 257 black-hole mergers, while objects in the 12-to-16-solar-mass range are unusually scarce. The pattern in gravitational-wave observations suggests some massive stars may collapse into black holes without exploding as supernovas [1][2][3]. The researchers also found a narrower range of spins among the roughly 10-solar-mass black holes, supporting the possibility that they share a formation process. Failed supernovas have not been definitively observed, and the team says further analyses and observations are needed to test whether they explain the pattern [1][2][3].

Primary Source: View original article

These results represent a connection between the gravitational-wave data and underlying astrophysics we are trying to extract.

— Katerina Chatziioannou, California Institute of Technology astrophysicist (Space.com)

Key Points

  • Hard to observe: A star that collapses without a bright explosion may simply disappear, making failed supernovas difficult to detect directly [1][3].
  • Standard pathway: In the usual account of massive-star death, a supernova ejects the star’s outer material, while its collapsed core can become a neutron star or black hole [2][3].
