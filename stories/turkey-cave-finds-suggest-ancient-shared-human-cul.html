Turkey cave finds suggest ancient shared human culture

Category: Science

Archaeologists reported evidence from Üçağızlı II Cave on Turkey's Mediterranean coast that Neanderthals and later Homo sapiens may have used similar cultural practices around 59,000 years ago, including hunting strategies, stone tools and shell collection [4][2][5][9]. The findings were published Monday, July 6, in the journal PNAS by a Kyoto University-led international research group [4][9][8]. Neanderthals inhabited the cave first, followed by Homo sapiens. Researchers said continuity in tools and personal objects suggests possible cultural transmission, although the two groups did not overlap at the site [5][4]. Phys.org described the evidence as a possible shared culture spanning more than 20,000 years, while Discover Magazine focused on a shell-collecting tradition that may have lasted about 20,000 years [6][3]. The finds include fossils, stone tools and seashells from a limestone cave on a coastal route north of Syria, in a region described as a prehistoric corridor between the eastern Mediterranean and Anatolia [4]. Live Science reported that related cave finds in Turkey and Israel suggest different human populations exchanged ideas while remaining biologically distinct [4].

Primary Source: View original article

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty: The evidence points to possible cultural exchange or continuity, but the reports frame the conclusion as suggestive rather than definitive because similar artifacts can also result from shared environments or convergent choices [1][4][5].
  • Limitations and future work: The case relies on material traces from one cave sequence and comparisons with other regional sites. Broader confirmation would require additional dated layers and comparable artifact patterns from nearby corridors between the Levant and Anatolia [4][6].

Historical Background

Neanderthals and Homo sapiens coexisted for tens of thousands of years in parts of Eurasia before Neanderthals disappeared roughly 40,000 years ago [common][6]. Archaeological discoveries in recent decades have challenged older assumptions that Neanderthals lacked complex behavior comparable to early modern humans [4].
