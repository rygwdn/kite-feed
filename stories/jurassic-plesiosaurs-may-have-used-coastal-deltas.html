Jurassic plesiosaurs may have used coastal deltas to regurgitate shells

Category: Science

Fossilized regurgitated material beneath the North Sea suggests Jurassic plesiosaurs entered river deltas to expel shells and other hard, indigestible remains from marine meals. Researchers identified more than 20 accumulations in sediments off Norway, including mollusk shells and plant material, and interpret them as plesiosaur vomit rather than feces [5][3]. The study was published in Geological Magazine [3]. The locations suggest the reptiles moved from feeding grounds to shallow coastal environments before regurgitating the material. Paleontologist Dirk Knaust said the study is the first reported identification of plesiosaur vomit containing mollusks. He compared the behavior with pellet regurgitation by owls and with fur seals and marine crocodiles moving onto land or shore [5][1][3].

Key Points

  • Body plan: Plesiosaurs had long necks and sleek, flippered bodies [3].
  • Animal size: Plesiosaur species ranged from about 2 to 13 meters in length. The group persisted for more than 140 million years [5][3].

Perspectives

Dirk Knaust: Earlier fossilized-vomit research mostly examined vertebrates that ate other vertebrates, providing a contrast with the mollusk-related evidence in this study [5].

Technical Details

  • Regurgitalites and coprolites: The study distinguishes fossilized vomit, or regurgitalites, from coprolites, or fossilized feces. Researchers classify the material based on concentrated undigested food remains [3].
  • Delta drill core: The evidence includes a clump preserved in delta sand dated to about 160 million years ago and recovered through deep-sea drilling [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Behavioral evidence: The deposits provide direct evidence of feeding and digestive behavior in a long-extinct marine reptile [5][3].
  • Post-meal resting: The fossilized vomit may help researchers identify where plesiosaurs rested after feeding [2].
  • Geographic context: Plesiosaur fossils have been found on practically every continent [5].
