Chile's Rubin Observatory finds possible sixth dwarf planet beyond Pluto

Category: Science

Astronomers using Chile’s Vera C. Rubin Observatory identified 2026 ED138, an icy object beyond Neptune. It could become the solar system’s sixth recognized dwarf planet if further observations show it is large enough for its gravity to make it round [1][5][4]. Current estimates put its diameter at 600 to 1,500 kilometers and its distance from the Sun at about 46 times Earth’s, farther out than Pluto’s average orbit [5][9]. Astrophysicist Matthew Holman identified the object on Oct. 13 while searching Rubin observations for moving objects. Its apparent brightness suggests a large body, but the size estimate depends on how much sunlight its surface reflects. Astronomers need infrared observations to measure its physical size more precisely [2][4][3]. Rubin recorded it repeatedly before the discovery, and researchers later found it in older telescope archives. The observatory’s decade-long survey will continue scanning the southern sky while astronomers pursue further measurements of ED138 [5][3].

Key Points

  • Older sightings: A separate search found ED138 on photographic plates from 1954, and another archive reaches back to 1937 [3][6].
  • Repeat detections: Rubin captured the object 30 times on 20 nights between March 4 and July 11, 2026, allowing researchers to refine its orbit [5][3].
  • Hard to spot: ED138 moves slowly, takes about 280 years to orbit the Sun and currently crosses a crowded Milky Way star field, factors that helped it evade identification [3][2].
  • Survey capacity: Rubin’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time uses a 3,200-megapixel camera to image the southern sky every few nights over 10 years [5].

Perspectives

University of Regina astronomer Sam Lawler warned that increasingly numerous and brighter satellites crossing Rubin’s field of view could make future discoveries harder [3].

Timeline

  • 2016: Archived Dark Energy Camera images recorded ED138 [2][5]
  • 4 March 2026: Rubin's recorded observations of ED138 began [5][3]
  • 13 October 2026: Holman identified the object while searching Rubin data [1][5]
  • 27 October 2026: Researchers presented ED138 findings at a planetary-science meeting [5][3]

Scientific Significance

  • Orbital history: ED138's orbit is tilted nearly 25 degrees from the plane of the solar system, making it a hot-classical trans-Neptunian object; the tilt may reflect gravitational interactions with Neptune [1].
  • Surface-color clue: Rubin's six-filter images show ED138 is red, unlike neutral-colored members of the Haumea family, making it unlikely to be part of that family [6].

Historical Background

The International Astronomical Union reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet in 2006 after astronomers formalized a definition requiring a planet to clear its orbital neighborhood. The discovery of Eris in 2005 helped prompt that debate [5][9]. Five dwarf planets are currently recognized: Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake [2][4].
