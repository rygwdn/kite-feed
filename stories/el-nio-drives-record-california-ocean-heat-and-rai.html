El Niño drives record California ocean heat and raises global weather risks

Category: Science

Record ocean heat linked to a strengthening El Niño extends hundreds of meters below the surface off California, where researchers measured 77°F water at a depth of 200 feet near San Diego—21.3°F above normal—and record warmth in Monterey Bay [4]. The climate pattern, a natural warming of the tropical Pacific that shifts weather worldwide, is also helping fuel heavy storms and unusually high seas along California’s coast [4][6]. The World Meteorological Organization forecasts that the event will peak in December and gives it a near-100% chance of lasting through February 2027. Sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific could reach 3.7°C above normal, exceeding the previous record anomaly of 2.6°C reported for 2015-16 [13][14]. Forecasters expect warmer conditions across much of the world and shifts in rainfall, with drought and flood risks varying by region [13][3]. Recent storms have flooded parts of California. Scientists say warm coastal water can supply storms with more moisture and energy but does not guarantee a wetter winter [19][6]. El Niño’s effects are expected to continue into 2027: drought threatens crops in parts of Central America and Southeast Asia, while East Africa faces heightened flood risk into early 2027 [3].

Key Points

  • Food price risk: Generali Asset Management economists estimate a strong El Niño could raise world food prices by as much as 20% by the first quarter of 2028, adding up to 0.4 percentage point to inflation in the United States and eurozone [23].
  • Water readiness: Santa Clara Valley Water is staging flood-fighting supplies and securing specialized services to protect infrastructure ahead of severe weather [21].
  • Plains outlook: The Central Plains entered winter with snow-water equivalent at about half of normal and low reservoirs; climatologists warned that a warmer, drier winter could compound water stress [2].
  • Snow contrast: Colorado State University snow hydrologist Steven R. Fassnacht said Chile’s unusually deep 2026 snow season may offer clues for the Sierra Nevada and southern Rockies, where El Niño often brings above-average snowfall [17].
  • Trade exposure: Drought has reduced traffic through the Panama Canal, which faced water shortages during the 2023-24 El Niño, while economists warn that agricultural and shipping disruptions can compound food-market pressures [23].

Perspectives

Bridgette Mason, Wisconsin assistant climatologist, warned that low snow cover leaves plants and underground infrastructure exposed to deeper frost during a sharp cold spell, even in a generally warm winter [2].

Paolo Zanghieri, an economist at Generali Asset Management, described El Niño as an exceptionally severe negative supply shock that compounds other disruptions, including conflict and climate change [23].

Catherine Gamper, an adaptation policy specialist at the OECD, said national adaptation plans have not translated into concrete requirements for economic actors to invest in resilience [23].

Technical Details

  • Coastal marine heat wave: California’s coastal waters have been in a major heat wave since January 2025, with temperatures in some places reaching 8°F above average in August [4].
  • Kelvin waves and sea level: Eastward-moving Kelvin waves push down the ocean’s thermocline and help keep water from moving offshore; warm water also expands, contributing to higher coastal sea levels [4].

Scientific Significance

  • Compound warming: El Niño is adding heat to California’s coastal waters, which researchers say have already warmed substantially since the 1970s [4].
  • Hurricane patterns: El Niño has fueled an exceptionally active Pacific hurricane season while contributing to an unusually quiet Atlantic season [3].
  • Projected health burden: Science News reports that the global temperature increase could contribute to 451,000 additional heat-related deaths by February; this is a projection, not a reported death toll [3].

Historical Background

The 1997-98 El Niño brought intense rainfall to California; February 1998 saw as much as 30 centimeters of rain in parts of the state, and flooding destroyed about 1,700 homes in Palo Alto and Menlo Park [1]. That event also brought drought to Southeast Asia and deadly summer floods to central Europe and Peru; El Niño-related weather extremes were linked to more than 22,000 deaths worldwide [1].

Humanitarian Impact

Recent storms flooded streets and prompted a helicopter rescue of a woman from Los Angeles’ Sepulveda Basin; residents near the Pacific Palisades burn scar faced mudslide warnings [19]. Heavy rain and rapidly melting snow in the Four Corners region also damaged hundreds of homes [17].

Travel Advisory

  • Safety: Snow levels over Washington’s Cascade passes were forecast to rise from about 2,500 feet early in the week to 4,500 feet midweek and 6,000 feet by Friday [5]. Carry snow chains when driving over mountain passes and be prepared to use them if conditions require [5].

Q&A

Q: How is El Niño affecting hurricane activity?

A: The pattern has fueled an exceptionally active Pacific hurricane season and an unusually quiet Atlantic season [3].

Q: When could El Niño give way to La Niña?

A: A Central Plains outlook says the event could shift toward neutral conditions in late spring or early summer 2027, with La Niña possible later in 2027; the timing remains uncertain [2].
