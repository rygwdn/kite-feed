Octopus study maps two tactics for squeezing through tight openings

Category: Science

Researchers have documented how common octopuses change their movements to pass through small openings, adding a step when a gap is narrower than the distance between their eyes. For the study, published in the Journal of Experimental Biology, researchers filmed octopuses probing openings with an arm, passing other arms through, then using them to pull the rest of the body through. In tighter gaps, the animals passed their remaining arms through before squeezing the mantle and head through separately [1][2]. The experiments included three subadult common octopuses. A report on the study also described tests involving four Mayan octopuses, which did not attempt to pass through the narrower openings [5]. The researchers say the movement patterns could inform the design of soft robots for search and rescue, where current slender designs leave limited room for equipment. An octopus-like robot would need flexible arms that can both squeeze through gaps and pull the rest of the machine forward [3][4].

Primary Source: View original article

So we are thinking about whether we can bring this capability to the robots so that they can go through those kind of spaces that are really hard to reach.

— Wenci Xin, roboticist (NPR)

Key Points

  • Study limits: The researchers cautioned that results from just three common octopuses cannot be generalized. Some escape attempts also failed, suggesting the animals may not precisely judge whether a gap is passable [5].
  • Possible constraint: Although the beak has often been treated as the rigid body part that limits passage, roboticist Wenci Xin said the experiments suggest the arms near the body may instead set that limit [2][6].
  • Robot challenge: Conventional actuators used for complex arm movements add rigid parts that can stiffen a robot, making it difficult to combine softness with the strength and dexterity needed to pull its body through [4][2].
  • Tactile probing: Study co-author Michael Kuba said octopuses investigate openings with their touch-sensitive arms, while species in different habitats or with different body proportions may use other strategies [2][4].

Perspectives

Mishu Duduta, University of Connecticut engineer: Duduta, who was not involved in the research, said the octopuses’ consistent reach-and-pull sequence has implications for robot design and strengthens the case for pulling rather than pushing through obstacles [3].

Sources: NPR

Scientific Significance

  • The researchers describe the movement sequences as combinations of motor primitives: basic movement building blocks that can be recombined into different actions [1].
