MIT course and essays probe AI rationality limits

Category: AI

A set of new pieces in the AI section takes a step back from product launches to ask a more foundational question: what does it mean for an artificial system to be “rational,” and how should humans think about AI decision-making as the technology becomes more capable [5][1]. MIT’s news coverage highlights a new interdisciplinary course that uses AI research as a lens for classic philosophical problems about beliefs, learning, goals, and agency, aiming to give students sharper critical-thinking tools for the field [5]. Other commentary-style articles widen the angle to how today’s systems appear to “intuit” rather than reason the way people expect, and to debates about who benefits when AI becomes embedded in everyday thinking and work [2][3][4]. Together, the sources frame an optimistic throughline—more people are learning to question AI claims carefully—while also underscoring the need to keep humans meaningfully in the loop when the stakes are high [5][4].

Primary Source: View original article

You’d imagine computer science and philosophy are pretty far apart, but they’ve always intersected.

— Leslie Kaelbling (MIT News)

Did you know: MIT notes that Leslie Kaelbling holds an undergraduate degree in philosophy from Stanford, reflecting the cross-training that often underpins AI’s philosophical roots [5].

Key Points

  • New MIT class: MIT says 6.S044/24.S00 (AI and Rationality) was offered for the first time in fall 2025 as part of its Common Ground for Computing Education initiative [5].
  • Interdisciplinary teaching: The course is co-taught by MIT professor Leslie Kaelbling and philosopher Brian Hedden, and it explicitly draws on the long-standing overlap between computer science and philosophy, including early AI roots associated with Alan Turing [5].
  • Rationality focus: MIT frames the class around probing rational beliefs, learning from experience, and decision-making toward goals, while also emphasizing humans’ cognitive limits and subjective notions of “what is or isn’t rational” [5].
  • Intuition framing: An EL PAÍS English piece argues that a “big surprise” about AI is that it can appear to ‘intuit,’ challenging everyday expectations about how machine “thinking” works [2].
  • Cautionary view: A separate essay warns about the risks of AI “thinking for us,” emphasizing potential downsides when people over-delegate judgment to automated systems [4].

Perspectives

MIT course instructors: The course is designed to have students explore philosophical problems through the lens of AI research rather than claim to settle what “rational AI” ultimately is [5].

Technical Details

  • Rationality and agency: MIT frames “rationality” and “agency” as concepts that could matter for AI decision-making, especially because human judgments about rationality are constrained and subjective [5].
  • Rational beliefs: The MIT course description highlights formal questions about what it means to form rational beliefs, learn from experience, and make rational decisions toward goals—topics that sit at the boundary of philosophy and AI research [5].
  • AI ‘intuition’: EL PAÍS English describes a “big surprise” that AI can appear to ‘intuit,’ which can affect how users interpret outputs and explanations [2].

Industry Impact

  • AI education: MIT positions the course as part of a broader Common Ground for Computing Education effort, signaling growing institutional investment in training technologists to reason about AI’s conceptual foundations—not just build systems [5].
  • Workplace adoption: Commentary warning about AI “thinking for us” highlights a stakeholder impact for employers and workers: tool adoption choices can shift where human judgment sits in workflows [4].
  • Incentive design: A capitalist framing of AI points to how funding models and profit motives can steer product priorities, affecting what kinds of AI get built and deployed [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: By centering rational belief formation and decision-making as research questions, the MIT course treats AI not only as engineering but also as a target for formal theories about reasoning and learning [5].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Across the sources, several pieces emphasize that apparent competence can mask limits—whether through humans’ constrained views of rationality or over-delegation to AI—suggesting readers should calibrate trust carefully even when systems seem convincing [5][4].
  • Limitations and future work: MIT’s framing explicitly avoids claiming a final answer to how rational AI can be, instead positioning the topic as an ongoing set of problems for students and researchers to explore [5].

Historical Background

Computer science and philosophy have intersected since early work on computing and reasoning; MIT’s coverage points to Alan Turing as a figure associated with both computer science and philosophy in the origins of AI thinking [5].

Q&A

Q: Why do some articles describe AI as “intuiting” rather than reasoning?

A: Because at least one source frames modern AI behavior as producing answers that look like intuition, challenging common expectations of step-by-step humanlike reasoning [2].

Q: Who is teaching MIT’s AI and Rationality course, and what are their disciplines?

A: MIT says Leslie Kaelbling (computer science and engineering) and Brian Hedden (linguistics and philosophy) co-teach the class, reflecting an intentional CS–philosophy pairing [5].

Q: What kinds of non-technical concerns do these pieces raise about AI adoption?

A: They highlight social and institutional issues such as over-reliance on automated judgment and the influence of economic incentives on how AI is developed and used [4][3].

Action Items

  • Practice a ‘rationality check’ on AI outputs: When an AI gives an answer, write down its goal, assumptions, and what evidence would change your mind—mirroring the course’s focus on beliefs, learning, and goal-directed decisions [5].
  • Use AI as a second opinion, not an autopilot: For high-stakes decisions, keep a human decision step and ask the tool to list uncertainties and alternatives, aligning with warnings about AI replacing human thinking [4].
