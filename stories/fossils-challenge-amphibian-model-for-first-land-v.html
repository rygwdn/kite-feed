Fossils challenge amphibian model for first land vertebrates

Category: Science

A study published Thursday, June 19, in Science reports that rare fossils from the Mazon Creek fossil beds in northern Illinois challenge the long-held idea that some of the earliest land-dwelling vertebrates developed like modern amphibians [3][4][5]. Researchers from the Field Museum studied baby embolomeres, crocodile-like predators that lived in rivers, lakes and swamps between about 350 million and 280 million years ago, and found evidence that the juveniles lacked expected tadpole-like features such as external gills [2][4][5]. The fossils instead point to direct development: the animals appear to have grown from small hatchlings into larger versions of the same body plan, rather than undergoing a major metamorphosis like frogs [2][3][4]. The Mazon Creek site preserves iron carbonate concretions formed about 309 million years ago and is known for exceptionally preserved fossils, including soft tissue, from ancient swamps, shallow seas and river deltas southwest of Chicago [4][5]. One key specimen came from the collection of Richard Rock, a Vietnam War veteran and fossil collector whose specimen had been mislabeled “baby lamprey” until Andrew Young noticed it in 2023 [6]. Field Museum paleontologists Arjan Mann and Jason Pardo described that specimen alongside another Field Museum fossil and dozens of related specimens. Pardo said the study shows the premise that the first four-legged vertebrates grew up like amphibians “is wrong” [2][6].

Key Points

  • Specimen provenance: The pivotal baby embolomere fossil came from the collection of Richard Rock, a Vietnam War veteran and master gardener who spent decades collecting fossils; he had labeled the specimen as a baby lamprey before amateur fossil enthusiast Andrew Young spotted it in 2023 and referred it to the Field Museum [6].
  • Juvenile anatomy: Researchers said a key baby embolomere specimen was about the size of a short, narrow macaroni noodle and did not show the external-gill anatomy expected in a tadpole-like juvenile [4][5].
  • Evolutionary scope: Live Science reported that early tetrapods eventually gave rise to humans, while UNIAN noted that the lineage led to modern amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals [3][6].

Perspectives

Jason Pardo, Field Museum paleontologist: Pardo described the fossil as a tiny but anatomically complete embolomere lacking any larval features, resembling a scaled-down adult [2].

Technical Details

  • The hatchling embolomere specimen has a fully ossified skull and a long snout typical of the group, with no trace of gill arches or other larval specializations, supporting direct development [2][4].
  • The exceptional soft-tissue preservation at Mazon Creek is crucial because larval features such as external gills usually decay before fossilization; their absence in these specimens is not merely a preservation artifact [4][5].

Scientific Significance

  • The findings imply that the lineage leading to reptiles, birds and mammals, known as amniotes, may not have passed through a tadpole-like larval stage in its evolutionary history [3][2].
  • The researchers compared bone development and body proportions between the hatchling and adult embolomere fossils, finding that the tiny specimen is morphologically consistent with a miniature adult rather than a larva [2][5].
  • Limits of inference: The sources identify embolomeres as early land-dwelling vertebrates but also describe them as mostly aquatic animals with small legs, so the finding revises developmental assumptions rather than proving a single life cycle for every early tetrapod lineage [2][4][5].

Historical Background

Early tetrapods evolved from lobe-finned fish during the Devonian and were among the first vertebrates with limbs. For decades, many paleontologists assumed these animals had an amphibian-like life cycle, beginning as aquatic, tadpole-like juveniles with external gills that later metamorphosed into land-capable adults [4][5].
