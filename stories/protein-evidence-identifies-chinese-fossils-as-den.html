Protein evidence identifies Chinese fossils as Denisovans, revealing likely facial traits

Category: Science

Researchers have identified human fossils from Xujiayao in northern China as Denisovans using ancient proteins, then compared their bones with other remains to infer the group’s likely appearance. The fossils suggest Denisovans had low, backward-sloping foreheads, heavy brow ridges and broad, relatively flat faces, with features distinct from those of Neanderthals [2][3][4]. The team recovered a Denisovan-linked collagen variant from two fossils with no surviving DNA. The Xujiayao collection includes 20 specimens excavated decades ago. The researchers argue that some Chinese fossils previously assigned to separate human groups may instead represent variation within the Denisovan lineage. That classification remains uncertain for specimens identified only by resemblance, and researchers say additional samples would help test it [2][3].

Key Points

  • The team also compared Xujiayao anatomy with roughly 200 remains from other human groups to help distinguish Denisovan traits [3].
  • Taxonomic debate: The Xujiayao fossils had been proposed as Homo juluensis, while the Harbin skull was classified as Homo longi; the new study argues that differences such as skull size may reflect variation within Denisovans rather than separate branches [2].
  • Geographic reach: Evidence places Denisovans from Siberia and the Tibetan Plateau to northeastern China and the Taiwan Strait, suggesting they occupied a wide range of Asian environments [2].
  • Reconstruction limits: Bones and molecular evidence cannot establish details such as skin tone or hairstyle, so lifelike portraits necessarily include artistic interpretation [2].

Perspectives

Qiaomei Fu, a geneticist at the Chinese Academy of Sciences and a corresponding author of the study, said comparing fossil anatomy could help researchers classify other remains that may be Denisovan but have not yet been recognized as such [3].
