Curtin researchers find molecular evidence of blood in 115-million-year-old dinosaur fossil

Category: Science

Researchers at Curtin University in Western Australia report finding mineralized structures resembling red blood cells and heme, a component of hemoglobin, in limb bones of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a theropod dinosaur from Brazil that lived about 115 million years ago. The study appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences [4]. The team says chemical analyses detected heme in the fossil structures, something earlier studies of cell-like shapes in ancient fossils had not established [4][1]. Paleontologist Mary Schweitzer questioned the interpretation, noting that the structures are concave, unlike the expected shape of nucleated dinosaur blood cells, and could be something else [1][2]. If the structures are blood cells, researchers could use such molecular traces to investigate extinct animals’ physiology. The finding does not demonstrate that dinosaur DNA survived [4][3].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Fossil anatomy: Researchers found mineralized structures resembling red blood cells in small channels that once housed blood vessels [4].
  • Other fossils: The researchers said molecular traces may be present in other fossils but go unnoticed with conventional examination methods [4].
  • Beyond dinosaurs: The researchers said the preservation findings could inform searches for molecular traces of ancient life in astrobiology, although the article reports no extraterrestrial evidence [4].

Perspectives

Kliti Grice, a Curtin University professor who led the study, said the findings challenge assumptions about how long biomolecules such as hemoglobin can persist [4].

Sources: Gazete Oksijen

Technical Details

  • Hausmannite preservation: The researchers propose that the mineral hausmannite stabilized the mineralized cells and their molecular components over geological time [4].
  • Burial environment: The team suggests that rapid burial in sediment after death, low oxygen levels and high microbial activity may have helped preserve biological material that would otherwise degrade [4].

Scientific Significance

  • The analysis also identified amino acids associated with collagen, a structural protein found in bone and other connective tissues [4].
