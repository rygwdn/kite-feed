Australian study finds bark microbes cut warming gases

Category: Science

Researchers studying eight common Australian tree species report that microbes living in bark can strongly influence exchanges of climate-active gases between forests and the atmosphere, beyond the well-known role of leaves in taking up carbon dioxide [6][7]. Using gene-focused and genome-resolved metagenomics alongside gas-flux measurements, the work links bark-dwelling communities to consumption of methane, hydrogen and carbon monoxide, with some microbes also capable of producing warming gases depending on conditions [6][4]. The finding suggests forests may provide additional “hidden” atmospheric cleaning services—and that better accounting of bark microbiomes could refine climate and air-quality estimates [1][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: The same tree can host distinct microbial communities in different tissues; in this case, bark supports its own microbiome that can influence atmospheric gases [6].

Key Points

  • Big surface area: Tree bark collectively adds up to a surface area comparable to Earth’s total land area, making it a potentially important but previously undercounted habitat for atmospheric chemistry [4].
  • Many unknown species: Reports highlight that bark hosts a diverse microbial community, including species not yet described by science, underscoring how much biology remains to be cataloged even on familiar trees [4].
  • More than methane: The bark microbiome can process not only the greenhouse gas methane but also hydrogen and carbon monoxide, gases that shape atmospheric chemistry and air quality [1][7].
  • Toxic gas removal: The researchers and coverage note that bark microbes can help remove “greenhouse and toxic gases,” pointing to potential co-benefits for air quality alongside climate impacts [2][5].
  • Forest-scale rethink: Australian science communicators describe the study as revising how scientists think about trees and their microbial partners in shaping the atmosphere, by adding bark processes to the forest gas-budget story [3].

Technical Details

  • Genome-resolved metagenomics: A method that reconstructs microbial genomes directly from environmental DNA, which the study used to identify bark microbes and infer metabolic capabilities related to climate-active gases [6].
  • Gene-centric metagenomics: An approach that focuses on specific genes across a community to estimate what functions are present; the researchers used it to connect bark microbial genes with processing of methane, hydrogen and carbon monoxide [6].
  • Gas fluxes: Measurements of gases moving between bark and the atmosphere; reporting notes bark microbes can drive uptake or emission of warming gases, so flux direction matters for climate accounting [4].

Industry Impact

  • Carbon accounting: If bark gas exchanges prove significant across ecosystems, climate models and greenhouse-gas inventories may need to incorporate bark microbiome processes alongside leaves, soils and deadwood to better estimate methane, hydrogen and carbon monoxide cycling [6][4].
  • Urban forestry: Because bark microbes can remove greenhouse and toxic gases, city tree-planting programs may gain an additional air-quality angle—though outcomes will depend on which tree species and microbial communities dominate [2][5][4].
  • Biotech research: The identification of bark microbes with genes tied to consuming methane, hydrogen and carbon monoxide may steer future discovery of enzymes or pathways useful for environmental applications, as discussed in the context of “climate management” [7][6].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The work broadens the forest greenhouse-gas narrative from “trees take up CO2” to “tree-associated microbes also regulate other climate-active gases,” potentially improving how scientists represent forests in atmospheric budgets [1][6][7].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Coverage emphasizes that bark microbes can either consume or emit warming gases depending on conditions, which signals that net climate impacts will vary by species, location and environment and need direct measurement rather than assumption [4].
  • Limitations and future work: Because the primary study focuses on eight common Australian tree species, researchers will likely need broader sampling across forest types and climates to learn how generalizable bark-gas effects are globally [6][3].

Historical Background

Scientists have long recognized trees as a carbon sink because photosynthesis draws down atmospheric CO2 [1][3]. Over the last decade, researchers have increasingly mapped plant-associated microbiomes (on leaves, roots and soils) to understand how microbes alter nutrient cycles and greenhouse-gas fluxes [common]. This new work extends that lens to bark—an abundant but historically overlooked habitat—by pairing genomic tools with measurements of climate-relevant gases [6][4].

Q&A

Q: Do different tree species appear to host bark microbes with different gas-processing abilities?

A: Yes; the study examined bark from eight common Australian tree species and used metagenomics to compare microbial identities and functions, indicating species-level differences are part of the research focus [6].

Q: Why do hydrogen and carbon monoxide matter in climate science if they are not as famous as CO2?

A: The articles note that bark microbes metabolize hydrogen and carbon monoxide, gases that influence atmospheric chemistry and indirectly affect climate processes, making them relevant to comprehensive air–climate accounting [1][7].

Q: What determines whether bark becomes a net sink or source of warming gases?

A: Coverage reports bark microbes can either take up or emit climate-warming gases depending on conditions, implying that local environment and microbial community makeup affect the direction of gas flux [4].
