Science outlets map 2026’s biggest skywatching moments

Category: Science

Three science outlets are looking ahead to 2026 as a particularly busy year for space science and stargazing, spanning crewed exploration plans, new instruments to study the universe, and headline-worthy sky shows [1][2][3]. The New York Times highlights NASA’s Artemis II as a key milestone on the path back to the Moon, alongside other major space and astronomy moments expected during the year [1]. Scientific American frames 2026 as a “banner year,” pointing to flight tests of fully reusable rockets and launches of new orbital telescopes designed to probe the cosmos in new ways [2]. Smithsonian spotlights a public-friendly calendar of celestial events—from planet lineups to meteor showers—culminating in a total solar eclipse that offers a rare chance to see the sun’s corona [3].

Q&A

Q: What makes a “blood-red moon,” and which lunar event causes it?

A: A “blood-red moon” is commonly associated with a total lunar eclipse, when Earth’s atmosphere bends and filters sunlight onto the Moon, giving it a reddish color [common]; Smithsonian highlights a blood-red moon among 2026’s viewing targets [3].

Q: Why do space-based telescopes matter if we already have giant ground observatories?

A: Observing from orbit avoids atmospheric turbulence and absorption that can blur or block portions of incoming light, enabling clearer measurements across certain wavelengths [common]; Scientific American notes new orbital telescopes planned for 2026 to study the cosmos [2].

Q: What’s the practical difference between a rocket being reusable and being “fully reusable”?

A: “Fully reusable” generally means both the booster and the upper-stage elements are intended to be recovered and flown again, not just one component [common]; Scientific American specifically highlights 2026 flight tests of fully reusable rockets [2].
