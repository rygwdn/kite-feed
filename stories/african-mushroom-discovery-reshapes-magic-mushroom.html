African mushroom discovery reshapes magic mushroom evolutionary history

Category: Science

Researchers have described a wild African mushroom species, Psilocybe ochraceocentrata, found in grasslands of South Africa and Zimbabwe, and say it is the closest known wild relative of the widely cultivated psychedelic mushroom Psilocybe cubensis [2][1]. The study, published in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, used genetic analysis to estimate the two species last shared a common ancestor about 1.5 million years ago—meaning the P. cubensis lineage is far older, and potentially more widely dispersed, than a popular narrative that links it to colonial-era cattle movements [1][2]. For decades, many ecologists argued that P. cubensis reached the Americas in the 1500s with cattle introduced by European colonists, since the mushroom often grows on cow dung and cattle were absent from the Americas before colonization [1][2]. The newly described African species looks much like P. cubensis and has been cultivated under names such as “Natal super strength (NSS)” and “Transkei.” But the genetic work shows it is distinct, pushing mycologists to rethink when—and how—these psychedelic fungi spread across continents [1]. The find does more than settle a biogeography debate. Researchers say a close wild relative could also serve as useful comparison material for ongoing work on psilocybin-producing mushrooms and their potential mental health applications [2][1].

Key Points

  • Why it’s the iconic “magic mushroom”: Popular Science says that when people talk about “magic mushrooms,” they usually mean P. cubensis; at moderate and high doses it can trigger sensory hallucinations and altered perceptions of time, and evidence suggests microdoses may have therapeutic potential [1].
  • Where the new relative was found: Science Media Exchange reports that Psilocybe ochraceocentrata turned up in grasslands in South Africa and Zimbabwe [2].
  • Ecology that fueled the mystery: Popular Science notes that P. cubensis thrives in tropical climates and is strongly associated with cow dung—an ecological combination that shaped (and complicated) earlier ideas about how it spread across the Americas [1].

Technical Details

  • Phylogenetic divergence time: The study estimates that P. cubensis and P. ochraceocentrata shared a common ancestor roughly 1.5 million years ago. Researchers use that split to argue their lineages separated long before human-led cattle transport in the 1500s [1][2].
  • DNA-based species delimitation: Genetic analysis of the African specimens showed they were not P. cubensis despite their close visual similarity, supporting the formal description of a distinct species, Psilocybe ochraceocentrata [1][2].
  • Biogeography vs. niche: The reporting highlights a familiar tension in fungal biogeography: a dung-adapted niche can steer assumptions about dispersal routes, but genetics-based evolutionary relationships can point to older, non-colonial pathways of spread [1][2].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: By identifying the closest known wild relative of P. cubensis in Africa, the work adds a new reference point for reconstructing the evolutionary history and biogeography of psilocybin-producing Psilocybe mushrooms. It also challenges a widely repeated dispersal story centered on post-1500 cattle introductions [1][2].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The articles cite a divergence-time estimate (about 1.5 million years) based on genetic analysis. But the news coverage does not include the statistical uncertainty (such as confidence intervals) or methodological detail needed to judge how precise that estimate is [1][2].
  • Limitations and future work: The reports point to follow-up work that re-examines dispersal scenarios and uses the newly described species as a comparative reference for psilocybin-related research, but they do not lay out specific next-step experiments or sampling plans [1][2].

Historical Background

European colonists introduced cattle to the Americas in the 16th century [1][2]. Because Psilocybe cubensis often grows on cow dung in tropical environments, that link made colonial-era cattle movements an intuitive, widely repeated explanation for its broad presence in the Americas [1][2].
