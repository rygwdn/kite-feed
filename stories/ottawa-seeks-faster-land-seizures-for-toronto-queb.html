Ottawa seeks faster land seizures for Toronto-Quebec City rail line

Category: Canada

Canada’s federal government has proposed giving Alto, the Crown corporation developing a high-speed rail line between Toronto and Quebec City, expanded powers to acquire land through expropriation without public hearings and transfer property directly to a private consortium [2][1]. Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon said the measures would prevent land acquisition disputes from delaying the project for years and argued they do not remove due process [1][3]. The proposals, included in a Budget 2026 motion published Nov. 20, would exempt Alto from the federal Expropriation Act and parts of several rail and environmental laws [2][6]. The project is expected to cost $60 billion to $90 billion; construction of its first phase, between Montreal and Ottawa, is scheduled to begin in 2029 or 2030 [1][3]. Farmers, opposition politicians and municipal representatives have raised concerns about property rights, farmland and consultation, while Alto says it will pay fair compensation and limit the impacts [2][6].

Key Points

  • Property restrictions: The proposed rules could let the transport minister prohibit work on affected land for four years and preserve a government right of first refusal for as long as eight years [2].
  • Crossing disputes: Where the rail corridor splits farms or roads, the proposal would send disputes to private arbitration, with each party potentially paying up to $25,000 plus its own legal costs [2][1].
  • Farm impacts: Alto’s Montreal-Ottawa segment would cross about 1,700 properties, including roughly 500 agricultural properties, according to Alto’s chief executive [1].
  • Projected benefits: A study commissioned by Alto estimates the operating line could generate about $24.5 billion in annual economic benefits [2].

Perspectives

Avi Lewis, NDP leader: Lewis criticized the project’s public-private ownership model, saying smaller communities feel excluded and insufficiently consulted, and argued major projects need a transparent, democratic process [1].

Sources: The Globe and Mail

Martin Caron, president of the Union des producteurs agricoles: Caron argued that bypassing ordinary expropriation rules could restrict challenges, and said the farmers’ organization is campaigning against the rail project [4].

Sources: Le Devoir

Yves-François Blanchet, Bloc Québécois leader: Blanchet warned that the powers could extend to provincial government land and properties associated with Crown-owned utility Hydro-Québec, and called the proposal excessive [4].

Technical Details

  • Grade-separated crossings: With trains travelling at speeds of up to 300 km/h, crossings must use overpasses or underpasses rather than meet the tracks at road level, and the corridor would need to be fenced [2].
  • Work prohibitions: A proposed notice could prohibit significant construction or improvements on a targeted property for up to four years, and the legislation would exclude value added by such work from expropriation compensation [2].

Historical Background

Parliament’s High-Speed Rail Network Act, passed in March 2026, already allowed Alto to bypass upfront purchase negotiations and replace public hearings with written objections while retaining the federal Expropriation Act [2]. The latest draft would remove that remaining statutory framework and expand the project-specific exceptions [2][6].

Q&A

Q: What route options remain under consideration in eastern Ontario?

A: Alto is considering a direct Ottawa-Peterborough corridor and a more southerly route that could include Kingston as a stop [1].
