Pope Leo urges justice on Equatorial Guinea visit

Category: World

Pope Leo XIV used the final stop of his four-country Africa tour to press for freedom, justice, and a narrower gap between rich and poor during events in Equatorial Guinea on April 22 [1][2][3][4][5][6]. In Malabo, he said the country should serve the common good rather than private interests. He also criticized the "colonisation" of Africa’s minerals and what he called a "lust for power," delivering that message in a nation led by President Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo since 1979 [1][3][4][5]. The visit drew large, enthusiastic crowds and marked the first papal trip to Equatorial Guinea since John Paul II visited in 1982, underscoring the stop’s symbolic weight for local Catholics [1][6]. Leo’s remarks put inequality and governance at the center of his closing message in Africa, while also offering a hopeful call for believers to help bridge long-standing social divides [2][3][5].

Did you know: Equatorial Guinea had not hosted a papal visit since 1982, so many people welcoming Leo in 2026 were seeing an event like this in their country for the first time in 44 years [1][6].

Key Points

  • Mass audience: Leo celebrated Mass with members of the presidential family present, giving his appeal for justice and fairness added political visibility in the country [3].
  • Catholic majority: Euronews said roughly three-fourths of Equatorial Guinea’s population is Catholic, helping explain the scale of the public welcome for the pope [4].
  • Tour finale: The stop in Equatorial Guinea concluded a four-country African trip, making the speech his last major message of the tour [4][2].
  • Public reception: Crowds lined the road from the airport into Malabo, cheering and waving flags as Leo arrived in the administrative capital [1].

Equatorial Guinea has been ruled by President Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo since 1979, a fact that shaped the context for Pope Leo’s criticism of power and inequality [1]. The visit was the first by a pope since John Paul II traveled there in 1982, making Leo’s stop a rare moment of direct Vatican engagement with the country [1][6].
