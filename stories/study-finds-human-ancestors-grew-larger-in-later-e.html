Study finds human ancestors grew larger in later evolutionary leap

Category: Science

A new study published this week in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences challenges the idea that human ancestors steadily became larger over millions of years. It found that the largest increase in body size came around 2 million to 2.5 million years ago, with the appearance of Homo rudolfensis or Homo erectus/ergaster [1][2][4][6]. The analysis covered 386 fossil specimens across about 2.5 million years and concluded that early hominins such as Australopithecus averaged roughly 40 kg, while Homo erectus/ergaster were the first hominins to average around 60 kg or more, near many modern adult weights [1][2][6]. The findings complicate the familiar straight-line image of human evolution because not every branch followed the same trajectory: Homo floresiensis and Homo naledi remained small even as other Homo lineages grew larger [2][6]. Reports on the study linked the timing of the size increase to behavioral and ecological changes, including more efficient bipedal walking, increased meat consumption and wider geographic ranges, while the authors used statistical models designed to account for gaps in the fossil record and relationships among species [2][5][6].

Study authors: The researchers described the findings as a dual-mechanism model, with gradual size change among early hominins and a later non-linear shift within Homo.

Scientific Significance

  • Methodological scope: The analysis used 386 fossil specimens representing 21 hominin species and accounted for missing fossil data and family-tree relationships, letting researchers test both gradual trends and lineage-specific jumps in one model [1][6].
  • Limits of interpretation: The sources report correlations between larger bodies, bipedal efficiency, meat consumption and range expansion, but they do not establish a single causal driver for the size shift [2][6][5].

Historical Background

The “march of progress” image of human evolution became widely recognized in the 20th century, but modern paleoanthropology treats human evolution as a branching family tree rather than a straight ladder [common]. Australopithecus species such as the famous Lucy specimen predate Homo and are part of the small-bodied baseline in the new analysis [5][2].
