Researchers link personality traits to 1,260 genetic variants

Category: Science

An international team published a study in Nature that identified 1,260 genetic variants associated with the Big Five personality traits: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism [7][9][10]. The analysis combined DNA and personality-questionnaire data from 1.14 million people across 46 research cohorts in 13 countries, making it the largest genetic investigation of personality, the researchers said [9][8]. The researchers said 824 of the variants had not previously been linked to personality, and the findings do not point to a single “personality gene”; instead, each trait reflects many variants with very small individual effects [7][12]. The study also found genetic overlap between personality and outcomes including mental and physical health, education, career trajectories, longevity and social relationships [8][13][12]. The findings build on twin-study evidence that personality is partly heritable, while researchers stressed that DNA cannot usefully predict an individual person’s character from genetic data alone [2][3][13]. Family comparisons in the study suggested shared traits among relatives were more strongly tied to genetics than to their shared education or household environment [4][10][13].

If we'd want to know someone's personality at an individual level, genetics isn't really a fruitful path; we can simply measure personality and get a very clear picture, genes wouldn't add anything there

— Michel Nivard (ScienceAlert)

Key Points

  • Population patterns: Michel Nivard said genetic data can connect personality to other genome-wide association findings across groups, but adds little when assessing one individual [2].
  • Trait framework: Scientists favor the Big Five over Myers-Briggs or Enneagram systems because the model measures traits on continuous dimensions rather than sorting people into fixed types [9].
  • Cross-population findings: Libertad Digital reported that some associations held across different populations, including extraversion-linked variants seen in young adults in the Netherlands [12].

Perspectives

Matthew Keller: The University of Colorado Boulder behavioral geneticist told Popular Science that personality reflects both genes and environment rather than a clean split between the two.

Sources: Popular Science

Ted Schwaba: The Michigan State University psychologist described personality as a difficult construct that includes traits, identity, life story, reputation and how other people perceive a person.

Brent Roberts: The University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign psychologist said skill, ability, interests and motivations also shape how personality expresses itself in behavior.
