Powerful El Niño nears peak, raising global flood and drought risks

Category: Science

A powerful El Niño is strengthening in the tropical Pacific and is expected to peak around December. Forecasters give it an 83% chance of remaining strong or very strong through early 2027. Sea-surface temperatures in the monitored Pacific region are already about 3.4°C above normal and could approach 4°C at the peak, according to climate scientists [5][2]. The warming shifts atmospheric circulation and rainfall, but its effects vary by region. Forecasts point to increased chances of storms across the southern United States, while drought is affecting parts of the Amazon and Indonesia. Atlantic hurricane activity has also been unusually low, while Pacific storms have been more active [1][6][4]. Scientists caution that the atmosphere’s response and the location of the warmest water shape regional impacts, so resemblance to a past event does not guarantee the same weather [2][16]. The event is also expected to raise global average temperatures. One projection puts 2026 at 1.52°C above preindustrial levels and 2027 at 1.74°C, although a single year above 1.5°C does not mark a permanent crossing of that long-term threshold [5]. Forecasters expect El Niño to persist into early 2027, while its precise regional effects remain uncertain [8][10].

Did you know: In 2015–16, warm, elevated Pacific water did not extend as far toward South America as it did in 1997; rainfall that winter was concentrated more over Central America and the southeastern United States, with significant flooding in Mexico [2].

Key Points

  • Coastal California: Near-shore waters from central Baja California to Sonoma County were more than 10°F above average; thermal expansion contributed an estimated 12 inches to coastal sea-level anomalies of 12–18 inches [3].
  • Typhoon activity: China’s National Climate Center counted 32 typhoons in the western North Pacific and South China Sea this year, the highest annual total since 2000; forecaster Zhao Junhu linked the active season partly to a northward shift in subtropical high pressure [14].
  • Health risks: The World Health Organization’s response plan for 2026–27 uses a Level 3 response category, citing potential illness and deaths linked to heat, drought, heavy rain, wildfire smoke and poor air quality [9].
  • East Africa: Somalia’s 1998 floods killed 2,000 people and forced 250,000 to flee; up to 80% of crops were destroyed in some areas, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization [15].
  • Climate attribution: Scientists say El Niño’s strength is not proof that climate change caused this event; research suggests warming may favor stronger events, but the record is limited and natural variability also matters [1][6].

Perspectives

Emily Becker, research associate professor at the University of Miami, said the current event’s atmospheric response is about tied with that of 1997–98, despite much higher sea-surface temperatures. She warned that hotter ocean conditions elsewhere could amplify rainfall impacts [16].

Ken Graham, director of the U.S. National Weather Service, said more rain and snow could help drought-stricken areas, but intense rain on dry ground can trigger flash floods and mudslides [18].

Bramwell Simiyu, Nairobi County disaster-management chief, said Nairobi’s drainage system was designed 30 years ago for about 2.5 million residents, compared with roughly seven million people in the city during the day. He estimated a full upgrade would cost up to $192 million [15].

Timeline

  • June 2026: The World Meteorological Organization said the current El Niño had been declared [8].
  • August 2026: Niño 3.4 sea-surface temperatures averaged 2.5°C above normal [8].
  • 8 October 2026: A NOAA outlook gave an 83% chance of strong or very strong conditions in early 2027 [2].
  • December 2026: Forecasters expect the event to reach its peak [8][1].

Technical Details

  • Niño 3.4 index: The index tracks rolling three-month sea-surface temperature departures from normal in a reference area of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. The current event’s unusually high readings measure ocean conditions, not local weather forecasts [16][8].
  • Satellite altimetry: Warm water expands and raises sea levels, allowing satellites to detect El Niño-related patterns. NASA’s comparison used TOPEX/Poseidon measurements from October 13, 1997, and Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich measurements from October 12, 2026, with seasonal effects and long-term trends removed [2].

Industry Impact

  • Agriculture and water: Ghana’s meteorological agency warned that hotter, drier conditions and prolonged dry spells could reduce farm yields and lower reservoir levels, affecting household water supply and hydropower generation [12].
  • California water and emergency planning: Over 14 days, California’s forecast sequence of storms could bring rainfall totals above the November monthly average across much of the state. The first heavy rains may prime watersheds for faster runoff in later storms [3].

Scientific Significance

  • After El Niño’s heat pulse fades, a strong La Niña could temporarily lower global temperatures. Global temperatures in 2022, a La Niña year, were still higher than in 1998, an El Niño year [5].
  • NOAA’s Relative Oceanic Niño Index compares Pacific warming with conditions across the wider tropical oceans. By that measure, the current event is among the strongest on record, but not far above earlier events [16].
  • Possible climate feedbacks: Researchers have raised the possibility that additional sea-ice loss, permafrost thaw and wildfire emissions could retain some warming that would otherwise be temporary. The scale and likelihood of such feedbacks remain uncertain [5].

Historical Background

Direct sea-surface temperature records cited by climate scientists date to the 19th century, with the previous record high set during the 1877 El Niño. Coral and tree-ring records allow estimates back to about 1100 [1][5]. The 1997–98 and 2015–16 events were linked to weather disruptions including fires in Indonesia and the Amazon, drought-related crop failures in southern Africa, and heavy rain and coastal flooding in Southern California [2].

Humanitarian Impact

In Kenya, officials estimate up to 100,000 people in low-income settlements could be at risk in a worst-case flood scenario; the International Organization for Migration warned that almost 900,000 people could be displaced nationally this year, mostly because of predicted floods [15]. In Somalia, the disaster-management agency warned that 1.2 million people could face displacement from extreme weather, including both floods and drought [15].

Q&A

Q: How far ahead can forecasters make a reliable local winter forecast?

A: The NOAA Climate Prediction Center uses seasonal averages and probabilities rather than specific local forecasts. The North Carolina report says NOAA does not make specific temperature or precipitation predictions more than about a week ahead [17].
