Umbra unveils Freetail 5 radar satellites with five-centimeter imaging

Category: Science

U.S. space technology company Umbra unveiled Freetail 5, a synthetic-aperture radar satellite model designed to produce images with resolution as fine as 5 centimeters. The first spacecraft is expected to launch in early 2028; Umbra says it will weigh about 200 kilograms and provide hourly global revisit capability [2][3]. Finnish satellite maker ICEYE is marketing its next-generation Gen5 radar spacecraft, which offers 11-centimeter imagery to government customers and 16-centimeter imagery to commercial customers. Commercial service is expected to begin in 2027 [3]. Radar can collect images through cloud cover that can hinder optical satellites, and governments in Europe are seeking sovereign Earth-imaging capabilities [3].

Time to decision is everything in space-based intelligence, whether you’re tracking a vessel or responding to a flood.

— Rafal Modrzewski, ICEYE CEO and co-founder (Breaking Defense)

Key Points

  • Expanded missions: Umbra says Freetail can support formation flight, single-pass 3D imaging, maritime search and direct-to-theater data downlink [2].
  • Radar upgrades: Umbra says Freetail 5 has four times the radar power and twice the antenna area of its previous spacecraft [2][3].
  • ICEYE approach: ICEYE plans to add an onboard artificial intelligence processor to screen and preprocess imagery in orbit rather than wait for data to reach the ground [3].

Perspectives

Umbra: CEO David Langan said the company is building a compact spacecraft to deliver the performance and mission flexibility of much larger systems for the United States and its allies.

Sources: Satellite Today

ICEYE: The company is pitching Gen5 to civil, commercial and military customers, broadening its target market beyond government users [3].

Sources: Breaking Defense

Industry Impact

  • Earth-imaging market: Both companies are offering imagery finer than the industry's previous standard of around 25 centimeters, allowing customers to see smaller ground targets with higher fidelity [3].
