Alberta rejects separation vote as First Nations legal challenge continues

Category: Canada

Alberta voters rejected starting the legal process for a binding separation referendum, with about 75% choosing to remain in Canada and 25% backing the process in the Oct. 19 vote, according to the complete but unofficial count. Elections Alberta expects to release official results on Oct. 29 [15][12]. Voters supported proposals on immigration and citizenship rules but rejected all four proposed constitutional changes [8][7]. Premier Danielle Smith has said she will pursue the measures that passed and work with Prime Minister Mark Carney on immigration [8][12]. Separately, the Alberta government is continuing its appeal of a ruling that quashed a citizen-led separation petition. First Nations chiefs and Alberta’s opposition are urging it to withdraw the appeal; the government says the court decision contained errors of law [9].

Primary Source: View original article

The big question now is … remain and do what?

— Preston Manning, former Calgary MP and founder of the Reform Party of Canada (National Post)

Key Points

  • Rural divide: Only two ridings had majority support for moving toward a separation vote: Rimbey-Rocky Mountain House-Sundre, at 51.1%, and Central Peace-Notley, at 50.4%. Support was 15% in Edmonton and 18% in Calgary [1][7].
  • Constitutional proposals: The four rejected proposals covered electing judges, abolishing the Senate, opting out of some federal programs while keeping funding, and allowing provincial laws to override conflicting federal laws. Each would require support from other provinces [8].
  • Petition threshold: The separatist group Stay Free Alberta gathered more than 300,000 signatures, nearly twice the legal threshold [10].
  • Turnout: Nearly 2.1 million people voted, about 64% of eligible voters, in the province’s largest electoral event [12][7][15].

Perspectives

Siksika Nation Chief Samuel Crowfoot: He urged the Alberta government to drop its appeal, arguing the referendum made the separation petition issue moot and that withdrawing could help repair the province’s relationship with First Nations [9].

Sources: CBC News

Alberta Justice Minister Mickey Amery: He said the province will continue its appeal because the judge’s decision contained errors of law, and argued citizens should not have to meet a duty to consult to gather petition signatures [9].

Sources: CBC News

Stay Free Alberta petition leader Mitch Sylvestre: He wants the appeal to continue, saying a ruling against the petition could make it harder for citizens to put other questions to a vote [9].

Sources: CBC News

Preston Manning, former Calgary MP and Reform Party of Canada founder: He warned against interpreting the separation question’s defeat as evidence that Western grievances have disappeared, and called for federal-provincial negotiations over jurisdiction and autonomy [2].

Sources: National Post

Timeline

  • 19 February 2026: Smith announced the referendum, citing concerns over immigration [12].
  • May 2026: A judge quashed approval of the separation petition after finding consultation failures [9].
  • 19 October 2026: Albertans voted on separation and nine additional provincial policy questions [2][12].
  • 23 October 2026: Legal scholar Kathleen Mahoney said the government’s appeal could clarify when consultation is required; she noted the case would continue because the petition leader is appealing too [9].

Historical Background

Under a 1991 immigration accord with Ottawa, Quebec has exclusive control over selecting incoming economic migrants. Premier Danielle Smith has repeatedly cited that arrangement as a model for Alberta [7].

Q&A

Q: What happens if the appeal succeeds?

A: If an appeals court rules that consultation could have occurred later in the process, the government would have to deal with the original citizen petition; no deadline is set. If it upholds the lower court’s ruling, the petition process ends [9].
