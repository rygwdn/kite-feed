Anthropic mathematician claims AI-assisted proof of Riemann hypothesis

Category: Science

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at artificial intelligence company Anthropic, said on Nov. 20 that he and the company’s Claude model had proved the Riemann hypothesis, a 167-year-old conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers. He released a formal proof written in 17 million lines of Lean code, which lets software check mathematical steps [3][10]. The Lean Focused Research Organization and Mathlib, a volunteer-led mathematics project, said they had “full confidence” in the proof’s soundness and confirmed that its code compiles [3]. The full argument has not yet been explained in a readily understandable form. The machine-checkable proof establishes a result without, by itself, showing researchers why it works or how to build on it [3][16]. Mathematicians are working to digest the proof and extract its ideas. The result does not break RSA encryption, which depends on the difficulty of factoring large numbers, not on proving how prime numbers are distributed [5][14].

Primary Source: View original article

I don't think this kind of proof would ever come from a human.

— Abhishek Saha, professor of mathematics at Queen Mary University of London (WIRED)

Did you know: The generalized Riemann hypothesis can help mathematicians determine which numbers can be written as a square plus another square plus seven times a third square [5].

Key Points

  • Proof dissection: More than 60 people were using AI agents to translate sections of the proof, and contributors had produced explanations for over 3,000 segments. One mathematician estimated the work could take months [3].
  • AI research: OpenAI released 722 AI-generated mathematics papers the month before Alpöge’s announcement. Some were later retracted after errors were found [1].
  • Small result: Mathematicians have already extracted one smaller result from Alpöge’s proof and written it up separately [3].

Perspectives

Association for Human Mathematics: The group argued that the announcement prioritizes technical validity over clarity and accountability, and said no person can reasonably claim to understand the document [3][2].

Sources: WIRED

David Manderscheid, mathematician: Manderscheid argues that graduate mathematics programs should ask students not only to solve problems but also to explain and contextualize proofs, since AI can now complete work once assigned to doctoral students [1].

Sources: The Conversation

Cybersecurity experts: Experts urged security teams to inventory cryptographic systems, replace obsolete 1024-bit RSA keys and continue planned migrations to post-quantum standards [14].

Sources: Computerworld

Timeline

  • 1859: Riemann formulated his conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers [10]
  • 2000: The Clay Mathematics Institute named it one of seven Millennium Prize Problems [10]
  • October 2026: OpenAI released 722 AI-generated mathematics papers [1]
  • 21 November 2026: The Lean Focused Research Organization and Mathlib expressed confidence in the compiled proof [3]

Industry Impact

  • Cryptography: Beyond the Riemann hypothesis, Computerworld reported that Claude Mythos found a mathematical flaw in HAWK, a proposed post-quantum signature standard, and a faster attack on weakened AES; AI agents have also reduced the cost of running existing software on graphics chips [14].

Scientific Significance

  • Mathematical methods: AI systems can draw on tools across mathematical fields, but traditional work on proofs also produces connections, shortcuts and methods that other researchers can reuse [16].
  • Limits of AI-led discovery: So far, AI has tackled problems people already identified as important; there is no evidence it can independently recognize or formulate significant open questions [16].

Historical Background

Riemann used the zeta function to explain discrepancies between Gauss’s estimates of prime counts and the actual counts; if true, the hypothesis would reveal order in that distribution [10]. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute named it one of seven Millennium Prize Problems and offered $1 million for a solution [10][14]. The institute’s prize program was intended to encourage new mathematical ideas, not simply the completion of a list of challenges, according to a September open letter signed by 28 Fields Medal winners [2].
