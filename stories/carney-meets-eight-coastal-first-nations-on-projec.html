Carney meets eight Coastal First Nations on projects

Category: Canada

Prime Minister Mark Carney is meeting Coastal First Nations leaders in Prince Rupert, B.C., on Tuesday to discuss proposed “nation-building” resource and infrastructure projects alongside ocean conservation priorities [1][2][4][5][6][7]. The talks come after Coastal First Nations and other B.C. Indigenous leaders said they were not properly consulted on an Ottawa–Alberta energy agreement that would set the stage for a new oil pipeline to the West Coast and could reopen debate about the federal tanker ban [2][6]. Carney is expected to be joined by Natural Resources Minister Tim Hodgson, as Ottawa works to rebuild trust and clarify how Indigenous communities would participate in decisions affecting their rights and territories [3][6].

Did you know: The Great Bear Rainforest on B.C.’s Central Coast is referenced in the coverage as the region where the Coastal First Nations alliance is based [6].

Key Points

  • Great Bear coast: Coastal First Nations is an alliance of eight Nations in B.C.’s Central Coast region, an area defined as the Great Bear Rainforest [6].
  • Firm opposition: The alliance has publicly rejected the idea of a pipeline terminating at Prince Rupert or Kitimat and says it does not support oil tankers on the coast [6].
  • AFN support: The Assembly of First Nations passed a resolution in December rejecting the Ottawa–Alberta agreement and pledging support for Coastal First Nations [6].
  • Overseas pitch: The Prince Rupert meeting is happening ahead of Carney’s major overseas trip aimed at seeking new markets and investment for Canadian goods [2].
  • Rocky start: Natural Resources Minister Tim Hodgson’s relationship with Coastal First Nations has been described as getting off to a rocky start, adding pressure for clearer consultation going forward [3].

Perspectives

Coastal First Nations leadership: Leaders have said they oppose a new oil pipeline to the coast and do not want oil tankers on their waters.

Technical Details

  • Tanker ban: The articles refer to a federal ban on oil tankers on B.C.’s coast; the Ottawa–Alberta accord is described as potentially reopening the issue, which matters because it affects whether marine shipping would accompany any new West Coast pipeline [2].
  • Energy accord: The Ottawa–Alberta energy agreement is described as an intergovernmental deal that, among other things, “sets the stage” for a new oil pipeline to the West Coast, triggering concerns about Indigenous consultation and consent [2][6].
  • Consultation and decision-making: Carney has pointed to “true decision-making authority” for First Nations on major projects affecting their rights, highlighting that project planning is not only engineering and economics, but also governance and rights-based processes [6].

Industry Impact

  • Project permitting: The meeting signals that consultation gaps can quickly become a constraint on timelines for pipelines and other large developments, making early engagement with rights holders a practical factor in whether projects proceed [2][6].
  • Marine conservation: By placing ocean conservation on the agenda alongside major projects, Ottawa is framing coastal development decisions as involving both economic planning and stewardship, which could shape the conditions attached to future proposals [1][4].
  • Regional economy: The federal government is presenting the talks as a way for Indigenous communities to be included in efforts to strengthen the Canadian economy, suggesting future policy emphasis on Indigenous participation in large-scale development benefits [1][5].

Historical Background

Canada has maintained a moratorium on crude oil tankers along British Columbia’s north coast in recent years, which is why any discussion of changing a “tanker ban” tends to draw intense attention in coastal communities [2]. In late 2025, an energy accord between Ottawa and Alberta surprised Coastal First Nations leaders, who said they were caught off guard, and the agreement outlined pathways toward a new pipeline to the West Coast and a potential reconsideration of the tanker ban [2][6].
