Early outer Solar System bodies formed mostly from rock, study finds

Category: Science

Bodies that formed in the outer Solar System during its first million years consisted of 83% to 92% chondrules—rock fragments created by intense heat—with little icy dust, an international team reports in Nature Astronomy [1][2]. The study examined the composition of the earliest planetesimals, the building blocks of planets and moons. The researchers inferred the mix from iron meteorites that preserve chemical traces of their parent bodies. They found more ice in bodies that formed later, consistent with a shift toward icy material after about two million years [1][2].

Primary Source: View original article

Our work shows that this assembly process was remarkably selective from the very beginning.

— Damanveer Grewal, Earth and planetary scientist at Yale University (ScienceAlert)

Key Points

  • Sorting mechanism: The researchers attribute chondrules’ early dominance to aerodynamic sorting: their stronger, more compact structure made them more likely to clump together [1][2].
  • Missing evidence: Many early chondrules are absent from the meteorite record because internal heating later melted their parent rocks, erasing traces of the earlier material [1][2].

Technical Details

  • Composition tracers: The team used estimates of sulfur abundance, which is higher in matrix than in chondrules, and iron oxidation, which indicates contributions from water ice and oxidized dust [1][2].

Scientific Significance

  • Inference limits: Both tracer methods require estimates about the larger planetesimals from which the iron meteorite fragments came, so the conclusion rests on indirect evidence [1][2].
  • Change in composition: The cause of the later shift toward icy material remains unclear [1][2].
