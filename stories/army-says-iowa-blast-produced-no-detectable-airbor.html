Army says Iowa blast produced no detectable airborne lead; testing continues

Category: Science

The U.S. Army says air monitors detected no airborne lead after it detonated the nation’s stockpile of degraded lead azide Wednesday at its Iowa ammunition plant. Officials later lifted the shelter-in-place order for nearby residents [6][15][27]. The blast destroyed 68 barrels of the explosive, which the Army said had become unstable in storage and could detonate without warning [3][26]. The initial air readings are not the final environmental assessment. The Iowa Department of Health and Human Services said it would review soil-sampling results before deciding whether further public-health advice is needed. The Army and Federal Emergency Management Agency said more testing and updates would follow [15][27]. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency said it had collected more than 1,200 air, land and water samples before the blast and would continue monitoring. NASA deployed research aircraft and used its space-based TEMPO pollution instrument to collect environmental and atmospheric data, while independent researchers planned further testing. Laboratory analysis of environmental samples is expected to take days to weeks, Purdue University environmental engineering professor Andrew Whelton said [15][9][27].

Key Points

  • NASA monitoring: NASA deployed a P-3 Orion, a Gulfstream III and a B200 research aircraft, and used its space-based TEMPO pollution instrument to observe the event [9][10].
  • Follow-up guidance: Whelton said eventual recommendations could differ among communities based on distance from the detonation area and whether they were upwind or downwind [27].

Perspectives

U.S. Army: Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley called the operation successful and said mitigation measures reduced the risk to nearby residents to as close to zero as possible [15].

  • 23 November 2026: A federal appeals court ruling put the detonation back on track, and the Army set the blast for Wednesday [3][16]
  • 25 November 2026: The Army carried out the blast; officials later issued an all-clear [6][27]

Historical Background

The national stockpile was transferred to the Iowa ammunition plant in 2009 and retained for possible future use [3][12]. The military had moved away from using lead azide because of its sensitivity and handling risks [7]. An earlier disposal in Colorado in 1977 left 75% of lead particulate at the site without mitigation, according to Army explosives expert Col. Aaron Teller [1].
