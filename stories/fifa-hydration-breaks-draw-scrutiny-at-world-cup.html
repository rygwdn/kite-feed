FIFA hydration breaks draw scrutiny at World Cup

Category: Science

FIFA’s mandatory three-minute hydration breaks in each half of every 2026 World Cup match have become a point of dispute, with fans, players and coaches criticizing the pauses despite FIFA’s December statement that it introduced them as a player-welfare measure [1][3][5]. The rule applies at all venues, including stadiums with roofs or air conditioning. It replaced the previous system, in which cooling breaks were triggered only when wet-bulb temperatures reached 32 degrees Celsius, or about 90 degrees Fahrenheit [3][5]. The Guardian reported that two of the first 24 matches, Saudi Arabia against Uruguay in Miami and Sweden against Tunisia in Monterrey, were played in wet-bulb temperatures of 28C or above at stadiums without air conditioning [4]. Fifpro, the global players’ union, has previously argued that games at 28C or above should be delayed or postponed [4]. Scientific American reported that teams have tried measures ranging from hydration breaks to cooling-gel vests, while physiologists and a World Cup team doctor said feeling cooler does not necessarily mean core body temperature has fallen [2].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Venue differences: Four other first-round games took place in cities above the 28C wet-bulb level, but air conditioning mitigated conditions inside those stadiums [4].
  • Fan reaction: Spectators booed the stoppages during England-Croatia and Canada’s 6-0 win over Qatar at BC Place in Vancouver [3][5].
  • Tactical effect: Emma Hayes wrote that momentum shifted after several breaks and that the stoppages give coaches time to analyze and adjust [8].
  • Commercial concern: STA reported that broadcasters welcomed the mandatory pauses because they create mid-match advertising slots [7].

Perspectives

FIFA: FIFA declined Grist’s request for comment, referring to its December statement that said hydration breaks were meant to ensure the best possible conditions for players.

Sources: Grist

Emma Hayes: Hayes said she dislikes the interruptions but understands why FIFA standardized them, because limiting breaks to hot cities could give some teams extra tactical time.

Sources: The Guardian

Didier Deschamps: The France manager said the breaks can split matches and interrupt momentum, leaving players and coaches to adapt.
