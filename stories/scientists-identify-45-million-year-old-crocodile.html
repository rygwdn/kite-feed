Scientists identify 45-million-year-old crocodile species in Sphinx limestone

Category: Science

Paleontologists have identified Herugavialis cairensis, an extinct crocodilian, from fossils recovered in Egypt’s 45-million-year-old Mokattam Formation, which supplied limestone for the Great Sphinx [6][7]. Researchers first unearthed the nearly complete skull in the 1920s and classified it as a different crocodile; CT scans and evolutionary analyses led them to place it in a newly recognized genus [5][7]. The animal belonged to the long-snouted gavial group and lived in shallow marine waters in what is now Egypt [7][6]. Fossils indicate that ancient gavials had a much wider range than the two living species, which are now found in Asia [7][11]. The researchers published their study in Royal Society Open Science in October 2026 [5][6].

Primary Source: View original article

Given this coincidence with the famously missing nose of the Great Sphinx, we decided to name the new crocodile after the Egyptian god Heru, who was possibly the inspiration for this iconic monument

— Philip Mannion (Popular Science)

Key Points

  • Likely diet: Its pointed teeth and long, narrow snout were suited to catching fish; the formation also preserves fossils of bony and cartilaginous fish [7].
  • Ancient diversity: Co-lead author Paul Burke counts Herugavialis among at least six gharial species documented in the Mediterranean over a 43-million-year span [7].

Perspectives

Philip Mannion, University College London: Mannion said researchers may find other large marine-animal fossils in the formation, noting prehistoric turtles as one possible example [7].

Sources: KRDO (CNN)

Alex Hastings, Science Museum of Minnesota: Hastings, who was not involved in the study, said the reclassification adds to knowledge of ancient Mediterranean crocodile diversity and helps researchers understand these predators’ ecosystems [7].

Sources: KRDO (CNN)

Timeline

  • 1920s: Researchers recovered the skull from Egypt’s Mokattam Formation [5][7]
  • 1927: Researchers classified the fossil as the extinct species Tomistoma cairense [7]
  • December 2023: Paul Burke began preparing an updated description of the fossil [7]
  • October 2026: Researchers published their description of the new genus and species in Royal Society Open Science [5][6]

Technical Details

  • CT examination: Researchers used computed tomography to inspect the fossil skull’s internal anatomy and identify differences from Tomistoma [6][7].
  • Specimens reviewed: Alongside the skull, researchers analyzed five other fossil specimens initially classified as Tomistoma cairense [7].

Scientific Significance

  • Museum collections: Reexamining museum fossils revealed previously overlooked diversity; researchers said such work often depends on the time and resources available to researchers and curators [7].
  • Regional extinction: Mediterranean crocodile diversity fluctuated with global temperature and sea level before crocodiles disappeared from the region as the Mediterranean dried about six million years ago [5][6].
  • Limits of the record: Researchers note that the fossil record may not capture all ancient Mediterranean gavial species; Burke said additional species could remain undiscovered [7].

Q&A

Q: Where else have fossil gharials been found?

A: The fossil record includes finds in the United Kingdom, Sweden, Italy, Egypt and Japan, among other places [7].
