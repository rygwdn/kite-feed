ESA revises BepiColombo maneuvers after Mercury insertion software fault

Category: Science

The European Space Agency (ESA) has revised BepiColombo’s remaining maneuvers after a software fault cut short the joint European-Japanese mission’s Mercury orbit-insertion burn. ESA says the revised sequence can correct the spacecraft’s trajectory without additional thruster burns, keeping the separation of its two science orbiters and the start of science operations on schedule [11][13][10]. The fault involved attitude guidance and thruster control, and the spacecraft entered a protective safe mode when the burn stopped. The spacecraft reached a stable Mercury orbit; ESA and its partners continue to investigate the software problem [4][11][8]. The European Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan’s Mio orbiter remain joined and are scheduled to separate on Dec. 9–10, with routine science operations planned for April 2027. The European orbiter is expected to reach its final orbit in March, while Mio will investigate Mercury’s magnetosphere [4][6][10].

After each burn, the Flight Dynamics team determines the achieved orbit to then design the next manoeuvre

— Frank Budnik, ESA BepiColombo Flight Dynamics Manager (The Register)

Key Points

  • Orbit adjustment: The insertion burn achieved about 77% of its planned velocity change, leaving BepiColombo in a 75-hour orbit rather than the planned 58-hour orbit [11][4][13].
  • Two investigations: The European Mercury Planetary Orbiter carries 11 instruments to survey the planet, while Japan’s Mio orbiter carries five instruments focused on its magnetosphere [6].
  • Weak-stability capture: BepiColombo entered orbit using a technique involving a slower approach than typical planetary insertions. Previously, only some missions to the Moon had used the technique [6][9].
  • X-ray instrument: Before arrival, the MIXS X-ray instrument imaged the Crab Nebula to help scientists measure its angular resolution [6].

Timeline

  • October 2018: BepiColombo launched on its mission to Mercury [16]
  • 3 September 2026: The transfer module separated, exposing instruments for testing [16]
  • 9 December 2026: Mio and the European orbiter are scheduled to separate [10][11]
  • 16 December 2026: Mio’s protective sunshield is scheduled to detach [10][6]
  • 10 March 2027: The European orbiter is expected to reach its final orbit [10][16]
  • 6 April 2027: BepiColombo’s science phase is scheduled to begin [10][6]

Technical Details

  • Compact X-ray optics: MIXS focuses X-rays with micro-pore optics rather than conventional nested mirrors. Its pores are about 20 microns wide, allowing the instrument to measure one meter and weigh 11 kilograms [16].
  • Surface X-ray fluorescence: MIXS will measure X-rays emitted when solar X-rays or magnetospheric particles strike Mercury’s surface. Their characteristic energies can identify surface elements, and the instrument is designed to produce higher-resolution images than earlier missions [16].

Scientific Significance

  • Simultaneous measurements: The two orbiters will observe Mercury from different orbits, allowing scientists to compare measurements taken at the same time; project scientist Geraint Jones described this as unprecedented for Mercury exploration [14].
  • Hollows research: Scientists hope to investigate Mercury’s mysterious surface hollows, which may form as volatile substances are driven off by intense solar heat [2].

Historical Background

Underperforming transfer-module thrusters in 2024 forced ESA and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency to revise BepiColombo’s route and delay its planned arrival [1]. The spacecraft’s journey included gravity-assist flybys of Earth, Venus and Mercury to reduce its speed before orbit insertion [9][16].
