Researchers identify new dinosaur genus from fossil found in England in 2001

Category: Science

Paleontologists have classified a fossil found in a Surrey clay quarry in 2001 as Paludohadros proudlovii, a previously unknown genus and species of plant-eating dinosaur that lived about 125 million years ago [1][2][3][5]. The four-legged animal was about 5 metres long and weighed an estimated 1.5 to 2 metric tons, but its bones indicate it had not reached adulthood [1][2]. The fossil was donated to London’s Natural History Museum, where it remained for years before a detailed comparison with other iguanodontians revealed distinguishing features [1][2]. The identification also helped researchers identify fossils collected from the same quarry in the 1950s as belonging to the newly named dinosaur [1][2]. The research, led by Ricardo Sacamoto, a PhD student at the Natural History Museum and the University of São Paulo, was published in the Journal of Systematic Palaeontology on Oct. 26, 2026. Sacamoto plans to continue examining the museum’s iguanodontian collection [1].

Paludohadros is the first species I’ve ever named and I’m honored to have been able to work on it. I still remember the incredible moment when I realized that this was a new species that no one had ever identified before.

— Ricardo Sacamoto, PhD student at the Natural History Museum and the University of São Paulo (SciTechDaily)

Key Points

  • Distinctive anatomy: Researchers distinguished the fossil by features including ridges on the upper arm bone, bumps on the shinbone and a curved pelvic bone with overlapping ridges [2].
  • Taxonomic revisions: A 2023 reassessment of Mantellisaurus helped paleontologists identify overlooked differences in fossils from southern England; other named iguanodontians include Brighstoneus, Comptonatus and Istiorachis [1][2].
  • Predator evidence: The fossil was found about 100 metres from Baryonyx remains, which include evidence of a young iguanodontian in the predator’s last meal, though researchers cannot identify the prey species [1][2].
  • Name dedication: The species name honours former quarry manager Will Proudlove, who died shortly before the fossil was found; the genus name refers to the swamp habitat and derives from the Latin word palus [1][2].

Perspectives

Rachael Proudlove, Will Proudlove’s widow, said the discovery had become part of her family’s story and that naming the dinosaur for her late husband would preserve his legacy [1][2].

  • 2001: The Paludohadros fossil was found and donated to the Natural History Museum [1][2]

  • 26 October 2026: Paludohadros research appeared in the Journal of Systematic Palaeontology [1]

Technical Details

  • Chewing teeth: Rows of ridged, diamond-shaped cheek teeth helped the herbivore process vegetation [2].
  • Preserved thumb spike: One fossilized thumb spike retains grooves where researchers say a protective keratin sheath likely attached [2].

Q&A

Q: Could Paludohadros have grown much larger than the fossil suggests?

A: The specimen’s bones were not fully developed, and researchers suggest a mature animal may have grown to roughly twice its estimated size [1][3].

Q: Were all the recently named southern English iguanodontians alive in the same place and at the same time?

A: Not necessarily: their fossils come from different geological formations and sedimentary basins, and researchers say differences could reflect habitat, geographic separation or different periods [2].
