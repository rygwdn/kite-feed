Astronomers witness dying giant star engulfing companion in real time

Category: Science

Astronomers watched a dying giant star in the Andromeda galaxy engulf and merge with its smaller companion, recording the process as it unfolded for the first time. The companion spiraled through the giant’s outer envelope for about seven years before the system erupted in October 2025 [1][2][3]. The observation provides direct evidence of a short-lived phase called common-envelope evolution, previously studied mainly through models. The James Webb Space Telescope observed the remnant 47 days after the eruption, but dust from expelled material now obscures the remnant. Researchers plan to monitor the system as it cools and settles over the coming years or decades [2][3][1].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Star masses: The giant had about seven times the Sun’s mass, while its companion had about 70% of the Sun’s mass [2].
  • Rapid outburst: The eruption peaked about a week after the system began to brighten, sooner than researchers expected [2].
  • Possible outcomes: In some other stellar pairs, the giant’s gas is expelled before a merger, leaving a close orbit that can eventually produce neutron stars, black holes or gravitational waves [2].

Perspectives

Viraj Karambelkar, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University and study co-author, said the remnant could have an unusually strong magnetic field and unusual chemical composition, temperature and brightness [1].

Sources: Scientific American

Technical Details

  • Orbital-energy loss: Gas drag drained orbital energy from the smaller star, while friction carried heat to the giant’s surface and produced gradual brightening [2].
  • Ejected material: The plunge hurled about one-tenth of the giant’s outer material into space, with gas and dust streaming mostly from its poles [2].

Scientific Significance

  • A different kind of collision: Earlier documented stellar collisions mostly involved young stars; this event involved an older, dying star with a diffuse envelope [1].
