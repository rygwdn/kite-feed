Scientists identify deep heat anomaly inside Mars

Category: Science

Researchers reported a large temperature imbalance inside Mars, estimating that the planet’s southern interior is about 200 to 400 degrees Celsius warmer than its northern interior and may be partially molten [2][3][4]. The study, led by Caltech alumnus Alexander Berne, now a postdoctoral associate at the University of Arizona, was published Aug. 27 in Nature [2][3][4]. The team used decades of gravity observations from NASA’s Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter to infer the planet’s internal structure [2][4]. Researchers said the finding may help explain long-running questions about Mars’ north-south surface divide, unusual magnetic remnants in southern rocks, seismic behavior measured by NASA’s InSight mission and evidence of the planet’s ancient water history [2][3][4].

Key Points

  • Surface divide: Mars’ southern hemisphere is higher, more heavily cratered and appears geologically older, while the northern hemisphere is lower, smoother and dominated by broad plains [3].
  • Possible causes: Researchers are examining several explanations, including a giant northern impact that removed heat, past self-driven convection in the southern mantle, or thick southern geological layers that trapped heat [3][4].
  • Magnetic clue: Iron-rich minerals in southern rocks contain unusual magnetic remnants that may point to a once-strong magnetic field linked to the hotter southern mantle [4].
  • Seismic behavior: NASA’s InSight mission found that seismic waves lost energy faster in the south, a pattern the higher-temperature interior could help explain [4].

Technical Details

  • Tidal tomography: Berne’s model uses tiny gravity-field variations to infer internal structure. The Mars study applied it to spacecraft tracking data collected over decades [2][4].
  • Spacecraft Doppler tracking: The researchers reconstructed Mars’ gravitational field by measuring very small changes in the velocities of orbiting spacecraft from three NASA missions [2].
  • Seasonal solar forcing: Mars’ slightly elliptical orbit and tilted rotation axis change the Sun’s gravitational pull over the Martian year, producing the tidal signal used in the analysis [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Interior contrast: The result extends the known hemispheric dichotomy from Mars’ surface into its interior, suggesting the planet’s deep mantle may not be globally symmetric [3][4].
  • Uncertainty: The cited temperature contrast is model-based, derived from gravity observations rather than direct sampling, and the sources state that scientists have not determined the cause of the imbalance [2][3][4].
  • Future work: Berne said more detailed gravitational-field data could support three-dimensional mapping of Mars’ interior and help distinguish among proposed origins for the anomaly [3].

Historical Background

Planetary scientists call Mars’ stark north-south contrast the hemispheric dichotomy [3]. NASA’s InSight mission previously supplied seismic measurements that researchers are now comparing with the gravity-based temperature model [4].
