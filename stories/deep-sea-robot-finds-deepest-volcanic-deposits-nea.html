Deep-sea robot finds deepest volcanic deposits near Challenger Deep

Category: Science

The remotely operated vehicle Jason/Medea has found what researchers describe as the deepest documented underwater pyroclastic deposits, on the Mariana Trench wall about 6,000 meters below sea level [1][3][5]. The rocks provide evidence of volcanic flows in the trench’s western forearc, where geologists expected little volcanic activity because the mantle is generally not hot enough to melt [1][4]. The findings were published Nov. 23 in Frontiers in Earth Science [3][5]. The team’s observations support the idea that the Pacific Plate bends steeply as it sinks beneath the Mariana plate. That could help explain Challenger Deep’s depth and bring magma-generating processes closer to the trench [5][4]. But researchers have not identified the eruption sites or established how eruptions could produce these deposits under immense water pressure. They say further sampling and radiometric dating are needed. Jason/Medea cannot reach the seafloor below 6,500 meters, while Challenger Deep extends to about 11,000 meters [1][4].

Imagining this happening is … pretty mind-boggling.

Key Points

  • Survey footprint: Jason/Medea made seven dives across roughly 300 kilometers of the forearc; researchers also examined the Shinaki Seep Field and retrieved fluid-sampling devices left by Japanese scientists in 2016 [1][2].
  • Unlocated source: The pyroclastic flows appear to have traveled only a short distance because they retained enough heat to fuse into hard rock. They would have lost that heat on a longer journey [1][4].

Perspectives

Sune Nielsen, a geochemist at the University of Lorraine, described the finding as perplexing and said there was no clear explanation [3].

Sources: Live Science

William Chadwick, a geologist at Oregon State University, said Challenger Deep may be uniquely suited to explosive volcanism in the super-deep ocean [5].

Sources: Science News

Technical Details

  • Supercritical fluid hypothesis: Researchers propose that water released from magma could enter a supercritical state under extreme pressure, potentially producing dense, fragmented deposits without the gas expansion typical of surface eruptions. This mechanism has not been confirmed [3][5].
  • Forearc extension: The study distinguishes mechanical plate stretching, inferred from older eastern rocks, from the addition of magma, suggested by younger western volcanic rocks. Additional sampling and radiometric dating are needed to establish the difference [1][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Distinctive trench segment: Unlike the better-known volcanic-arc pattern, the Challenger Deep segment has no organized volcanic arc; an ocean ridge lies there instead [3].
  • Potential regional effects: As the forearc stretches, the associated subduction zone may widen, which researchers say could affect plate motions in the western Pacific [1][4].

Historical Background

The Mariana Trench formed about 52 million years ago as the Pacific Plate began sliding beneath the Philippine Sea Plate [1][4].
