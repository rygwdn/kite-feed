Supplier breach exposes remote controls at French blood-storage sites

Category: Technology

An attack on a refrigeration contractor, rather than France’s national blood service, exposed remote monitoring and control systems connected to cold-storage rooms at three blood-service sites. Attackers changed temperature and defrost thresholds. The French Blood Establishment (EFS) said alarms alerted staff, who secured or moved products before they were damaged [4][3][16]. Refrigeration supplier Coolworld said the vulnerability involved a third-party component outside its direct control and that only a limited number of its units were affected [4][8]. Researchers have documented authentication-bypass and remote-command flaws in some Danfoss refrigeration controllers, but investigators have not confirmed that those flaws were used in the blood-storage attack [14][4]. Cybersecurity firms are examining decoy systems and other evidence from the wider wave of attacks, but experts have not established who carried them out. France’s cybersecurity agency and Health Ministry are investigating. Remote access to the affected refrigeration systems was disabled, and EFS reinforced monitoring [10][15][3].

Primary Source: View original article

Retailers need to act now. They should identify whether their refrigerator controllers are vulnerable, check if they are connected to the internet, ensure they've got the latest security updates and are supported by the manufacturer. And if they don’t even know, then remove them from the public internet immediately

— Frank Groenewegen, cybersecurity expert at Deloitte in The Hague (The National)

Key Points

  • Exposed controllers: Researchers counted about 1,000 Danfoss StoreView systems exposed globally; that count does not establish that those systems were involved in the blood-site attack [14].
  • Retail losses: Dutch retailer Ahold Delhaize estimated October’s refrigeration disruption could cut fourth-quarter sales by as much as €250 million and operating income by up to €100 million [8][16].

Perspectives

Ryan Dewhurst, a researcher at Spanish cybersecurity company Previdian: Dewhurst said simultaneous attacks and overnight temperature changes point to deliberate targeting and advance coordination, including preparation of a list of publicly reachable devices [10].

Sources: BBC

Sekoia, a French cyber-defence firm: Sekoia said its technical findings did not support claims of Russian-speaking involvement and noted that the group claiming responsibility was not known for effective operations beyond denial-of-service attacks [10].

Sources: BBC

Timeline

  • 2025: Researchers notified Danfoss of controller flaws under coordinated disclosure [14]
  • August 2026: Researchers publicly disclosed two vulnerabilities in Danfoss controllers [14]
  • October 2026: French supermarket refrigeration attacks disrupted hundreds of stores [4]
  • 2 November 2026: Attackers changed cold-storage temperature and defrost thresholds at three EFS sites [4][3]

Technical Details

  • Controller flaws: The disclosed vulnerabilities CVE-2025-41450 and CVE-2025-41451 affect Danfoss AK-SM 8xxA controllers. Researchers said one permits authentication bypass and remote code execution, but links to the current incident remain unverified [14][4].
  • Remote defrost control: Defrost functions are designed to clear ice buildup [4][14].

Industry Impact

  • Retail response: Sweden's Coop said affected stores were back in operation with their refrigeration systems disconnected from the internet. The chain was moving systems to 5G networks and reviewing potential vulnerabilities across its network [29].
  • Controller support: Copeland confirmed that some of its XWEB controllers were targeted in the latest attacks. It had previously said some XWEB devices involved in earlier attacks may no longer have been receiving support [14].

Historical Background

In October, attackers disrupted refrigeration controls at French supermarkets; technical reporting said some affected systems used older Danfoss software and remote defrost functions [4].
