Simulations estimate 170 million Milky Way black holes

Category: Science

Astronomers led by Flatiron Institute astrophysicist Tom Wagg used new computer simulations to estimate that the Milky Way may contain about 170 million stellar-mass black holes, the remnants of massive stars that collapsed after exhausting their fusion fuel [2][3][4]. The study has been submitted to American Astronomical Society journals and posted on arXiv while it awaits peer review [2][3][4]. The team modeled roughly 13.6 billion years of Milky Way evolution, using research on star formation, supernova explosions, black hole formation and galactic history to reconstruct a hidden “Galactic Underworld” [2][3][4]. The simulations also forecast where these black holes should be distributed, how massive they may be and what kinds of explosions formed them, giving astronomers targets and tests for future observations [2][3][4].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Formation rate: Spread across the Milky Way’s history, the estimate implies an average of roughly one new stellar-mass black hole every 80 years, though formation was more frequent when early star formation was more intense [3][4].
  • Birth kicks: The simulations suggest many black holes did not remain where they formed because supernova explosions can impart high velocities, with lower-mass black holes receiving stronger kicks that move them farther from the galactic plane [3].

Perspectives

Tom Wagg’s research team: The team presented the simulation as a predictive map for upcoming observations rather than a completed census.

Sources: ScienceAlert

Future observational astronomers: Researchers said later observations could test the number of hidden black holes, as well as their masses and motions.

Sources: Evrensel

Scientific Significance

  • The result gives a quantitative prediction for the Milky Way’s unseen stellar-remnant population rather than a direct count, making black hole demographics a testable model output [2][4].
  • The work remains a preprint and has not completed peer review, so its numerical estimate and distribution predictions should be treated as provisional until independent scrutiny and observations test them [2][3][4].
