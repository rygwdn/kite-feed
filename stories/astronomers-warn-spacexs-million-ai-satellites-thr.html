Astronomers warn SpaceX’s million AI satellites threaten research

Category: AI

Astronomy organizations warn that SpaceX’s proposed network of AI-computing satellites could disrupt scientific observations after the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) authorized up to 1 million Starmind spacecraft on Nov. 25. The American Astronomical Society said the satellites could be brighter and more numerous than existing objects, remain visible longer, and produce radio emissions and waste heat that could affect sensitive instruments [2][1]. Starmind would use computing hardware on solar-powered satellites to process AI workloads in orbit, then send results to Earth through SpaceX’s satellite network. The plan aims to meet AI’s demand for more computing capacity, but experts cite unresolved challenges in removing heat in a vacuum and moving large volumes of data. SpaceX warned in a regulatory filing that the technologies were unproven and might not be commercially viable [15][13][2]. A European Southern Observatory simulation estimated that satellite trails could obscure up to 28% of images captured by its Very Large Telescope in Chile under specified conditions, even if SpaceX substantially dims its satellites [5]. The FCC authorization sets deployment deadlines through 2040, requiring an operational satellite by November 2033 [2][4].

Key Points

  • Observatory disruption: A simulation by European Southern Observatory astronomer Olivier Hainaut estimated that hundreds or thousands of satellites could be visible at once, rivaling the number of stars visible under pristine conditions [5].
  • Instrument interference: The American Astronomical Society warned that radio emissions and infrared waste heat from the satellites could threaten sensitive instruments used in astronomy [37].
  • Strategic control: An expert raised concerns that one entity would control a capability of this scale and that it could become a target for malicious actors [53].
  • Launch dependency: SpaceX’s deployment plan depends heavily on lower launch costs from Starship, its reusable next-generation rocket, which is still under development [4].
  • Existing traffic: About 17,000 satellites were already in orbit when the FCC approved the plan [24][37].
  • Earth-side pressure: A Data Center Watch tally cited by ZME Science said local opposition delayed or derailed at least 120 U.S. data-center projects worth $198 billion in the first half of 2026 [5].
  • Satellite scale: SpaceX’s proposed AI1 spacecraft is designed with a wingspan of nearly 230 feet [33].
  • Orbital disposal condition: The FCC approved SpaceX’s debris plan only for satellites below 600 kilometers; the company must submit a final disposal plan before deploying satellites in higher orbits [3].
  • Communications: FCC approval allows radio-frequency links as a backup for telemetry, tracking and command if the satellites’ optical links fail [42].
  • Collision risks: SpaceX can maneuver functioning satellites to avoid potential collisions, but failed satellites cannot maneuver and could pose a danger in orbit [56].
  • Visible objects: Astronomer Michael Brown says each proposed satellite would be about 75 metres wide and brighter than any star in the early evening [56].
  • Orbit design: The FCC order places the satellites in orbital shells between 500 and 1,002 kilometers, with 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations [42].
  • Other projects: Blue Origin has asked the FCC to approve up to 51,600 satellites for Project Sunrise [33].
  • Environmental risks: Launches and reentries could pollute the atmosphere, while in-orbit collisions could create debris clouds that threaten other satellites [1].

Technical Details

  • Hardware reliability: Unlike terrestrial servers, orbital processors are difficult to repair or replace. Technical challenges outlined for Starmind include the need for radiation protection and fault-tolerant hardware [9].
  • Distributed computing clusters: SpaceX executive Michael Nicholls described clusters of roughly 10 satellites linked at about 10 terabits per second, with connections to Starlink and the wider constellation [42].
  • Thermal radiation: In orbit, air cannot carry heat away from processors; the proposed satellites would have to release waste heat as infrared radiation through radiators [15][9].
  • Compute payload: SpaceX’s proposed payload is rated for roughly 175 kilowatts of average computing power and up to 250 kilowatts at peak; these are design specifications, not demonstrated operating results [9].
  • Payload power: SpaceX’s planned AI1 satellite is rated to provide its computing hardware with 120 kilowatts of sustained power and 150 kilowatts at peak [33].
  • Instrument risks: The American Astronomical Society also cited radio emissions and waste heat as threats to sensitive instruments used in astronomy research [37].

Scientific Significance

  • Disposal requirements: The FCC has not approved SpaceX’s proposal to send retired satellites into solar orbit and requires a final debris plan before deployment above 600 kilometers [33].
  • Atmospheric uncertainty: Scientists have raised concerns that repeated satellite burn-ups could deposit metals in the upper atmosphere, including aluminium compounds that researchers have linked to ozone destruction. The projected scale depends on the satellites’ eventual operating lifespan and replacement rate [56][53].
  • Proposed limit: Hainaut recommended limiting the total satellite population to approximately 100,000 sufficiently dim satellites to protect astronomical observations [5].
  • Environmental effects: Critics also cited atmospheric pollution from satellite launches and reentries as a potential concern [32].
  • Hardware resilience: Experts identify processors’ ability to withstand space radiation and micrometeorite impacts as unresolved obstacles [30].

Historical Background

Satellite operators sometimes seek approval for more satellites than they ultimately intend to deploy to preserve flexibility in their network designs. SpaceX sought approval for 42,000 Starlink satellites, while just over 11,000 are now in orbit [4][24].

Q&A

Q: Does approval mean SpaceX will launch all one million satellites?

A: Not necessarily. Operators may request approval for more satellites than they intend to deploy. SpaceX sought clearance for 42,000 Starlink satellites and has put just over 11,000 in orbit [24].

Q: What disposal review remains before some Starmind satellites can be deployed?

A: The FCC deferred SpaceX’s proposal to place retired satellites in solar orbit and requires a final debris plan before deployment above 600 kilometers [33].

Q: Why could the highest satellite orbits raise disposal concerns?

A: The FCC approved disposal planning for satellites below 600 kilometers but deferred deployment in higher orbits until SpaceX submits a final disposal plan. The company had not supplied enough information for the FCC to assess its proposed methods [3].
