European-Japanese BepiColombo reaches Mercury before orbit-insertion burn

Category: Science

BepiColombo, a joint mission of the European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, reached Mercury on Saturday after an eight-year, roughly 6-billion-mile journey, as controllers prepared a braking maneuver to place the spacecraft in orbit [9][11][1]. The mission would be only the second to orbit Mercury and the first to send two orbiters there together [3][19]. The spacecraft are still attached as they approach the planet. After further engine burns, the European Mercury Planetary Orbiter will separate from Japan’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, called Mio, on Dec. 9–10; full science operations are scheduled to begin in April 2027 [19][20][9]. The orbiters will investigate Mercury’s surface and interior, its unusually large metal core, its magnetic field and its thin atmosphere. Scientists also want to examine ice in permanently shadowed craters and puzzling surface hollows. The mission is expected to conduct the first close study of Mercury’s south polar region [1][5][3].

Key Points

  • Gravity assists: The spacecraft used nine planetary flybys—one of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury—to shed speed and adjust its path toward the Sun’s innermost planet [1][3][5].
  • Orbit design: Mio is expected to follow an approximately 9.3-hour elliptical orbit, while the European orbiter will circle Mercury roughly every 2.3 hours [7][10].
  • Magnetic measurements: Both spacecraft will measure Mercury’s magnetic field at the same time, allowing researchers to study how it responds to the solar wind [3].
  • Surface hollows: Thousands of shallow, steep-sided hollows are not impact craters, and researchers still do not know how they formed [5][14].
  • Surface composition: BepiColombo’s Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer will analyze the geochemical composition of Mercury’s surface [6].

Perspectives

Emma Bunce, a University of Leicester professor and principal investigator for the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer, described competing explanations for Mercury’s disproportionately large metal core: collisions may have stripped away the planet’s outer layers, or it may have formed in a metal-rich region [5][9].

Sources: BBC News, The Guardian

Shoshana Weider, NASA’s Mercury science lead, said many once considered reaching Mercury orbit impossible because of the difficulty of slowing a spacecraft enough for the planet’s gravity to capture it [3].

Sources: Scientific American

Timeline

  • October 2018: BepiColombo launched from French Guiana on its Mercury-bound journey [5].
  • April 2024: A propulsion-system problem delayed the planned arrival by about a year [10][7].
  • 3 September 2026: The transfer module separated from the spacecraft pair as arrival operations began [20][17].
  • 9 December 2026: The European and Japanese orbiters are scheduled to separate [19][20].
  • April 2027: The mission’s full science operations are scheduled to start [9][19].

Scientific Significance

  • Mercury’s changing surface: As the planet cools, it appears to be shrinking and forming large escarpments; some thrust faults may still be moving, suggesting its tectonic activity continues [3].
  • Magnetic field: Mercury and Earth are the only rocky planets in the solar system with global magnetic fields; Mercury’s field is about 100 times weaker than Earth’s [3].
  • Mission duration: New Scientist reports a primary science mission of one Earth year, with a possible one-year extension [7].

Historical Background

Before BepiColombo, only two spacecraft missions had visited Mercury: NASA’s Mariner 10 flew past the planet three times in 1974 and 1975, and MESSENGER orbited it from 2011 to 2015 [3][5]. MESSENGER found evidence of ice in polar craters, but the ice has not been directly observed [8].

Q&A

Q: Could BepiColombo lead to a future Mercury lander?

A: The mission could inform plans for the first Mercury lander. Scientists have said that after BepiColombo, they will have completed the orbital investigations they can currently envision [3].
