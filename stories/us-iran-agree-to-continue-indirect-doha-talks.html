US, Iran agree to continue indirect Doha talks

Category: World

Qatari and Pakistani mediators said U.S. and Iranian negotiators met separately in Doha and made “positive progress” on issues tied to the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding. They said they would schedule the next round after funeral processions for Iran’s former Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei [3][8][9][32][38][52]. Iran’s Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said participants agreed to set up a communication channel by Thursday to report and record violations of the memorandum [3][5][13][17][21][24]. Sources familiar with the talks said negotiators spent two days discussing maritime traffic in the Strait of Hormuz and frozen Iranian funds, while the technical sessions did not address Iran’s nuclear programme [3][39][45][56]. President Trump told reporters that “the denuclearisation of Iran is moving along well,” but Vice President JD Vance said nuclear issues would be addressed later [3][39][45][19]. The talks followed an interim accord intended to halt the war that began with U.S.-Israeli strikes on Iran in February, reopen the Strait of Hormuz and create a 60-day window for negotiations on a permanent agreement [2][44][60][58]. Several accounts reported no clear breakthrough toward a lasting peace, and neither side announced resolutions to core disputes over Hormuz, Lebanon or a final nuclear settlement [16][39][45][61].

Key Points

  • Talks format: The two sides did not meet face to face. They met separately with Qatari and Pakistani mediators during lower-level technical sessions [4][9][45][59].
  • Envoy role: Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Qatar’s leadership to discuss the negotiations but did not take part in the technical sessions, sources cited in several accounts said [4][15][45][50].
  • Assets dispute: Iranian officials discussed frozen assets in Qatar and said the funds would be used to buy needed goods, while U.S. officials denied that a partial release agreement had been reached [10][11][20][50].
  • Hormuz leverage: Iran wants recognition of its control over the Strait of Hormuz and the ability to levy fees on ships entering or leaving the Gulf, according to senior Iranian sources cited by Reuters [58][60][47].

Perspectives

Iranian delegation: Kazem Gharibabadi said participants were forming follow-up mechanisms, but negotiations with Washington on a final deal had not begun.

Industry Impact

  • Energy markets: About one-fifth of global oil and liquefied natural gas trade passed through the Strait of Hormuz before the war, making the waterway central to energy pricing and supply risk [31][60][58].
  • Shipping: Commercial traffic has partially resumed through the strait, but Iran’s reported push for route control and transit fees leaves shipping arrangements unsettled [60][58][47].
  • Oil prices: Prices fell after reports of progress in the talks, with Bursa citing Reuters that prices reached their lowest level in four months [1][47].

Business Angle

  • Energy-linked companies could benefit from lower oil prices if the Hormuz shipping channel stays open under rules that insurers and vessel operators can price reliably [1][47][common].
  • The potential for Iranian-imposed transit fees and route-approval demands could create persistent legal and insurance uncertainty for shipping companies, keeping war-risk premiums high even if the strait remains open to some traffic [58][60][47].
  • Sanctions relief and frozen-asset releases could affect Iran’s purchasing power, but sources disagree over how much money is being released and under what conditions [11][20][50].
