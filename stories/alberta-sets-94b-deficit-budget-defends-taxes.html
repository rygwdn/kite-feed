Alberta sets $9.4B deficit budget, defends taxes

Category: Canada

Alberta’s government has tabled a new provincial budget that raises overall spending while projecting a $9.4-billion deficit this year, with more deficits expected in the years ahead [3][4]. Finance Minister Nate Horner has been out defending the plan, including a stop in Calgary, while stressing the province is not considering a provincial sales tax as it tries to keep up with growth and pressure on services [4][3]. The budget has drawn pushback on several fronts. Calgary city hall is zeroing in on a provincial, property-tax-related increase; Mayor Jeromy Farkas called it “incredibly egregious” and set a special council meeting for March 4 to debate the city’s response [7]. Separately, francophone advocates got unwelcome news: funding for French-language initiatives dips slightly even as overall provincial spending rises, according to Le Devoir [1]. Politically, Premier Danielle Smith has tied Alberta’s deficit to Ottawa’s immigration policies. The Globe and Mail reported the budget documents themselves don’t make that link; when asked, Horner said he didn’t have numbers on how newcomers affected the province’s bottom line [2].

Key Points

  • Immigration claim vs budget documents: The Globe and Mail reported Smith blamed Ottawa’s immigration policies for the deficit, but the budget documents don’t draw that connection; Horner said he didn’t have figures on newcomers’ net fiscal impact [2].
  • Horner’s justification: Horner said the government’s options were constrained (“hands were tied”) as it tried to keep up with growth and rising service demands [3].
  • Calgary response: Calgary’s mayor called the provincial property-tax-related increase “incredibly egregious” and called a special council meeting for March 4 [7].
  • French funding: Le Devoir reported Alberta’s funding for French-language initiatives is down slightly even as overall provincial spending increases [1].
  • Commentary framing: The Tyee argued the budget’s higher spending and deficits look like what critics often label an NDP-style approach, despite coming from a conservative government [6].
