Researchers document sustained sexual bond between Ethiopian bull and male donkey

Category: Science

An Italian-led research team documented repeated sexual interactions between a bull and a male donkey on an Ethiopian plateau, describing a selective social bond between the animals. During eight days of observation in March 2026, the bull, Achilles, mounted the donkey, Patroclus, 51 times; 34 mountings involved penetration. The findings appeared Nov. 2 in the journal Current Biology [1][2][3][13]. The pair also licked, rubbed and nuzzled one another, and researchers repeatedly saw them seek each other out despite access to other potential mates. The team cautions that observations of one domesticated pair cannot establish why the behavior occurred or what the animals felt. Dry-season conditions led herders to move them to separate pastures; researchers in Ethiopia are trying to arrange a reunion to observe whether their interactions resume [2][4][8].

Primary Source: View original article

Animal behavior is not a picture painted in black and white: It uses the whole palette, with all the colors of the rainbow.

— Elisabetta Palagi, ethologist at the University of Pisa (ScienceAlert)

Key Points

  • Different responses: When Achilles mounted another male donkey, that animal avoided him and displayed threatening behavior, while Patroclus did not; Patroclus also approached during one such encounter [2][5][13].
  • Shared pasture: The animals belonged to different owners, spent nights with their own herds and met while grazing [6][13].
  • Wider context: Same-sex sexual behavior has been reported in more than 1,500 animal species, but researchers know far less about recurring, selective sexual relationships across species [7][3].

Perspectives

Vincent Savolainen, an ecologist at Imperial College London, said the animals' domestication limits what the case can show about wild behavior. He argued that researchers would need to study a larger group of wild animals to draw broader conclusions.

Sources: Scientific American

Primatologist Jo Setchell argues that sexual behavior need not contribute directly to reproduction to have evolutionary significance, because social relationships can affect survival, access to resources and later reproductive opportunities. She says the study cannot identify the evolutionary function of the pair's behavior.

Sources: The Conversation

Kees Moeliker, former director of the Natural History Museum Rotterdam, argued that animal behavior does not always conform to textbook categories, drawing on his experience documenting unusual pairings.

Sources: de Volkskrant

Technical Details

  • Behavior coding: Researchers classified licking, rubbing and nuzzling as affiliative contacts—observable behaviors associated with social closeness—rather than assuming they were preparation for sex [2].
  • Facial-action analysis: Researchers used EquiFACS to analyze Patroclus' facial movements and found no detectable differences between mounting, other interactions with Achilles and periods alone. The authors said this was consistent with Patroclus not perceiving the encounters as threatening, but did not establish his emotional state [13].

Scientific Significance

  • Unresolved sequence: Researchers do not know whether social bonding preceded sexual contact or developed alongside it; proposed benefits such as social support, rewarding contact or reduced tension were not tested [6][13].
