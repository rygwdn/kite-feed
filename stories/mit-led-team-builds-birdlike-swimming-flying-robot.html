MIT-led team builds birdlike swimming-flying robot

Category: Science

Engineers at MIT and EPFL in Lausanne, Switzerland, built a lightweight flapping-wing robot that can swim underwater and then leave the water by flapping into flight, a maneuver modeled on diving birds such as puffins, gulls, loons and petrels [4][3][7]. The robot, called FAAV, for “flapping-wing aerial-aquatic vehicle,” weighs about 250 grams [2][8]. The prototype uses a slender carbon-fiber body, a small motorized tail and flexible membrane wings that beat like a bird’s. It can fly at about 6.3 meters per second and swim underwater at nearly 1 meter per second [2]. Researchers tested the machine in Lake Geneva and showed it had enough speed and power to lift itself out of the water with its wings alone [3]. The team designed the robot to study how animals move between air and water and to explore possible uses in coastal monitoring, ocean sampling and access to aquatic areas that boats or conventional systems cannot easily reach [4][8][6]. Raphael Zufferey, an MIT mechanical engineering assistant professor, said the goal is for users to launch the robot from a boat or shore, have it dive to take measurements or collect samples, and fly back with data [2].

Key Points

  • Density challenge: Water is about 1,000 times denser than air, which makes the transition between swimming and flight a major mechanical challenge for both birds and engineered vehicles [2][6].
  • Bird benchmark: About 100 bird species can both fly and swim, giving engineers biological examples of wings that work across two very different fluids [4][2][8].
  • Research need: Infobae reported that studies of live animals are difficult and computer simulations do not fully reproduce the interaction among flapping, forces and the water-to-air transition [8].
  • Lab setup: Zufferey’s MIT lab includes a bright turquoise water tank and fans that can generate strong wind for tests of small aerial-aquatic robots [3].

Technical Details

  • Untethered flapping design: FAAV is untethered and uses flexible membrane wings, not propellers, folded wings or paddling feet, for its water-to-air exit [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Evidence base: The evidence cited in the articles includes field testing in Lake Geneva that showed a wing-powered water exit, but the articles describe a prototype rather than a deployed ocean-monitoring system [3][4].
  • Future work: Researchers named measurements, sampling and coastal monitoring as potential applications, but the sources do not report routine payload capacity, endurance or operational range for real-world missions [2][4].
