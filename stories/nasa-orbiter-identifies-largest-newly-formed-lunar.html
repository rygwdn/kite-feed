NASA orbiter identifies largest newly formed lunar crater

Category: Science

NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has identified McGetchin, a 222-metre-wide, 43-metre-deep impact crater on the Moon’s eastern edge. Scientists determined that a comet or asteroid roughly the size of a three- to six-story building struck the surface sometime between April 11 and May 22, 2024, creating the largest newly formed impact crater yet identified in the Solar System [3][4][5]. An impact of this size is estimated to occur once a century or less often [9][10]. Robert Wagner, an image-processing specialist working with Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera data, noticed an unusually bright patch surrounded by a dark halo during a routine lunar-map check on October 24, 2025. High-resolution follow-up images confirmed the feature and allowed researchers to study the surrounding terrain. The crater was named for lunar scientist Tom McGetchin [4][8][1].

Did you know: McGetchin is about three times wider than the previous record-holding newly formed crater identified by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter roughly a decade earlier [18].

Key Points

  • Thermal disturbance: NASA’s Diviner instrument detected a nighttime cold zone about 6 kilometres wide around the crater, where temperatures are roughly 9 degrees Celsius lower than nearby ground [4][9].
  • Surface reach: The collision loosened lunar soil beyond the crater itself, with reported disturbance extending more than 100 kilometres across the surrounding terrain [11].
  • Mission relevance: The spread of impact debris is relevant to hazard assessments for future crewed lunar activity [10][18].

Timeline

  • 11 April–22 May 2024: A comet or asteroid struck the Moon’s eastern edge sometime during this period, forming McGetchin [4][9]
  • 24 October 2025: Robert Wagner spotted an anomalous bright patch during routine data checks [4][13]
  • 5 December 2025: LRO’s Narrow-Angle Camera captured detailed follow-up images of the crater [4]
  • 18 September 2026: NASA’s discovery and crater analysis appeared in public reports [5][3]

Technical Details

  • Two-scale imaging: LRO’s Wide-Angle Camera global maps use pixels roughly the size of American football fields, while its Narrow-Angle Camera resolves details at about 3 feet per pixel for precise crater measurements [5].
  • Regolith thermal response: The impact aerated and reduced the density of the lunar regolith, lowering its ability to retain heat through the lunar night [4][17].
  • Image footprint: The detailed Narrow-Angle Camera image covered an area about three-quarters of a mile wide [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Fresh impact record: Identified soon after its formation, the crater provides a rare modern case for testing impact-crater formation models against observed changes in lunar terrain [4][10].
  • Uncertainty: Available reports identify the impact window and likely projectile class, but they do not provide a confirmed asteroid-or-comet identity or an exact impact time [3][4].
