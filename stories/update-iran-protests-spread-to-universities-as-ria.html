Update: Iran protests spread to universities as rial slides

Category: World

In the latest development, cost-of-living protests in Iran have widened from Tehran’s Grand Bazaar and shopfront strikes to multiple universities, with student demonstrations reported in Tehran and other cities [1][5][3][7]. The unrest is linked to a sharp decline in the rial and high inflation, with reports noting the currency has lost nearly half its value against the dollar this year and inflation running at about 42% this month [13]. President Masoud Pezeshkian has publicly signaled openness to hearing what he called protesters’ “legitimate demands,” while officials say they are setting up a mechanism for dialogue even as parts of the security and judicial establishment warn against unrest that “creates insecurity” [13][7][15][17].

Primary Source: View original article

We officially recognise the protests. We hear their voices and we know that this originates from natural pressure arising from the pressure on people’s livelihoods,

— Fatemeh Mohajerani (Dawn)

Did you know: Iran’s Grand Bazaar has long functioned as both a commercial hub and a political barometer, with merchant strikes historically carrying outsized symbolic weight [common].

Key Points

  • Cities involved: Verified and reported demonstrations have appeared beyond Tehran, including in Karaj, Hamedan, Qeshm, Malard, Isfahan, Kermanshah, Shiraz and Yazd, as the unrest entered a third day [9].
  • Police response: Some footage and reporting described police using tear gas to disperse demonstrators in certain locations [9].
  • Central bank change: Iran replaced its central bank head as authorities sought to contain the economic fallout and public anger driving the protests [16].
  • Foreign pressure: Several outlets linked the tense domestic climate to heightened regional and international pressure, including Tehran’s warnings and exchanges with Washington and Israel during the protest wave [8][6].
  • Protest slogans: Reports from Tehran described chants including “Rest in peace Reza Shah,” referencing Iran’s pre-1979 monarchy, signaling that some crowds are also voicing broader political dissatisfaction alongside economic demands [13].

Perspectives

President Masoud Pezeshkian: He said he asked the interior minister to listen to protesters’ “legitimate demands,” and his government has indicated it wants to open a channel for talks.

Sources: Dawn

Government spokesperson Fatemeh Mohajerani: She said the state “officially recognise[s] the protests” and plans a dialogue mechanism including talks with protest leaders.

Sources: Dawn

Prosecutor-general Mohammad Movahedi-Azad: He warned authorities would respond “decisive[ly]” if protests are used to foment “insecurity.”.

Sources: France 24

Protest participants and supporters (as described by local reporting): Demonstrators and online supporters have framed the unrest around unaffordable prices and corruption, with some warning protests could spread further.

Sources: Dawn

Timeline

  • Sunday: Shopkeepers in Tehran’s Grand Bazaar began strikes as rial hit record low [9].
  • Monday: President Pezeshkian posted he would hear “legitimate demands” via interior minister [13].
  • Tuesday: Student protests reported at universities in Tehran and other cities [1][5].
  • Wednesday: Prosecutor-general warned of a “decisive response” if unrest creates insecurity [17].

Technical Details

  • Currency devaluation: A falling currency means imports cost more in local money, which can quickly raise consumer prices in an economy that relies on imported goods and inputs [common].
  • Inflation rate: Inflation measures how fast prices rise; Iranian reporting cited inflation around 42% this month, a level that can erode wages and savings rapidly [13].
  • Rial exchange rate: Several reports tied the protest trigger to the rial hitting a record low against the US dollar on the open market, a common stress signal for household purchasing power [9][15].

Industry Impact

  • Retail and small business: Strikes and shop closures—especially around Tehran’s Grand Bazaar—can disrupt everyday supply chains and signal stress among merchants who often serve as a bellwether for broader economic confidence [1][13].
  • Higher education: As protests reach universities, campus disruptions can affect exams, research activity and student mobility, and may widen the social base of the movement beyond traders and shopkeepers [5][7].
  • Financial governance: Changing the central bank leadership can reshape near-term monetary management and messaging—important for banks, importers, and any firm trying to plan costs in a volatile-currency environment [16][common].

Historical Background

Iran’s 1979 revolution ended the Pahlavi monarchy and created the Islamic Republic; references to Reza Shah in today’s chants underscore how economic protests can also tap into older political currents [common][13]. In recent years, Iran has experienced repeated nationwide protests over issues including economic conditions, drought, women’s rights and political freedoms, with authorities often responding with arrests and security measures [13][4].

Q&A

Q: What form will the government’s proposed “dialogue mechanism” take, and who will represent protesters?

A: Officials have said a mechanism will be set up and include talks with protest leaders, but reports did not specify the format or representatives [13].

Q: What evidence has been presented for claims of foreign intelligence involvement in the unrest?

A: DW reported that there are complaints alleging interference by a foreign intelligence agency, but the reporting did not provide publicly detailed evidence in the summary provided [4].

Q: How are regional security fears affecting Iran’s currency and public sentiment?

A: NPR reported that the currency’s plunge is occurring amid concerns Israel could restart war with Iran—an action President Trump signaled he would approve—adding anxiety to the economic crisis [6].
