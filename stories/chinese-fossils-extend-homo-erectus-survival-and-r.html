Chinese fossils extend Homo erectus survival and reveal rainforest adaptation

Category: Science

Two studies published in Nature on Nov. 18 report that Homo erectus lived in southern China far later than previously documented and inhabited tropical rainforest there. Nine fossil teeth from Naxian and Baxian caves in Guangxi date to 324,000–184,000 years ago. Researchers identified eight as Homo erectus, extending its known mainland Asian record by roughly 200,000 years [3][2][13]. A ninth tooth, PA1796, does not match Homo erectus, Neanderthals or modern humans. Researchers cannot yet tell whether it belonged to a Denisovan or another hominin. Its presence suggests at least two human groups inhabited the region during the period represented by the finds [3][4][1]. Chemical evidence from the teeth and animal fossils indicates that the Homo erectus individuals lived in a rainforest ecosystem, challenging the idea that only Homo sapiens could use such habitats. Strontium signatures also suggest some individuals moved across a wide area as children. Researchers continue searching southern China’s caves for fossils that could help identify the mystery tooth and clarify the species’ history [12][2].

Key Points

  • Dating evidence: Researchers combined 43 radiometric age estimates from five techniques, including luminescence dating of sediments and uranium-series dating of teeth and mineral deposits [3].
  • Diet clues: Isotope readings do not identify the specific foods Homo erectus ate, only the vegetation represented in the surrounding animals’ diets [3][13].
  • Childhood movement: Strontium isotope patterns in enamel suggest some individuals moved between local limestone terrain and more distant regions, possibly as far as northern Vietnam, about 250 kilometers away [2][12][8].
  • Protein analysis: Ancient proteins survived in all five sampled teeth. Four teeth showed an ameloblastin variant associated with Chinese Homo erectus fossils but lacked a second variant found in older specimens [3][2].
  • Cave deposits: Researchers recovered more than 18,000 mammal fossils from the caves and say porcupines likely accumulated many of the remains while collecting bones [4][10].

Perspectives

Paleoanthropologist Chris Stringer of the Natural History Museum in London said the late Chinese finds bring Homo erectus closer in age to late Javan examples and raise the possibility that populations connected during periods of lower sea levels [4].

Sources: Live Science

Anthropologist John Hawks of the University of Wisconsin-Madison said the combined evidence from tooth shape and proteins makes a convincing case for late-surviving Homo erectus, but argued that researchers should compare the mystery tooth with more Denisovan teeth [4].

Sources: Live Science

Scientific Significance

  • Ecological limits: The isotope evidence indicates Homo erectus was part of a rainforest ecosystem, but researchers caution that the habitat may not have been its ideal ecological niche [3].
  • Population history: Protein sequence analysis placed the sampled Homo erectus lineage on an early branch outside the group containing Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans [3].
  • Extinction questions: Because the finds show Homo erectus could persist in rainforest conditions in southern China, researchers say the role of rainforest expansion in the species’ eventual disappearance, including in Java, needs reassessment [13][12].

Historical Background

Homo erectus emerged in Africa about two million years ago and spread into Eurasia, becoming one of the longest-lived known human species [13][3]. The youngest known fossils elsewhere came from Java, where Homo erectus remains date to roughly 110,000 years ago [13][3].

Q&A

Q: Did Homo erectus encounter Homo sapiens?

A: The dates of the Chinese cave fossils and Java finds suggest the populations probably did not meet; available fossil evidence indicates Homo sapiens arrived in those areas after Homo erectus had disappeared [13].

Q: What evidence could help explain Homo erectus’ eventual disappearance?

A: Researchers say similar chemical-signature analyses of human and animal teeth at other sites could help test what led to the species’ disappearance, including in Java [12].
