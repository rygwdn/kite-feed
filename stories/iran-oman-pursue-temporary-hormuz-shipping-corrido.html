Iran, Oman pursue temporary Hormuz shipping corridor

Category: World

Iran and Oman said their foreign ministers discussed a framework in Tehran for a temporary navigational corridor through the Strait of Hormuz and a joint mine-clearing project, while a senior Iranian source told Reuters that no final accord had been reached [10][20][19][6][14][21]. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards separately said the two countries had reached understandings on their shares of the waterway and its revenues, but Tehran said the strait would not reopen unless Washington met Iranian conditions tied to a June memorandum of understanding [34][43][40][5]. The waterway handled about one-fifth of global oil and liquefied natural gas shipments before the war began in February; most shipping has since been shut down as Iran and the United States imposed separate blockades [6][15][50][40]. President Trump said all mines in international waters had been removed or detonated, while Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi rejected U.S. mine-clearing claims and said reopening would depend on Tehran’s demands being fulfilled [31][42][53][41].

Key Points

  • Military ban: Gharibabadi said Iran would not allow military vessels to transit the strait under the potential arrangement with Oman [7][31][22][46].
  • Permanent talks: Iranian officials said any temporary routing would require further talks on a permanent maritime traffic plan within 30 to 60 days [7][51][31].
  • Shipping risk: The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations said an oil tanker was disabled after an unidentified projectile struck it about 17 km northeast of Oman’s Ash Shishah [25][37][50].
  • Vessel blacklist: Iran announced a blacklist of 45 ships in an apparent attempt to curb ship-to-ship transfers used by Gulf energy producers to evade the Iranian blockade [15].
  • Price response: Global oil prices dipped after Iran and Oman announced progress toward a temporary lane and mine-clearing work [3].

Technical Details

  • Temporary routing: Gharibabadi said inbound commercial traffic to the Persian Gulf would use Iranian waters, while outbound traffic would pass partly through Iranian waters and partly through Omani waters [7][31][19].
  • Corridor width: Iranian accounts cited by Pakistan Observer described a proposed temporary route about seven miles, or 11 km, wide, with its entrance and part of its exit passing through Iranian territorial waters [45].
  • Operational gaps: The joint statement did not specify the route’s start date, exact coordinates, merchant-ship security procedures, or demining command structure [20].

Historical Background

A Pakistan-mediated U.S.-Iran memorandum of understanding in mid-June called for reopening the Strait of Hormuz and tasked Tehran and Muscat with defining future administration and maritime services, in consultation with Gulf states and in line with international law [10]. Article 5 said Iran should ensure safe commercial passage and consult Oman on the strait’s future management, including maritime services [32][33]. Iranian accounts say the memorandum later collapsed after disputes over U.S. implementation and an alternative route through Omani waters [55].

Business Angle

  • Do not treat a temporary corridor as a full reopening while Iran says Hormuz will stay closed unless its demands are met [5][21].
  • Companies tied to Gulf energy exports still face risks from ongoing attacks on shipping in the area [50].
  • Oil-price relief may be temporary if the operational details of the corridor remain unresolved [3][20].

International Reactions

  • 🇺🇸 United States: The Trump administration opposed parts of the evolving deal, including joint Iranian-Omani management of the outbound route and voluntary fees from ships, while Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent threatened further pressure on entities doing business with Iran [7][38][44][43].
  • 🇨🇳 China: China criticized President Trump’s “Economic D-Day” pressure campaign as Washington launched Operation Economic Outcast to target Iranian revenue sources including smuggled oil [4].
