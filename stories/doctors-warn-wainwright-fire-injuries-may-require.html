Doctors warn Wainwright fire injuries may require years of recovery

Category: Canada

Nearly six weeks after a wind-driven grass fire burned soldiers during a training exercise at Canadian Forces Base Wainwright in Alberta, doctors say some face months or years of physical and psychological recovery [2][3][4]. Five soldiers have died; as of Thursday, 19 injured soldiers remained in Alberta hospitals, seven in critical condition, while the military said 22 were still hospitalized across Canada [2][3]. Burn specialist Dr. Josh Wong said treatment for severe burns can involve repeated surgeries, rehabilitation and ongoing psychological support. The fire overwhelmed the Edmonton burn unit, and three investigations are examining how it started and why live-fire training continued as strong winds pushed flames across the base [4][7]. Health officials say they will review the emergency response after addressing immediate treatment needs [2][3].

Primary Source: View original article

Their care doesn’t end when they leave the ICU or even the hospital.

— Dr. Josh Wong, interim medical director of the Firefighters’ Burn Treatment Unit at the University of Alberta Hospital (CBC News)

Key Points

  • Capacity surge: The Edmonton burn unit admitted 20 critically injured soldiers in a short period—five times its specialized critical-care capacity—while already treating 15 other burn patients [2][3].
  • Provincial response: Alberta’s emergency response used 30 transport units—22 ground ambulances, six fixed-wing aircraft and two helicopters—to move patients, with transfers continuing until about 2 or 3 a.m. [7].
  • Staff support: Doctors say health-care workers and first responders may experience fatigue and psychological effects in the weeks and months after the emergency [2][4].
  • Treatment network: Soldiers received treatment at Royal Alexandra, Foothills and Red Deer Regional hospitals, while specialized medical teams from Ontario and Manitoba came to help [7][2].

Perspectives

Dr. Sayeh Zielke, interim chief executive officer of Acute Care Alberta, said Alberta’s health system passed a stress test by coordinating the mass-casualty response while continuing to care for other patients [3][2].

Sources: The Canadian Press via The Toronto Star

Dr. Josh Wong, a burn surgeon, warned that the psychological impact of burns can become more pronounced as patients recover and become more aware of their injuries [2][3].
