Psilocybin prevents chemotherapy nerve damage in mice

Category: Science

Researchers reported in Science that psilocybin, the psychedelic compound found in some mushrooms, prevented chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in mice when administered before cancer treatment [5][7][4]. The mouse experiments found that two doses protected animals from nerve damage through as many as six chemotherapy cycles, including symptoms linked to pain, numbness and heightened sensitivity to cold and touch [2][4]. The study identified preserved mitochondrial trafficking as the proposed mechanism, suggesting the drug protected peripheral nerves through a cellular process rather than through its psychoactive effects [5][4]. Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy affects roughly one-third to one-half of people receiving certain chemotherapy drugs, can be disabling and often irreversible, can limit cancer treatment, and has no proven treatment [4][5]. The New York Times reported that clinical trials are scheduled to begin in September 2026 [3].

Key Points

  • A companion Science commentary described psychedelics as possible prophylactic neuroprotectants, framing the work as prevention of nerve damage during chemotherapy [6][11].

Timeline

  • 3 September 2026: Science published the mouse study and companion commentary [5][6].
  • 3 September 2026: Health publications reported the findings as early animal research [3][4].
  • September 2026: Human clinical trials are scheduled to begin this month [3].

Scientific Significance

  • The work tested psilocybin before chemotherapy exposure rather than as a treatment for established chemotherapy neuropathy [6][10].
  • Researchers reported that the protective effect appeared independent of psilocybin's psychoactive properties. A key question is whether a non-hallucinogenic analog could provide the same benefit without the psychedelic experience, which may be challenging for some cancer patients [4][1].
  • The mouse study did not test whether psilocybin affects the anti-cancer efficacy of chemotherapy, a gap that human trials must address [3][1].
