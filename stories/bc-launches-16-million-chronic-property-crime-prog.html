B.C. launches $16 million chronic property crime program

Category: Canada

British Columbia will spend $16 million over two years to launch the Chronic Property Offending Intervention Initiative provincewide, focusing on roughly 420 chronic offenders linked to a disproportionate share of street disorder, property crime and retail crime [5][2][3]. Public Safety Minister Nina Krieger announced the program Monday in Nanaimo, where the province said similar problems have affected the community [2][5]. The program will create 12 regional hubs that bring together police, prosecutors, probation officers and other staff to identify people who need additional supervision and support based on criminal history and risk [2][5]. The government said interventions can include enhanced supervision and monitoring, closer collaboration with Crown counsel, and connections to housing, mental-health and addiction supports [5][3]. The initiative expands pilots that began in Kelowna, Nanaimo and Nelson in November 2025 and builds on B.C.’s Repeat Violent Offending Intervention Initiative, which the province says reduced offending and increased charges among high-risk violent offenders [5][3]. Save Our Streets, a coalition of more than 120 community and business organizations, welcomed the program but said B.C. also needs to address mental-health and addiction factors behind repeat crime [2].

Key Points

  • Government framing: The province says the approach combines “compassion and accountability” [5].
  • Hub locations: Vancouver will receive two hubs, while Victoria and Courtenay are among the Vancouver Island communities included in the expansion [3].
  • Target group: Police have said a small number of people cause a disproportionate amount of property crime, leading the initiative to focus on chronic offenders [2].
  • Business rationale: Krieger said the province is targeting chronic property offending because it threatens small businesses, which she called “the backbone of our communities” [5][3].

Perspectives

Business Angle

  • The program targets costs that directly affect small businesses, including retail theft, vandalism and street disorder, but its long-term value depends on whether repeat offending falls enough to reduce insurance, security and loss-prevention burdens [5][3].
  • The province is funding enforcement and supervision for two years, so investors and business owners should watch whether pilot results persist after the initial allocation ends [5].
  • Advocacy and police sources linked much of the problem to addiction and mental-health challenges, so enforcement alone may produce limited durable savings if treatment, housing and support capacity do not expand alongside monitoring [2][3].
