B.C. premier merges mining into resource ministry, removes climate portfolio

Category: Canada

British Columbia Premier Lorne Doerkson unveiled a smaller cabinet on Nov. 20, combining mining with energy and petroleum resources and removing climate change from the names of key ministries [1][3]. The cabinet has 18 ministers, plus Doerkson and six ministers of state; the former New Democratic government had 23 ministers. Doerkson’s Conservative government also says it will repeal the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act (DRIPA), which provides for joint project decisions with First Nations [1][2]. The new cabinet includes rookie MLAs in major portfolios. Finance Minister Peter Milobar says the government will commission an independent review of provincial finances; the latest forecast cited by The Canadian Press put the deficit at nearly $14 billion [3][4]. Doerkson said the legislature will not sit this fall, though his government is considering orders-in-council. The Narwhal reported that MLAs are unlikely to return before February. The first cabinet meeting is scheduled for next week [1][3].

Key Points

  • Health portfolio: Health Minister Ryan Peterson will oversee a ministry with about $40 billion in expenses; Doerkson said he selected him for his business background [3][4].
  • Resource projects: B.C. has 41 major projects, including 16 in critical minerals and mining; the Conservatives have pledged fixed permitting timelines and a “one project, one review” approach [1].
  • LNG target: The party’s platform promises to double liquefied natural gas production by 2032 and triple it by 2035; LNG Canada in Kitimat is the province’s only operating export facility [1].
  • Campaign spending: The Conservatives’ plan proposed adding $1.4 billion in annual operating spending while reducing the deficit within three years [3][4].

Historical Background

B.C.’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act allows joint decision-making agreements with First Nations over industrial projects on their traditional territories [1]. Agreements under that provision have helped advance the Eskay Creek revitalization, Red Chris mine, Galore Creek Project and a forestry plan on Vancouver Island [1]. The law was among the few pieces of legislation to pass unanimously in the B.C. legislature, according to The Narwhal [1].
