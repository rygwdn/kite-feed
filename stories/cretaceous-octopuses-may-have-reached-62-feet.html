Cretaceous octopuses may have reached 62 feet

Category: Science

Three science outlets report that researchers reexamined 27 fossil jaw pieces from extinct finned octopuses and argue that some came from enormous animals that lived in the Late Cretaceous, including specimens more than 72 million years old [1][2][3]. By comparing jaw size with that of living cephalopods, the team proposes that the largest individuals could have reached about 62 feet (19 meters) long — in the size range of whales and potentially the largest invertebrates yet identified [1][2][3]. If that interpretation holds, it would challenge the long-standing view that vertebrates such as large marine reptiles and fishes dominated the top of Cretaceous seas [1][2]. Instead, giant deep-water octopuses may have been major predators in their own right, offering a vivid new look at just how diverse ancient ocean ecosystems could be [1][3].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Because octopus bodies are mostly soft tissue, their beaks are among the few parts likely to survive in the fossil record, making tiny hard structures crucial for reconstructing giant ancient animals [1][3].

Key Points

  • Jaw evidence: The study centers on preserved beaks or jaw elements from 27 specimens, which the researchers say were overlooked clues to the body size of extinct cirrate, or finned, octopuses [1][2].
  • Deep-sea hunters: The articles describe these animals as likely living in deeper Cretaceous waters, where they may have stalked prey [2][3].
  • Vertebrate challenge: By proposing an invertebrate apex predator in the Cretaceous ocean, the work pushes back on the assumption that vertebrates alone dominated the top of marine food webs [1][2].
  • Whale scale: Science News reports that the largest reconstructed animals were as long as whales, underscoring how unusual it would be for a soft-bodied cephalopod to reach that scale [3].

Technical Details

  • Allometric scaling: The size estimates rely on comparing fossil jaw dimensions with body proportions in living cephalopods, an allometric approach that infers whole-body length from preserved hard parts when soft tissue is absent [1][2].
  • Cirrate octopuses: The fossils are attributed to finned octopuses, or cirrates, a group set apart from the more familiar incirrate octopuses by paired fins; that identification matters because it shapes how researchers interpret their ecology and role in ancient food webs [1][2].
  • Taphonomic bias: Soft-bodied animals rarely fossilize well, so durable jaw elements can disproportionately shape the record; that preservation bias helps explain why evidence for very large octopuses may have remained obscure until these fossils were reassessed [1][3].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: If the jaw-based reconstruction is correct, the work would significantly expand the known upper size range of octopuses and suggest that ancient marine ecosystems included giant invertebrate predators alongside better-known vertebrate hunters [1][2][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The claim rests on indirect body-size estimates from fossil jaws rather than complete skeletons or preserved soft tissue, so the proposed maximum length depends on how closely these extinct forms matched the proportions of living cephalopods [1][2].
  • Limitations and future work: Researchers will need more complete fossils, additional jaw specimens, and stronger comparisons with living species to test whether these animals truly reached whale-like lengths and acted as apex predators rather than simply being large deep-sea animals [1][3].

Historical Background

Modern giant cephalopods already loom large in science and myth, especially giant and colossal squids, but octopuses have generally not been seen as the biggest animals in that group [common]. The Cretaceous Period ended about 66 million years ago with the extinction event that also wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs [common]. These new reports matter because they suggest giant soft-bodied predators may have been part of Late Cretaceous marine ecosystems, expanding the known range of ancient invertebrate body sizes [1][3].
