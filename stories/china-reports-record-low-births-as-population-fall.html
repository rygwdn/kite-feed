China reports record-low births as population falls

Category: World

China’s National Bureau of Statistics reported that the country’s population declined for a fourth consecutive year in 2025, reflecting a sustained demographic shift with global economic implications [1][4][3][6][7][8]. Official figures showed births fell to 7.92 million—down from 2024—while deaths increased, deepening concerns about an ageing society and a smaller future workforce [6][7]. The data arrive despite expanded pro-family incentives and public campaigns aimed at encouraging marriage and childbearing, which several outlets say have not reversed the trend [2][4][8][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: China’s 2025 birth count was reported as the lowest since national records began in 1949 [3][5][7].

Key Points

  • Marriage indicator: China registered a sharp fall in marriages in 2024—reported as more than 6.1 million couples—often watched as a leading indicator for future births [9].
  • Policy tweaks: China began allowing couples to marry anywhere in the country (not only their place of residence), a procedural change demographers cited as a possible reason for a temporary bump in births [9].
  • UN projection: At the current pace, the United Nations projects China’s population could fall to about 800 million by 2100, highlighting how long-run demographic momentum can persist even after policy changes [4].
  • Retirement response: Beijing has moved to raise the retirement age as part of its effort to manage the fiscal and labor pressures associated with rapid population ageing [10].
  • Peak comparison: The 2025 birth total was reported to be roughly 10 million below the 2016 peak and has dropped by more than half in less than a decade, underscoring the speed of the shift [7].

Perspectives

China’s National Bureau of Statistics: Official data show a fourth straight year of population decline alongside record-low births and higher deaths in 2025.

Chinese policymakers (pro-natal efforts): Authorities have expanded incentives and messaging to encourage marriage and child-rearing, but recent figures suggest these measures have not yet shifted birth trends.

Demographers and analysts: Commentators cited in coverage warn that ageing and a shrinking workforce complicate goals like boosting consumption and maintaining pension sustainability, though some see limited potential for a short-term rise due to administrative changes around marriage registration.

Technical Details

  • Birth rate (per 1,000 people): China’s official statistics include a birth rate measure (births per 1,000 people); for 2025, the figure was reported as 5.63, which helps compare fertility trends across years and countries [6].
  • Dependency ratio (ageing pressure): As births fall and the population ages, a higher share of retirees relative to workers can strain pensions and healthcare systems; coverage links this dynamic to policy steps like raising retirement ages [10][common].
  • Household incentives: Several reports describe expanded subsidies and financial incentives aimed at reducing child-rearing costs, a common pro-natal policy tool that seeks to make having children more affordable [2][5][4].

Industry Impact

  • Education and childcare: A sustained drop in births can gradually reduce demand for newborn and early-years services while pushing local governments and providers to consolidate or redesign childcare and schooling capacity [4][common].
  • Pensions and elder care: Reports highlight pressure on pension budgets as more people leave the workforce, implying expanding demand for retirement services, assisted living, and healthcare capacity [9][10].
  • Consumer strategy: Coverage notes Beijing’s goal of boosting domestic consumption faces added complexity with an ageing population, nudging companies to adapt products toward older consumers and smaller households [9].

Historical Background

China ended its one-child policy in 2016 after decades of birth restrictions, aiming to slow population ageing and support longer-term economic and social stability [1][10]. In the years since, authorities have continued adjusting family policy and related social measures, but multiple outlets note the birth downturn has persisted, suggesting deeper factors—such as costs and changing family preferences—are hard to reverse quickly [8][4].
