Governments and AI leaders press for human oversight as safety rules diverge

Category: AI

Governments and technology leaders are pursuing AI safety measures ranging from voluntary international commitments to proposed inspections and mandatory incident reporting. The Paris Call, signed Nov. 9 by governments, AI companies and civil-society groups, is nonbinding, while the U.S. administration has favored industry-led safeguards and Congress has yet to pass proposed federal requirements [17][1]. California’s transparency law took effect in January 2026; New York’s RAISE Act is due to take effect in January 2027, and Japan’s government aims to submit legislation requiring reports of serious AI failures to its 2027 ordinary parliamentary session [1][14]. At a Nov. 23 United Nations summit in Barbados, Finland’s Transport and Communications Minister Lulu Ranne said human interests and governance should be central to AI development [2]. Experts at a Brookings Institution discussion urged the United States and China to cooperate on incident reporting, testing standards and researcher exchanges, even if their domestic regulations differ [3]. In Russia, President Vladimir Putin called for clear limits on what AI agents can do without human approval [10]. AI safety expert Stuart Russell called for international standards and mandatory pre-release testing, while Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang argued that regulators should use existing laws unless they identify a specific gap [20][5]. Japan’s proposed bill would allow inspections when safeguards are inadequate and penalties of up to 100 million yen, according to Nikkei [14].

Did you know: The Paris Call’s initial signatory count was reported as six governments and 10 companies, but the Élysée list later cited in the article showed 19 governments and 11 companies [17].

Key Points

  • Paris commitments: The Paris Call includes commitments to independent validation, model fail-safes, ongoing risk assessments and faster sharing of information about serious incidents, but sets no deadlines [17].
  • Signatory gaps: China, Google and Meta were not listed at the time described [17].
  • Thailand’s legal gap: Technology law specialist Paiboon Amornpinyo said Thailand’s cybercrime, data-protection and cybersecurity laws were not designed for AI-driven attacks and called for an emergency decree while longer-term legislation is prepared [15].
  • Regulatory comparison: Russell said China had implemented tougher AI regulations than the United States and Europe [20].
  • Federal proposals: Congressional proposals include independent audits, incident reporting and pre-deployment evaluations, but none had become law [1].

Perspectives

Stuart Russell, University of California, Berkeley professor and AI expert: He warned that AI loss-of-control incidents already seen in cyberspace could eventually spill over into financial markets [20].

  • 21 November 2026: Nobel Committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes addressed AI accountability in Manila [7].
  • 23 November 2026: UN special summit on AI safety convened in Bridgetown, Barbados [2].
  • 26 November 2026: Japan’s proposed AI safety bill details were reported, targeting parliamentary submission in 2027 [14].

Industry Impact

  • Frontier AI developers: California’s law requires large developers to publish safety frameworks and report critical incidents; Illinois has introduced annual independent compliance audits beginning in 2028 [1].
  • AI services in Thailand: A technology law specialist warned that foreign-operated AI platforms and distributed computing infrastructure could complicate domestic data processing, oversight and sovereignty as AI services expand [15].
  • AI laboratories: The Paris Call recognizes the potential benefits of open-weight models for research, innovation and validation, while leaving unresolved how risks should be handled once weights are openly available [17].

Historical Background

The Paris Call drew on earlier international work, including the Hiroshima process, the Frontier Safety Commitments established at the Seoul AI Summit, the G7 Evian AI meeting and a United Nations Security Council AI session on Sept. 23, 2026 [17]. The Call also followed an appeal initiated by Finland and Norway for control of frontier AI models, which France later joined [17].

Business Angle

  • Federal proposals include independent audits, incident reporting and pre-deployment evaluations, though they have not become law [1].
  • Companies may need to limit AI systems’ permissions and establish ways to suspend or disable autonomous functions when necessary [1].
  • Connecticut has taken a narrower approach focused on internal risk-reporting mechanisms and whistleblower protections [1].

International Reactions

  • 🇫🇮 Finland: At the Nov. 23 summit, Ranne called for AI’s benefits to be shared more evenly [2].
  • 🇷🇺 Russia: Putin proposed updating the national AI strategy through 2036 and approving an action plan, arguing that people and autonomous systems should work together harmoniously [16].
  • 🇺🇸 United States: The U.S. government had not signed the Paris Call, although Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent attended the meeting, according to the signatory account [17].

Q&A

Q: What are the Paris Call’s main limitations?

A: A French civil-society signatory said the Call lacks common criteria for deciding whether models should be developed or deployed and does not guarantee sufficient access for validation bodies and governments. A common validation system has not yet been established [17].

Q: Would Japan’s proposal apply only to domestic AI companies?

A: No. The proposal also targets overseas developers of high-performance AI, according to Nikkei [14].

Action Items

  • Check whether frontier-AI rules apply to your organization’s AI use: Review the system developers, deployment locations and applicable state requirements, including California’s current law and New York’s upcoming RAISE Act [1].
  • Review AI vendor contracts for safety obligations: Check whether contracts cover incident notification, safety testing, human oversight and suspension or shutdown rights [1].
