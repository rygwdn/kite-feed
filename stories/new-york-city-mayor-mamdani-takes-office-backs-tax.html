New York City mayor Mamdani takes office, backs taxes

Category: Business

Zohran Mamdani was sworn in on Jan. 1 and took office as New York City’s 112th mayor, with outlets describing his rise over the past year as historic and unlikely [1][6][7]. He has signaled he will press ahead with higher taxes on New York City millionaires, arguing the policy can help fund an affordability agenda and dismissing concerns that wealthy residents will leave the city [2]. The new administration’s early messaging frames the moment as a “new era” and leans into civic optimism around building a city that works better for everyday New Yorkers [6].

A physical monument to a city that dared to be both beautiful and build great things that would transform working peoples' lives.

— Zohran Mamdani (Business Insider)

Did you know: The Old City Hall subway station is only accessible by guided tour today, and Business Insider reported guides use a ramp to bridge the gap between train doors and the platform [4].

Key Points

  • Mayor’s compensation: Business Insider reported the mayoral job comes with a $258,750 salary and use of a rent-free official residence, underscoring the role’s high-profile public-service perks [3].
  • Historic ceremony site: Mamdani held a private swearing-in at the now-closed Old City Hall subway station, one of the city’s original 28 stations that opened in 1904 and stopped serving passengers in 1945 [4].
  • Transit tourism angle: The Old City Hall station is accessible today via guided tours run through the New York Transit Museum; Business Insider reported individual memberships start at $65 per year and tour tickets cost $50 [4].
  • Oath tradition: Economic Times reported Mamdani planned to take his oath on the Quran, making him the city’s first Muslim, South Asian, and African-born mayor according to the outlet [5].
  • Architecture highlight: Business Insider noted the Old City Hall station features tiled arches and ornate ceilings associated with architect Rafael Guastavino, spotlighting New York’s legacy infrastructure as a cultural asset [4].

Historical Background

New York City’s subway began service in 1904, and the City Hall station was among the system’s earliest stops [4]. The station later closed in 1945, leaving it as a preserved piece of civic infrastructure that is now mainly seen via museum-led tours [4].
