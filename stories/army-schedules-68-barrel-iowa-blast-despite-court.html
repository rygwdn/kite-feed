Army schedules 68-barrel Iowa blast despite court-ordered pause

Category: Science

The U.S. Army plans to detonate 68 barrels of degraded lead azide on Wednesday, Nov. 25, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, despite a federal appeals court order pausing the explosives disposal permit. The Army says it will rely on a separate emergency exception to proceed, arguing that the material could explode without warning as temperatures fall [11][13]. The Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club and Earthjustice challenged the plan over the risk that a blast could spread lead-bearing dust. The Justice Department says a controlled detonation is safer than an uncontrolled explosion. The groups say invoking emergency powers would bypass the court’s review and have asked to submit additional evidence challenging the Army’s risk assessment [3][17][13]. The Army moved the operation from Monday to Wednesday, saying the later date allows for favorable weather and time to put mitigation measures in place [18][11]. The groups’ legal challenge remains active, while the Army says it intends to proceed under the emergency exception [13][9].

Key Points

  • Alternative disposal: The Army hired a contractor to turn the explosives into harmless waste but later abandoned that approach in favor of detonating them in place [5].
  • Range estimates: Army projections have varied. Iowa officials’ reporting cited a recent model showing lead dust settling across an area about 25 miles long and 5 miles wide, while earlier plume estimates extended as far as 100 miles, depending on weather [18][3].
  • Blast-control concerns: Environmental engineer Ranajit Sahu questioned whether the proposed controls would adequately protect public health [9].
  • Farm concerns: Iowa agriculture officials advised nearby cattle farmers to move livestock, feed and equipment indoors or elsewhere, but farmers told the Iowa Environmental Council that keeping cattle indoors was not viable [11].
  • Local response: Mediapolis announced a school cancellation and library closure, and its school district said it would buy lead test kits to sample school grounds and buildings [4].

Perspectives

U.S. Army: The Army says the doors to the explosives’ storage bunker were removed in preparation for the planned blast and cannot be reinstalled, leaving the material more exposed to temperature changes [8].

Technical Details

  • Recrystallization: As liquid in the drums evaporated, needle-shaped lead azide crystals formed. The Army says the crystals are sensitive to friction and impact, making the barrels unsafe to open or move [3][8].
  • Blast controls: The Army’s plan uses earth-covered concrete magazines and sand barriers, with soil saturated and water sprayed before and during detonation to limit dust dispersal [7].

Scientific Significance

  • Lead exposure: Lead can damage the brain and nervous system, particularly in children. The environmental groups are concerned that deposited particles could reach homes, farmland and waterways [11][18].
  • Exposure measurement: University of Iowa professor David Cwiertny said field sampling after the blast would be needed to determine where lead settles, since farm fields and topsoil cannot simply be wiped clean like household surfaces [3].
  • Uncertainty in plume models: The Army’s maps depend on real-time wind conditions, and reported projections differ in the area they predict will be affected. Air and surface sampling after the blast would measure actual exposure [3][18].

Humanitarian Impact

The blast could produce loud noise and ground vibrations, with officials warning that windows could break; residents near Middletown City Park and Butterfly Park were told to leave for six hours to avoid vibration risks [18][3].

Q&A

Q: Will Illinois also monitor for effects from the plume?

A: The Illinois Environmental Protection Agency said it would watch data from its northern Illinois air-monitoring network for signs of impacts on Illinois communities [7].

Action Items

  • Follow local shelter and evacuation instructions if you are in the affected area: The Army advises people who cannot leave to remain indoors, close windows and doors, and turn off heating or cooling systems that bring in outside air during the exposure period [7].
  • Protect outdoor items and children’s play surfaces after officials end the exposure period: The Army advises wet-cleaning surfaces children may touch. Residents should follow local and Army guidance on when cleanup can begin [7].
