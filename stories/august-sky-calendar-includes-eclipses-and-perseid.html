August sky calendar includes eclipses and Perseid peak

Category: Science

August’s skywatching calendar includes a total solar eclipse on Aug. 12, the Perseid meteor shower peaking overnight Aug. 12-13, bright Venus after sunset in mid-August and a partial lunar eclipse on Aug. 27-28, according to NASA’s monthly skywatching guide [6]. The total solar eclipse path crosses northern Russia, Greenland, Iceland, northern Spain and a small part of Portugal, while parts of the United States will see only a partial eclipse [6]. Several European countries outside the path of totality will see large partial phases. The BBC reported that Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly could see about 95% of the Sun obscured, with Bristol, Somerset, Gloucestershire and Wiltshire expected to see around 90% [35]. Silicon Republic reported that Cork could see 96% coverage, Dublin 94% and Belfast 93%, making it the largest eclipse visible from Ireland until 2090 [47]. The Aug. 12 New Moon should keep moonlight from interfering with the Perseids [6][7]. NASA and ESA warned that eclipse viewers must use certified eclipse glasses or safe solar viewers whenever any part of the Sun is visible, and that regular sunglasses do not protect eyes during solar viewing [6][39].

Key Points

  • Planet viewing: Venus reaches greatest eastern elongation on Aug. 14-16 and appears low in the west after sunset, while Mercury and Jupiter make a predawn pairing later in the month [6].
  • Lunar eclipse: The Aug. 27-28 partial lunar eclipse will be visible from much of North and South America and parts of Europe and Africa [6].
  • UK timing: In Scotland, the eclipse is expected to begin shortly after 6 p.m., peak just after 7 p.m. and end around 8 p.m., with the Outer Hebrides among the best-placed areas [40].
  • Visibility limits: La Nación reported that the Aug. 12 total eclipse will not be visible from San José, Costa Rica, though Time and Date and the Royal Observatory Greenwich plan online broadcasts [44].

Technical Details

  • Solar viewing filters: Certified eclipse glasses must meet ISO 12312-2, and optical devices require solar filters mounted in front of the optics because magnified sunlight can damage eyes and equipment [45][47][6].
  • Zenithal hourly rate: The Perseids’ quoted ZHR of about 100 describes an idealized rate with the shower radiant overhead under dark skies, while Taiwan’s Taipei Astronomical Museum estimated a more realistic count near 70 meteors per hour for observers [24].
  • Venus dichotomy: Astronomy reported that Venus reaches a predicted half-lit phase around Aug. 11-12, but evening-apparition dichotomy can occur earlier than predicted because sunlight scatters through Venus’s thick atmosphere [8].

Historical Background

The Aug. 12 eclipse will be the biggest solar eclipse visible from Britain since August 1999, when a total eclipse crossed parts of Cornwall and Devon [35][41]. Austria also last experienced a total solar eclipse in 1999, and Vienna’s Aug. 12 event is described locally as its strongest partial eclipse since then [45].

Travel Advisory

  • Viewing: STV and Falter said the Sun will be low late in the day, making an unobstructed western view important in Scotland and Vienna [48][45]. Choose a site with a clear western horizon in evening-eclipse regions.

Action Items

  • Check your eclipse visibility: Use local eclipse maps or national astronomy guidance to confirm whether your area will see totality, a partial eclipse or no eclipse before making viewing plans [6][44].
  • Watch the Perseids after midnight: Find a dark location away from city lights and observe from after midnight until dawn on Aug. 12-13, when EarthSky says the radiant is higher and meteor counts improve [7].
  • Check the weather forecast: Clear skies are essential for eclipse viewing; check local forecasts before heading out [42].
