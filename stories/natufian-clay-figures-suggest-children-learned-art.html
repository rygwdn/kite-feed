Natufian clay figures suggest children learned art before farming

Category: Science

Archaeologists identified more than 150 clay-art fragments from Natufian sites in what is now Israel, dating from about 15,000 to 11,500 years ago. The oldest pieces predate farming in the region by thousands of years [1][2][4]. Fingerprints on the pieces, including a child’s print overlapping an adult’s on a small human figure, suggest that children helped shape the clay and learned by making figures with adults [3][4]. Clay figurines were long thought to have become common in the Middle East only with the Neolithic agricultural transition. The artifacts include animal and human forms, and some carry traces of red pigment. Researchers say the figurines’ purpose remains unclear [1][3][4].

This shift required diverse tools, skills, and knowledge, different from those needed for the manipulation of stone and bone.

— The study's authors (Popular Science)

Key Points

  • Artifact count: Only 15 of the 154 examined pieces were recognizable figures; the other 139 were cylinder-shaped fragments thought to belong to figures [3].
  • Who made them: Fingerprint ridge measurements suggest women and children, including some younger than 10, made most figurines; only a few fingerprint sets likely belonged to men [4].
  • Possible purpose: Most figurines measured less than 2 centimeters and showed no signs of intensive use, prompting lead author Laurent Davin to suggest they may have represented stories [4].

Perspectives

Steven Dorland, an archaeologist at Grand Valley State University, said the evidence shows the value of looking beyond adult men when studying ancient societies. His own research has used pottery and children’s fingerprints to examine social learning in Great Lakes Indigenous communities [4].

Sources: Science News

Technical Details

  • Microscopic re-examination: Researchers used microscopes to re-examine clay fragments from three sites that earlier studies had overlooked [3].
  • Pigment application: Researchers identified engobe, a thin wash of colored clay, on a piece that may be among the earliest known examples of the technique [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Limits of interpretation: The overlapping prints cannot establish the relationship between the adult and child; the evidence supports, but does not directly document, teaching [4][3].

Historical Background

Before these finds, archaeologists had identified Natufian figurines carved from bone and stone [1][3].
