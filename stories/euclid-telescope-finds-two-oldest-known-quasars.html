Euclid telescope finds two oldest known quasars

Category: Science

The European Space Agency said its Euclid space telescope has discovered 31 ancient quasars, including the two earliest yet observed, in findings published on July 6 in Astronomy & Astrophysics [3][4][7]. The two record-setting objects emitted their light when the universe was about 670 million years old, roughly 5% of its current age [1][3][4][5][7]. Quasars are intensely bright cores of early galaxies powered by supermassive black holes that draw in surrounding matter. ESA said the newly identified objects shone with about the light of a trillion Suns [3][5][7]. Astronomers use quasars to study how the first supermassive black holes and galaxies formed, but ESA said early quasars are rare, faint and easy to confuse with closer stars [1][3].

Timeline

  • 2021: The same team announced the previous record for oldest known quasar [4]
  • 2024: ESA launched Euclid to map the universe [1][3][4]
  • 6 July 2026: A study reporting 31 ancient quasars appeared in Astronomy & Astrophysics [4]

Scientific Significance

  • Early black-hole growth: The discovery extends direct observations of quasar activity to about 670 million years after the Big Bang, giving astronomers more targets to test how supermassive black holes accumulated mass so early [3][5][7].
  • Sample size: The 31 early quasars give researchers a larger comparison sample than isolated record holders. ESA says such objects are rare and easy to confuse with foreground stars [3][1].
  • Remaining uncertainty: The observations identify early luminous quasars, but they do not by themselves explain how their central black holes grew so quickly [4][7].

Historical Background

Before Euclid, ground-based telescopes had found only a few ancient quasars. The telescope's 2024 launch enabled the first wide-field space-based search for these faint early objects [1][4][3].
