Astronomers trace most distant radio burst to tiny, star-forming galaxy

Category: Science

Astronomers traced fast radio burst FRB 20240304B to a small, star-forming galaxy seen as it was when the universe was about 3 billion years old, making it the most distant burst with an identified host galaxy [1][2]. South Africa’s MeerKAT telescope detected the signal on March 4, 2024, after it had traveled for more than 10 billion years; the James Webb Space Telescope’s NIRCam located the faint host after ground-based telescopes failed to detect it [1][3]. The galaxy is metal-poor and unusually small. Its young stellar population supports a magnetar—a highly magnetized neutron star—as a possible source and makes a merger of two neutron stars less likely in this case [1][3]. The study, published in *Science*, does not establish what produces fast radio bursts; researchers say different bursts may have different origins [2][3].

Key Points

  • Tiny host: The host galaxy has a mass of about 10 million Suns, and researchers estimate 90% of its stellar mass formed in the past 30 million years [2][3].
  • Cosmic probe: As the burst crossed space, its radio signal recorded effects from an unidentified galaxy group about 3.5 billion light-years away and the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light-years from Earth [3].
  • Sparse host sample: Only about 100 FRBs have confirmed host galaxies, and most are billions of light-years closer [3].

Perspectives

Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney said researchers have theories about what generates FRBs but no conclusive proof. Proposed explanations range from neutron stars interacting with gas near supermassive black holes to possible technological signatures, according to Sci.News [1].

Sources: Sci.News

Ben Stappers of the University of Manchester said the next research goal is to determine how close to the first generations of stars astronomers can detect such bursts [1].

Timeline

  • 4 March 2024: MeerTRAP detected FRB 20240304B using South Africa’s MeerKAT telescope [1]
  • 8 October 2026: The study describing the burst appeared online in Science [1]

Technical Details

  • Dispersion: Charged material slows lower-frequency radio waves more than higher-frequency waves; measuring the resulting arrival-time delay helped estimate the burst’s distance and locate it in the sky [2].
  • Signal scattering: Turbulent gas, likely in the host galaxy, scattered the burst’s signal, spreading it over several milliseconds [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Cosmic timing: The detection shows FRBs were already occurring during cosmic noon, when star formation across the universe was near its peak [3].
  • Magnetic-field probe: Magnetic fields can twist the orientation of radio waves, leaving a measurable imprint that may help researchers study otherwise difficult-to-detect cosmic fields [2].
  • Energy scale: FRB energies are modest compared with those of supernovae or gamma-ray bursts, despite being extreme at radio wavelengths [3].
