Rubin Observatory starts 10-year southern sky survey

Category: Science

The NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory on Cerro Pachón in Chile began the Legacy Survey of Space and Time, a 10-year program to repeatedly image the southern sky with wide coverage and high detail [2][3][4][7][9][8]. The observatory, funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation and the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Science, is jointly operated by NSF NOIRLab and DOE's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory [2][8][19]. Rubin will scan the southern sky every few nights, taking hundreds of images a night and revisiting the same regions so researchers can detect objects that change brightness or position [2][8][15][16]. Scientists expect the survey to map billions of Milky Way stars and billions of galaxies, and to support studies of galaxy formation, dark matter, dark energy and solar system objects [8][9][10][12]. The launch followed a June 2025 First Look event, final commissioning work, an operational readiness review and the start of Rubin's alert stream [2][9][10]. Rubin Observatory Director Bob Blum said the launch followed more than two decades of work by the project team [13].

Key Points

  • Camera scale: Rubin's 8.4-meter Simonyi Survey Telescope uses the 3,200-megapixel LSST camera, described as the largest digital camera ever built [9][13][18].
  • Data flow: The observatory is expected to collect about 10 terabytes of data a night and generate millions of alerts for newly detected or changing objects [16][18][10].
  • Survey depth: Over the decade-long survey, Rubin is expected to produce nearly 1,000 full images of the southern sky and examine about 20 billion galaxies [9].

Perspectives

Phil Marshall, Rubin Observatory deputy director of operations: Marshall said the dataset would let scientists worldwide study the universe in ways not previously possible.

Timeline

  • June 2025: Rubin held its First Look event after construction [2][16].
  • October 2025: The facility transferred from construction to operations [13].
  • February 2026: The alert production pipeline began operating [10].
  • 30 June 2026: Rubin officially launched the Legacy Survey of Space and Time [2][13].

Technical Details

  • Cadence strategy: Rubin will repeatedly revisit southern-sky fields, with Serbian project partners saying each point will be photographed about 800 times over the survey [18].
  • Exposure throughput: Astronomy reported that the 3,200-megapixel camera produces a new sky image about every 40 seconds, allowing coverage of moving and variable targets [13].

Scientific Significance

  • Solar system census: The repeated imaging will enable discovery of millions of asteroids and other moving bodies, expanding the known inventory of small solar system objects and providing data to study their orbits and composition [10].
  • Cosmology tests: By measuring positions and brightness across billions of galaxies, Rubin data will support studies of dark matter and dark energy, including how galaxies cluster over cosmic time [9][8][12].
  • Data processing challenge: The project relies on software systems to process the nightly data output and filter the alert stream quickly, enabling astronomers to identify and follow up on transient events in near-real time [16][18][10].

Historical Background

Vera C. Rubin was a U.S. astronomer whose observations of galaxy rotation provided key evidence for dark matter [15][17]. The Rubin facility was formerly known as the Large Synoptic Survey Telescope before it was named for her [15].
