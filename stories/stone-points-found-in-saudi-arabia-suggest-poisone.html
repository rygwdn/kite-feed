Stone points found in Saudi Arabia suggest poisoned hunting weapons 125,000 years ago

Category: Science

Archaeologists found more than 5,500 stone artifacts at ‘Uruq Aljelibah, a site beside an ancient lake in what is now Saudi Arabia’s Empty Quarter. Chemical traces on several points suggest people may have used plant toxins in hunting about 125,000 years ago. Resin and plant fibers at the bases of some points, along with animal tissue near damaged tips, also indicate that people attached the stones to shafts and used them against large animals [1][4][3]. The lake once covered about 340 square kilometers and supported animals including hippos, buffalo and gazelles. The discovery suggests people lived and hunted in Arabia during a wet period, rather than simply crossing the peninsula. Researchers have not established whether people deliberately prepared the toxins or whether they were effective. The stone artifacts were collected from the surface rather than the dated lake layers, so the lake deposits provide a maximum age for the tools, not a direct date [6][1].

But we haven't demonstrated exactly how the poison was prepared or whether it was effective. So I'd describe these as potentially the oldest evidence for poisoned hunting weapons, rather than an unequivocally established first.

— Chris Clarkson, archaeologist and study co-author (Live Science)

Key Points

  • Plant toolkit: Other preserved traces include palm, wood, reeds and underground plant parts. Wear on toothed tools suggests people shredded plants, potentially for bindings and other equipment [4].
  • Earlier evidence: South African poisoned arrowheads have been dated to about 60,000 years ago. The Saudi lake deposits date to about 125,000 years ago, but that is only a maximum age for the surface-collected tools [1][4].
  • On-site toolmaking: Some stone flakes fit back together, indicating people made tools at the lakeshore rather than bringing them there ready-made [2][4].

Perspectives

Jeffrey Rose, Paleolithic archaeologist at the University of Algarve: Rose said sites in Arabia with both stone tools and animal bones are rare, and called this assemblage valuable new data [1].

Sources: Live Science

Technical Details

  • Luminescence dating: Researchers dated shoreline deposits by measuring signals in buried sand grains that record how long it had been since the grains were last exposed to sunlight. This dates the lake deposits, not the surface-collected tools directly [4][1].
  • Residue analysis: Chemical analysis identified biomarkers linked to Acokanthera schimperi, a tree historically used for its toxins [4][3].

Scientific Significance

  • Preserved organic traces: Plant residues reveal parts of early technology that usually disappear from the archaeological record, including materials used to make composite tools [3][4].
  • Toxin traces on a used point: At least one impact-damaged point carried potentially toxic plant compounds alongside animal residues, linking those traces to a point that had struck an animal [6][1][4].

Historical Background

Arabia lies between Africa and Asia. Earlier research documents repeated occupation by different human groups over the past 400,000 years during wetter phases [4]. A finger bone from northern Arabia places Homo sapiens there at least 85,000 years ago, while footprints in the Nafud Desert have been dated to about 130,000 to 115,000 years ago [4][5].

Q&A

Q: How might hunters have eaten animals struck with poison?

A: Archaeologist Chris Clarkson said poison entering through a wound may act differently from swallowed poison, and hunters have historically removed meat around the wound before eating the rest [1].

Q: What are researchers planning to investigate next?

A: The team plans to examine the poison residues further and explore other sites near the lake [6][1].
