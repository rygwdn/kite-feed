Australian researchers date oldest known impact crater to 3 billion years

Category: Science

Researchers at Curtin University dated the North Pole Dome impact structure in Western Australia’s Pilbara region to about 3.024 billion years ago, which would make it the oldest known asteroid impact crater on Earth if the interpretation holds [2][3][6][1][7][8][9][10][11][12]. The study, published in Geology, revised the team’s earlier estimate of about 3.47 billion years and addresses competing interpretations that placed the structure at less than 2.77 billion years old or within a much wider possible range [2][7][12]. The researchers dated tiny zircon crystals and apatite in shock-damaged rocks and said both mineral systems point to the same roughly 3-billion-year event [3][6][11][10]. The age would place North Pole Dome in the Archean eon, when Earth’s earliest continents were forming, and would make it the only recognized Archean impact crater currently known [2][6][9][11][12].

Primary Source: View original article

The impact left a 'mineral clock' behind. By dating minerals that were remade or newly grown in the damaged rocks, we can now pin down when this extraordinary event happened.

— Chris Kirkland (Popular Science)

Key Points

  • Preserved rocks: The Pilbara site contains dark, weathered volcanic rocks nearly 3.5 billion years old, including pillow basalts that formed when lava erupted underwater on an ancient seafloor [9][10].
  • Impact evidence: Researchers identified shatter cones at North Pole Dome, rock structures produced by extreme shock pressure and used as a diagnostic sign of meteorite impact [9][12].
  • Sparse record: Earth preserves far fewer ancient impact craters than the Moon, Mercury or Mars because volcanism, deformation and erosion recycle or erase old crustal evidence [2][12].

Perspectives

Chris Kirkland, Curtin University: Kirkland said the younger interpretation allowed the crater to be any age between about 2.77 billion and 400 million years old, spanning roughly half of Earth history, and said the new date places the impact firmly in the Archean.

Sources: ScienceAlert

Other researchers: New Scientist reported that some researchers dispute the proposed 3-billion-year age for the North Pole Dome impact structure.

Sources: New Scientist

Technical Details

  • Zircon recrystallisation: Some zircon grains showed unusual skeletal or branching forms interpreted as crystals disrupted, partly recrystallised and regrown during intense impact heating [3][11].
  • Apatite cross-check: Apatite formed as hot fluids moved through shock-damaged rocks, providing an independent date that aligned with the zircon result [3][11].
  • Isotopic dating workflow: Researchers searched microscopic rock slices for suitable crystals, then used laser analysis to measure chemistry and isotopic signatures used to calculate mineral ages [10].

Scientific Significance

  • Record holder: The revised age keeps North Pole Dome as the oldest known terrestrial impact structure, older than the next-oldest, Yarrabubba crater in Western Australia, which is about 2.229 billion years old [7].
  • Dating technique: The team used laser ablation split-stream analysis to measure uranium-lead ratios in both zircon and apatite, producing two independent age estimates from the same impact event [11].
  • Disputed finding: New Scientist reported that other researchers still dispute the dating interpretation [5].

Historical Background

The Archean eon spans roughly 4 billion to 2.5 billion years ago, when Earth’s crust, oceans, atmosphere and early life were still evolving [2][9]. The research team first reported the North Pole Dome rocks in 2025 as part of an ancient impact crater, but the impact age remained unresolved [3][9][12].
