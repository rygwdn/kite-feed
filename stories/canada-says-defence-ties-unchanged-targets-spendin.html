Canada says defence ties unchanged, targets spending of 4% of GDP by 2030

Category: Canada

Canadian Defence Minister David McGuinty said Tuesday that military cooperation with the United States remains unchanged and that Ottawa does not answer to Washington. He was responding to Pentagon criticism of Canada’s spending and its review of a planned F-35 fighter purchase [2][3][12]. NATO’s spending commitment calls for 5% of GDP by 2035. McGuinty said Canada is on a path to spend the equivalent of 4% of gross domestic product on defence by 2030. The federal budget projects combined defence and security spending of 4.1% of GDP in 2027-28, including 2.6% for core military spending. McGuinty’s office says the government has committed C$81.8 billion over five years to rebuild the armed forces [2][8][14]. The Pentagon says Ottawa lacks a credible, fully costed plan for the 5% goal and is delaying its largest increases until after the 2029 election [8][13]. McGuinty said Ottawa is focused on carrying out its plans, including rebuilding 33 bases and improving recruitment and retention [2]. Canada has not made a final decision on its F-35 order, which has been under review for more than a year [3][1].

Key Points

  • Fleet readiness: The Pentagon said 41.9% of Canada’s air force fleet and 53.9% of its naval fleet were serviceable in 2025-26, while army readiness stood at 48% [8][10][14].
  • Procurement shift: Prime Minister Mark Carney said Canada should no longer send three-quarters of its defence capital spending to the United States [8][14].
  • NATO review: McGuinty said NATO allies agreed to review the 5% spending commitment in 2029, including the pace and balance of spending against security threats and military needs [16].
  • Military rebuild: McGuinty said efforts to fix a cadet challenge were also underway [2].
  • Aircraft alternatives: Canada has examined Swedish-made Gripen fighters as an alternative to its planned purchase of 88 U.S.-made F-35s [8][10].

Historical Background

NATO set its benchmark of spending 2% of GDP on defence in 2014; Canada said it met the target for the first time in 2025 [3][6]. The U.S.-Canada Permanent Joint Board on Defense is a bilateral advisory forum established in 1940 [12][5].

Q&A

Q: Which major equipment purchases are part of Canada’s defence rebuilding plans?

A: The plans include submarines and early-warning aircraft; Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government has said it intends to acquire up to 12 replacement submarines [8][14].
