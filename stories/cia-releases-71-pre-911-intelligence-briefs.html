CIA releases 71 pre-9/11 intelligence briefs

Category: World

The CIA released 71 declassified President’s Daily Brief documents on September 11, 2026, marking the 25th anniversary of the attacks. The release contains more than 100 pages of intelligence prepared for Presidents Bill Clinton and George W. Bush from February 1998 through September 12, 2001, including warnings that Osama bin Laden and al-Qaeda were pursuing attacks against the United States [3][4][5][10][31]. CIA Director John Ratcliffe described the release as the agency’s largest publication of analysis related to 9/11 [7][10][31]. The documents trace bin Laden’s movements, support network and financing, and describe potential threats to U.S. targets in other countries [7].

Did you know: The documents include an assessment that extremist websites recruited fighters and circulated information about chemical and biological weapons [64].

Key Points

  • Airport trial run: A December 1998 assessment said members of bin Laden’s network had received hijacking training and that conspirators had evaded security checks during a trial run at an unidentified New York airport [11][28].
  • FBI investigations: The August 6, 2001 briefing said the FBI was conducting about 70 investigations considered related to bin Laden [11][27].
  • Post-attack account: A September 12, 2001 report cited a source saying the attacks had been planned for two years and had also targeted the U.S. Capitol [4][7][10].
  • Other targets: The released material included intelligence about possible attacks on a Washington shopping center, chemical weapons use, nuclear-material acquisition and the kidnapping of an American [28][27].

Perspectives

CIA: The agency said the documents trace analysts’ evolving understanding of al-Qaeda and their efforts to warn about bin Laden’s plotting despite sparse, vague and imperfect information [3][24].

Intelligence historians: Analysts quoted in coverage said the documents do not fundamentally change the established understanding of what the U.S. government knew before the attacks or why it failed to anticipate them [28][27][53].

9/11 Commission findings: The commission’s 2004 report concluded that intelligence agencies warned two successive administrations but failed to convey the threat’s scale and urgency effectively, while agencies worked too separately to connect the information [47][55].

Timeline

  • February 1998: CIA brief addressed terrorists seeking weapons of mass destruction [4][10]
  • 10 September 1998: Brief warned al-Qaeda might fly an explosive-packed plane into a U.S. city [3][26]
  • 9 March 2000: Assessment addressed al-Qaeda threats against Israel [26]
  • 6 August 2001: CIA brief titled “Bin Laden Determined To Strike in US” reached Bush [10][27]
  • 12 September 2001: Final document was timestamped 4 a.m. and quoted a Kandahar-based Arab source [27]
  • 11 September 2026: CIA released 71 documents marking the attacks’ 25th anniversary [31][3]

Technical Details

  • PDB structure: The collection consists of President’s Daily Brief products prepared for the president and senior officials, including a report dated the day after the attacks [31].
  • Redaction limits: Several documents, including the September 10, 1998 warning, remain partially redacted, leaving some sources and operational details unavailable [3][5].
  • Y2K risk assessment: A December 30, 1999 intelligence assessment examined whether computer failures linked to the Y2K date change could create opportunities for terrorists [64].

Historical Background

The documents cover the period after al-Qaeda bombed U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in August 1998, prompting U.S. missile strikes against al-Qaeda bases in Afghanistan and Sudan [17]. President’s Daily Briefs are normally among the most closely guarded intelligence records and are often withheld for about 40 years to protect sources and avoid tensions with allies [34][56].

Humanitarian Impact

The September 11 attacks killed nearly 3,000 people, including victims from about 90 countries [1][24]. The current release concerns historical records and does not report new casualties or immediate humanitarian needs.
