Studies find Alzheimer’s warning signals years before symptoms emerge

Category: Science

Separate studies report changes in the brain, sleep, navigation and nerve-muscle communication that may appear before Alzheimer’s symptoms, but none establishes a routine early-detection test [2][4][6][5][8]. A Johns Hopkins team found that the entorhinal cortex and amygdala shrank earlier and faster than the hippocampus, with tissue loss in those regions associated with local tau changes [1][2]. A Stanford-led team used resting-state brain scans to develop an experimental score linked to faster cognitive decline, amyloid buildup and later diagnoses among more than 1,300 initially healthy adults followed for up to 12 years [4][7]. Other research examined spatial navigation in 100 middle-aged adults at elevated dementia risk. A separate laboratory study found that familial Alzheimer’s mutations disrupted nerve-muscle communication in a model without a brain or spinal cord [6][5]. A sleep study of 116 adults linked features of deep-sleep waves with cognitive changes over time [8]. The findings come from distinct studies and require further validation; the Stanford score is not available for clinical use, and the nerve-muscle experiment used laboratory models [4][5][6][8].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Scan comparison: The Stanford team built its experimental ARC brain-connectivity score by comparing scans from 180 adults aged 75 or older who had unusually strong memory with scans from 110 people with Alzheimer’s [4][7].
  • Navigation differences: The virtual-reality study found a stronger difference in path integration among men, while researchers described possible navigation differences among women as tentative [6].
  • Measured outcomes: Researchers tested how effectively nerve impulses triggered muscle contractions and how long the muscles functioned before fatigue [5].
  • Sleep findings: Steeper and taller slow waves were associated with better memory and thinking later in cognitively unimpaired participants; the reported stronger association in men was exploratory [8].

Perspectives

Stanford research team: The team says its ARC brain-connectivity score is experimental. Yu Zhang characterized an individual result as a snapshot rather than a determination of that person’s future. Researchers plan to test whether cognitive training can shift the measured brain pattern.

Sources: WELT

Coco Newton, first author of the University College London study: Newton said navigation changes could mark an early point in the disease continuum. She said the team is developing a clinical decision-support tool for the UK National Health Service, but further work is needed to understand differences between men and women.

Sources: HuffPost UK

Allison B. Reiss, associate professor of medicine at NYU Grossman Long Island School of Medicine: Reiss said Alzheimer’s blood biomarkers should not be used for screening or as stand-alone diagnoses. She said clinicians need a comprehensive assessment and must interpret test results alongside other clinical information.

Sources: NeurologyLive

James Hickman, University of Central Florida professor and study lead: Hickman said the laboratory findings show that familial Alzheimer’s mutations can directly impair peripheral nerve function, but the study’s cell-based model does not establish that such changes reliably precede cognitive symptoms in patients.

Sources: Digi24

Technical Details

  • ARC brain-connectivity score: The score uses resting-state functional MRI patterns of communication among brain regions. The Stanford team derived it from two contrasting groups of older adults, then applied it to a separate cohort of more than 1,300 healthy adults [4][7].
  • Peripheral nerve-muscle model: Researchers recreated the neuromuscular junction using motor neurons derived from stem cells carrying the mutations and healthy muscle cells. They isolated peripheral effects by excluding the brain and spinal cord from the experimental system [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Limits and validation: The navigation work involved a relatively small high-risk cohort, while the sleep study found associations that do not establish causation [6][8].
