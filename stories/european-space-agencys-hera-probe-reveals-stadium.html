European Space Agency's Hera probe reveals stadium-sized crater from NASA asteroid test

Category: Science

The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft has revealed that NASA’s 2022 impact test carved a crater spanning nearly the full width of Dimorphos, a 151-meter asteroid moonlet, and left it tumbling chaotically around its larger companion, Didymos. Hera’s first close-up images also show hundreds of boulders around the pair; neither asteroid poses a threat to Earth [7][4][9]. The Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) shortened Dimorphos’s orbital period around Didymos by 33 minutes, but scientists had not seen the collision’s physical effects in detail. Hera’s measurements are intended to show how the asteroid’s shape, mass and structure affected the deflection and help refine predictions for future impact missions [7][13][5]. Hera is surveying the asteroids from about 25 to 30 kilometers away. The European Space Agency (ESA) plans to move Hera closer in December 2026 and deploy two CubeSats in January 2027; one is designed to use radar to examine Dimorphos’s interior [7][6][31].

Did you know: Didymos’s surface gravity is about 40,000 times weaker than Earth’s, according to Hera team member Naomi Murdoch [9].

Key Points

  • Uneven damage: Hera’s images show little apparent change to the boulder fields on the side of Dimorphos opposite the impact, despite the crater across the struck face [7][20].
  • Boulder hazard: The newly observed rocks range from tens of centimeters to several meters, and their origin—impact debris or material already in the system—has not been established [24][30][9].
  • Image resolution: Hera's first close-up imagery resolved features at about 2.7 meters per pixel [36][1].
  • DART benchmark: NASA had defined a reduction of at least 73 seconds in Dimorphos’s orbital period as a successful test; the measured change was about 33 minutes [8][3].
  • Surface composition: Italy’s Milani CubeSat is tasked with examining the asteroid’s surface composition and surrounding dust [7][29][31].

Perspectives

Ian Whittaker, a physics lecturer at Nottingham Trent University, cautioned that predicting a deflection is not straightforward because the result depends partly on how much material an asteroid ejects when struck [13].

Technical Details

  • Momentum transfer: Measuring Dimorphos’s mass alongside its observed orbital change will help researchers estimate how efficiently DART transferred momentum, including the contribution of ejected material [31][13].
  • Hera instruments: The spacecraft’s Asteroid Framing Camera records monochrome images, Hyperscout H observes in 25 spectral bands, and JAXA’s Thermal Infrared Imager measures surface temperature [7][42].
  • Close-range radar: The Juventas CubeSat is designed to use low-frequency radar to probe the asteroid’s interior, a measurement that could constrain its internal structure [7][31].

Scientific Significance

  • Impact physics: DART’s mass, speed and trajectory were known, and Hera’s images can be compared with pre-impact views, giving researchers an unusual dataset for studying impacts at asteroid scale [13].
  • Rubble-pile behavior: Dimorphos is thought to be an aggregate of smaller rocks held together by extremely weak gravity, making its response to an impact harder to predict than that of a solid body [8].
  • System-wide effects: DART changed not only Dimorphos’s orbit around Didymos but also the binary pair’s orbit around the Sun [2].

Historical Background

The collision destroyed DART, and impact debris obscured the site from the accompanying LICIACube spacecraft. By the time the dust cleared, LICIACube had departed, leaving Hera to document the crater directly [8][31].
