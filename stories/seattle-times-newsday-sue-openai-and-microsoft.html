Seattle Times, Newsday sue OpenAI and Microsoft

Category: Technology

The Seattle Times and Newsday sued OpenAI and Microsoft on Friday in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, alleging the companies copied their journalism without permission to train and operate AI products including ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Bing’s AI features [4][6][8]. The complaint says the companies scraped the newspapers’ websites, including paywalled content, and put articles into datasets used by the AI systems [4][6][2][8]. The newspapers say the AI products can reproduce passages, closely paraphrase articles and answer users in ways that reduce visits to their websites or paid subscriptions [4][6][8]. They also allege the systems can attribute false information to the outlets and strip copyright management information from articles [1][2]. OpenAI said its models are trained on publicly available data and supported by fair use, while Microsoft said it was surprised by the lawsuit and willing to discuss solutions [4][6][8][5].

Key Points

  • Court request: The newspapers asked the court to order destruction of copies of their works, training datasets or AI models that incorporated the material [6][8].
  • Broader litigation: Dozens of similar lawsuits have been filed against OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and others over use of copyrighted content for AI training [8].
  • Funding ties: Microsoft and OpenAI have funded some Seattle Times journalism projects and fellowships, making the lawsuit notable in the newspaper’s hometown market [3][5].

Perspectives

The Seattle Times and Newsday: The complaint describes generative AI as “a snake eating its own tail” and says AI could “destroy the very organizations” whose work it uses.

Sources: TechCrunch

Industry Impact

  • Local media: Newsday said AI chatbots trained on large text batches, including news articles, have alarmed media outlets across the United States [7].
  • AI licensing: AP and Vox Media have licensed archives to OpenAI, while some publishers have pursued court claims [1][2].
  • EU code: The EU's general-purpose AI code of practice commits signatories not to circumvent access restrictions, including paywalls [2].

Historical Background

The lawsuit follows The New York Times’ 2023 case against OpenAI and Microsoft over alleged use of its articles to train AI systems [1][5][8]. The cases are proceeding as AI developers and publishers test whether large-scale training on copyrighted works qualifies as fair use under U.S. copyright law [common].

Business Angle

  • OpenAI and Microsoft face potential downside if courts require paid licenses or destruction of datasets and models containing publisher content [6][8].
  • Publishers that secure licensing deals may create recurring revenue streams, while litigating publishers carry legal costs and uncertain timing [1][2].
  • Investors should watch whether courts treat paywall access differently from general web scraping because that issue could narrow AI developers’ data options [2].
