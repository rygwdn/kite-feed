OpenAI chief apologizes over unreported Tumbler Ridge suspect

Category: Technology

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman publicly apologized to the community of Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, saying he was "deeply sorry" that the company did not alert law enforcement after banning a ChatGPT account belonging to the alleged Feb. 10 shooter [1][2][3][4][5]. OpenAI suspended the account in June 2025 after its systems detected content suggesting possible real-world violence, but it did not notify police at the time [1][2][5]. The apology follows reporting that, after the first ban, the user created a second ChatGPT account that OpenAI did not identify until after the attack, which killed eight people in the small Canadian town [2]. Altman said OpenAI will work with governments to help prevent similar tragedies and reaffirmed a policy shift the company outlined earlier: it will notify authorities when it identifies imminent and credible threats in ChatGPT conversations [1][4].

I am deeply sorry that we did not alert law enforcement to the account that was banned in June.

— Sam Altman (Engadget)

Key Points

  • Second account: Mashable reported that after OpenAI banned the first account, the suspect created a second ChatGPT account that the company did not discover until after the shooting [2].
  • Government contact: Altman said he spoke with Tumbler Ridge mayor Darryl Krakowa and British Columbia premier David Eby, and that they agreed a public apology was needed after the community had time to grieve [1].
  • Premier response: Eby said Altman’s apology was necessary but called it "grossly insufficient for the devastation done to the families of Tumbler Ridge" [1].

Technical Details

  • Threat escalation threshold: OpenAI’s revised approach focuses on reporting "imminent and credible" threats, a narrower standard than simply detecting violent content. That distinction matters because it sets the threshold for notifying law enforcement [1].
  • Account re-creation risk: The suspect’s ability to open a second account after the first was banned highlights a basic enforcement challenge for online AI platforms: action on one account may not stop someone from returning under another identity [2].

Industry Impact

  • AI governance: The case adds pressure on AI firms to define when flagged conversations should trigger contact with law enforcement, especially after OpenAI said it would report imminent and credible threats going forward [1].
  • Platform safety operations: OpenAI’s failure to catch a second account after banning the first underscores how hard it can be to enforce safety rules across repeat users on consumer AI platforms [2].

Historical Background

OpenAI’s June 2025 account ban is central to the current scrutiny because the company’s systems had already flagged material suggesting possible real-world violence before the Feb. 10, 2026 attack [1][2][5]. After the shooting, OpenAI said it would change its safety protocols, and British Columbia premier David Eby said in March that Altman would apologize and call for stronger regulations [2]. The case now sits within a broader debate over when AI companies should escalate dangerous user behavior to law enforcement [common].

Action Items

  • Review AI threat-reporting policies: Organizations that deploy chatbots can audit whether flagged violent content is escalated to human reviewers and, when legally appropriate, to law enforcement, reflecting the tighter standards now under discussion [1][common].
  • Check account controls: Platform operators can test whether a banned user can create a replacement account, since this case showed how a second account can undermine an initial enforcement action [2].
