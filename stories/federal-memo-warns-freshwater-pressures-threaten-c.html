Federal memo warns freshwater pressures threaten Canada’s long-term security

Category: Canada

An internal federal memo warns that growing pressure on freshwater supplies could harm Canada’s long-term security and prosperity. It says it is unclear whether the country can meet future needs as its cities and economy grow [1][3][4]. The Canada Water Agency prepared the document for Environment Minister Julie Dabrusin as work proceeds on a national water security strategy. Dabrusin called the strategy a “mission critical priority” in response to Prime Minister Mark Carney’s request for cabinet priorities [2][5]. The memo links water risks to climate change, pollution, population growth, overuse and aging infrastructure. Public consultation ran from Sept. 9 to Oct. 25, and the agency also consulted provincial, territorial and Indigenous partners [1][3]. The memo calls for an assessment of water protection and sustainable management, including shared waters with the United States and global water risks. The 12-page memo was obtained through an access-to-information request, with some material withheld [4][5].

The Arctic and the North also face critical issues from increased development and climate change impacts on already overwhelmed infrastructure. Furthermore, First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities are among the most water insecure in Canada.

— Canada Water Agency (Winnipeg Free Press)

Key Points

  • River quality: From 2021 to 2023, water quality at 51% of 165 southern Canadian river monitoring sites was rated fair to poor; at 49%, it was rated excellent or good [1][3][5].
  • Uneven supply: Canada holds 20% of the world’s surface freshwater, but only one-third is salt-free and annually replenished by precipitation; much renewable surface water flows north, away from major population centres [2][4].
  • Groundwater gaps: Groundwater is Canada’s largest freshwater resource, yet aquifers remain unmapped in some areas even though nearly one in three Canadians depends on groundwater for clean water [3][5].
  • Shared waters: More than 40% of the Canada-U.S. border crosses water. The memo warns that disputes could become more frequent as supplies shrink and demand rises, despite more than a dozen bilateral water agreements [1][4][5].

Perspectives

Canadians opposed to bulk water exports: The memo says some Canadians oppose diverting lakes and rivers and extend that opposition to any form of bulk freshwater export to the United States or elsewhere [3].

Sources: Global News

Industry Impact

  • Electricity: More than half of Canadian electricity comes from hydropower, which the memo says supports the transition to cleaner energy and a low-carbon economy [1][3].
  • Food production: The memo identifies water storage and irrigation as critical to high-value crop production in the Prairies and interior British Columbia, as well as to greenhouse operations [4][5].
  • Manufacturing and digital economy: The document says manufacturing is among Canada’s largest water-consuming industries and that growth in the digital economy depends on a secure, sustainably managed supply [2][3].
