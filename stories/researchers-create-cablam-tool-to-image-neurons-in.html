Researchers create CaBLAM tool to image neurons internally

Category: Science

Researchers at Brown University and collaborators have developed a bioluminescence-based method that lets living neurons generate light signals researchers can measure, avoiding the need to shine external light into the brain during experiments [1][3]. In a Nature Methods study, the team describes Ca2+ BioLuminescence Activity Monitor (CaBLAM), which can capture activity at the level of single cells and even subcellular structures at high speed [1]. The tool works in animal models including mice and zebrafish, and it supports recordings lasting for hours, which could make it easier to observe how neural circuits operate over time with less specialized hardware than some laser-based approaches [1].

Key Points

  • NSF support: The Bioluminescence Hub at Brown’s Carney Institute for Brain Science launched in 2017 with a major National Science Foundation grant to develop and share tools that let nervous system cells both make and respond to light [1].
  • Collaborative build: The work grew from collaborations among Brown researchers Christopher Moore and Diane Lipscombe, plus Ute Hochgeschwender (Central Michigan University) and Nathan Shaner (UC San Diego) [1].
  • Molecule leadership: Nathan Shaner led development of the molecular device that became CaBLAM, according to Moore’s account of the project [1].
  • Why bioluminescence: The researchers frame bioluminescence as a way to sidestep some downsides of laser-based experiments, including reliance on “fancy hardware” and a lower rate of experimental success [1].
  • Origin story: The team traces the idea back about a decade, when scientists began exploring whether they could “light up the brain from the inside” to visualize neural activity [1][3].

  • CaBLAM sensor scope: The Ca2+ BioLuminescence Activity Monitor (CaBLAM) is described as capturing rapid activity at single-cell and subcellular scales [1].
  • Model compatibility: The team reports CaBLAM works in mice and zebrafish and can support multi-hour recordings [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: If CaBLAM performs as described, it adds a complementary way to observe neural activity—particularly when external illumination would be inconvenient—by shifting signal generation to inside the cell via bioluminescence [1][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The articles highlight capabilities (speed, single-cell/subcellular resolution, multi-hour recordings) but do not provide quantitative performance metrics (e.g., signal-to-noise, error bounds, or head-to-head benchmarks) in the text provided, so readers should treat the reported advantages as qualitative until reviewing the Nature Methods paper directly [1].
  • Limitations and future work: The reports focus on demonstrations in mice and zebrafish, implying that broader adoption will depend on how easily other labs can implement the approach and whether it translates to additional systems and experimental setups [1].

Historical Background

Fluorescence-based brain imaging commonly involves illuminating tissue with external light and detecting emitted signals, an approach that has powered many modern neuroscience experiments [1]. About a decade ago, researchers began exploring bioluminescence as an alternative way to visualize neural activity by generating light from within cells rather than shining it in from outside [1][3].
