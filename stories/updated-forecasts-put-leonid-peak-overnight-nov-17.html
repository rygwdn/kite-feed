Updated forecasts put Leonid peak overnight Nov. 17-18

Category: Science

Updated forecasts place the Leonid meteor shower’s peak between late Tuesday, Nov. 17, and dawn Wednesday, Nov. 18, revising earlier expectations that focused on Monday night [3][1][16]. The annual display occurs as Earth crosses debris from comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle [1][16]. Estimates differ sharply: the International Meteor Organization says as many as 15 meteors an hour may be visible under ideal dark skies, while NASA estimates fewer than three an hour [1][2][6]. The best viewing window is after midnight, when the moon has set and the constellation Leo climbs higher. Meteors can appear across the sky, so observers do not need to look directly at Leo [1][2]. Rates depend on darkness, weather and viewing conditions; the predicted peak rate does not guarantee how many meteors an observer will see [8][10]. No meteor storm is expected this year. The shower remains active through Nov. 30, and astronomers advise watching from a dark location with an open view and allowing time for eyes to adjust [3][18][11].

Key Points

  • Southern viewing: The shower is visible in both hemispheres, but Leo stays lower south of the equator, limiting viewing opportunities there [1][11].
  • Storm forecasts: Predictions for a future Leonid storm differ. One study cited by Space.com points to possible displays in 2033 or 2034, while an American Meteor Society forecast cited by FOX5 New York says a major storm may not come until 2099 [2][14].
  • Planet pairing: Bright Jupiter and Mars appear close together near Leo during the peak, offering another naked-eye target in the same region of sky [2][12].

Timeline

  • 1833: A Leonid storm helped spur scientific study of meteors [1][11]
  • 2002: The most recent major Leonid storm occurred, according to Scientific American [4]
  • 6 November 2026: The annual shower’s active period began [6]
  • 17 November 2026: The International Meteor Organization’s forecast puts the peak at 23:45 UTC [1][16]

Scientific Significance

  • Meteor speed: Leonid particles enter the atmosphere at roughly 71 kilometers per second, among the fastest speeds reported for annual meteor showers. Their rapid entry produces brief, sometimes bright streaks [1][7].

Historical Background

The Leonids can surge dramatically when Earth crosses particularly dense debris trails; outside those outbursts, their annual display is much more modest [1][4]. The parent comet’s roughly 33-year orbit replenishes the trail, and it is due back in 2031 [2][10].

Q&A

Q: What is the next major meteor shower after the Leonids?

A: The Geminids are expected to peak overnight Dec. 13-14; under ideal conditions they can produce up to 120 meteors an hour [3].

Action Items

  • Dress warmly and bring a sleeping bag for an extended wait outdoors [4].
