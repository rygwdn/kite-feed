TikTok closes US joint venture deal with Oracle

Category: Technology

TikTok says it has finalized a deal creating a new majority American-owned entity, TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC, to keep the app operating in the United States and avert a ban tied to national-security concerns over its China-based parent, ByteDance [1][18][16][6][15]. Under the arrangement, ByteDance retains 19.9% while a group of non-Chinese investors—including Oracle, Silver Lake, and Abu Dhabi-based MGX—take the rest, with the venture operating under promised safeguards for U.S. user data, algorithms, and content moderation [10][6][4][2]. The deal, which sources say has approval from both the U.S. and Chinese governments, ends years of regulatory uncertainty and lets U.S. users keep using the same app for now [6][1][15].

Key Points

  • China tech shift: In the wake of the TikTok outcome, some Chinese firms are rethinking how to expand globally amid US-China geopolitical tensions, with some opting to avoid the US market altogether [26][28].
  • New governance: The venture plans a seven-person board that includes TikTok CEO Shou Chew, and it names trust-and-safety executive Adam Presser as CEO of TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC [33].
  • Terms and privacy: U.S. users were prompted to accept updated terms and a privacy policy that, according to reporting on the changes, now permits precise location collection if users grant location permission and explicitly covers data tied to interactions with TikTok’s AI tools [23][21][22].
  • Policy responsibility: The joint venture will hold decision-making authority for U.S. trust-and-safety policies and content moderation, while TikTok’s global entities continue to handle international product integration and some commercial activities [4][10][19].
  • Privacy changes: A new US privacy-policy version says that if users enable location services, TikTok can collect approximate or precise location information [3].
  • Algorithm handling: The recommendation system for the US backend will be licensed from ByteDance, then retrained on US user data and updated under the new arrangement, according to reporting on the deal’s mechanics [30].
  • Ownership structure: ByteDance’s stake is set below the 20% threshold referenced in U.S. law, while Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX each take 15% as managing investors alongside smaller stakes from groups such as the Dell Family Office and affiliates of Susquehanna International Group and General Atlantic [4][10][6].
  • Politics and culture: Some commentators and users have raised concerns about how new U.S. ownership and politically connected investors could influence the platform’s cultural or moderation direction, even as the deal is framed as a national-security fix [14][11].
  • Ownership split: TikTok said the new venture is 80.1% owned by American investors and 19.9% by ByteDance, aligning with the divest-or-ban law’s cap on ByteDance ownership [16][33].
  • Algorithm controls: TikTok says its recommendation algorithm will be retrained only on U.S. user data and secured within Oracle’s cloud, with Oracle also serving as a “Trusted Security Partner” to review and validate TikTok source code [6][10].

Technical Details

  • Precise location collection: Reporting on TikTok’s updated privacy policy says the app may collect GPS-derived precise location if users grant location permission, adding a higher-resolution signal than broader location indicators [23].
  • Oracle “trusted security partner” role: TikTok says Oracle will host U.S. user data in a secure U.S. cloud environment and review/validate TikTok source code as part of software assurances [6][10].
  • U.S.-only model retraining: The company says its recommendation algorithm will be retrained exclusively on U.S. user data, a step intended to limit dependence on non-U.S. datasets for U.S. feeds [6][7].
  • USDS joint venture: TikTok’s deal creates a new US entity (TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC) meant to oversee US user data, trust-and-safety and content moderation functions [33].
  • Location data controls: TikTok’s updated privacy policy says location collection depends on users enabling location services, distinguishing approximate from precise location information [3].
  • Licensed algorithm: Under the reported structure, ByteDance licenses the recommendation algorithm for the US backend, which will then be retrained on US user data [30].

Industry Impact

  • Social app governance: The creation of a U.S.-majority-owned operating entity with defined security safeguards adds a potential playbook for addressing data-sovereignty disputes without fully replacing the consumer-facing product [1][19].
  • Cloud and security: Oracle’s participation underscores how cloud infrastructure and data-governance services can become core components of resolving cross-border tech policy disputes [16][33].
  • China tech expansion: The TikTok resolution highlights the hurdles Chinese tech firms face in the US, influencing some to adjust global growth plans and, in some cases, sidestep the US market [26][28].
  • Privacy competition: As TikTok’s updated policy draws attention to precise location and AI-tool data, rival platforms may face renewed pressure to simplify permissions and disclosures to keep users comfortable sharing data [23][common].
  • Platform compliance: New terms-and-conditions changes show how consumer apps may expand or clarify sensitive data categories, increasing pressure on privacy disclosures and user controls [3][25].
  • Cloud and compliance services: Oracle’s hosting and source-code review role positions it as a high-profile provider of “trusted” infrastructure for politically sensitive apps, a template other platforms may be asked to follow [6][10].
  • Digital advertising: Keeping TikTok available in the US preserves a major channel for brand marketing and performance ads, avoiding disruption that a ban would have caused [31][32].
  • Creators and commerce: TikTok says the safeguards extend to related apps such as CapCut and Lemon8, signaling continuity for creators and businesses that rely on TikTok’s ecosystem tools [10].

Historical Background

TikTok’s U.S. regulatory saga began in 2020, when President Trump attempted to ban the app over national-security concerns, setting the stage for years of negotiations and legal pressure [6][18]. In 2024, Congress passed a law requiring ByteDance to divest TikTok’s U.S. operations or face a nationwide ban; multiple reports say the app briefly went dark around the January 2025 deadline before President Trump used executive action to keep it operating while his administration pursued a deal [1][18][6].
