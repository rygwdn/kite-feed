Scientists propose supercritical fluids drove deep Mariana volcanic eruptions

Category: Science

Researchers propose that supercritical water in magma could explain how volcanic deposits formed more than 6,000 meters down the Mariana Trench wall, building on the discovery of unusually deep pyroclastic rocks. At those pressures, water behaves neither like an ordinary liquid nor a gas and expands far less than steam. The team says this may have enabled magma to fragment without the force of a land eruption [9][6][1]. The mechanism remains uncertain: researchers have not found the vents or directly observed an eruption. Rock samples contain “double breccia,” suggesting two closely spaced episodes of fracturing — one possibly linked to supercritical fluid and another to its conversion to steam at the seafloor [9]. The team plans further analysis of the volcanic material and aims to explore farther along the plate boundary. Jason/Medea cannot descend below 6,500 meters, short of the Challenger Deep floor at about 11,000 meters [9][5].

Primary Source: View original article

What would explosive volcanism involving a supercritical fluid look like? How would that work? We’re at the limits of our intuition as geologists.

— Robert Stern, geoscientist at the University of Texas at Dallas (Physics World)

Did you know: The Shinkai Seep Field visited by the expedition is described as the deepest known hydrothermal vent field, where methane produced by seawater reacting with mantle rock supports life without sunlight [9].

Key Points

  • Flow route: The hot pyroclastic material moved downhill along the seabed rather than drifting through the water like ash, and likely did not travel far [1][9].
  • Seep field: The team recovered fluid-sampling devices that Japanese researchers had left at the site in 2016 [9][3].
  • Vent search: Because the flows moved downhill but did not travel far, researchers think the source may lie upslope of the deposits, on a ridge or the trench-facing slope [1].

Perspectives

Robert Stern, University of Texas at Dallas geoscientist and study leader: He called for investment in deep-sea research and technology to better understand supercritical fluids, which are also relevant to underground carbon capture and storage.

Sources: Physics World

David Pyle, volcanologist at the University of Oxford: He called the work a strong example of observational geoscience and suggested researchers investigate whether thermal shock or stresses from bubbles caused the rocks to fracture.

Sources: Physics World

Eoghan Reeves, aqueous geochemistry researcher at the University of Bergen: He said exceptionally deep, hotter vents could dissolve more metals and generate hydrogen that microbes use, though researchers have not found such vents at the site.

Sources: Physics World

Technical Details

  • Volcanic glass: Glass in the recovered rocks erodes quickly in seawater, suggesting the eruptions were geologically recent, although researchers are still dating the samples [4].
  • Deposit texture: The flows had lobed edges where they stopped, and the rock cracked into roughly rectangular blocks a few feet across as it cooled [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Forearc extension: Rock ages suggest the eastern segment is stretching mechanically while the western segment is accumulating magma; further sampling and radiometric dating are needed to test this interpretation [5].
  • Near-trench volcanism: The deposits lie about 100 kilometers from the trench, closer than typical volcanic arcs; a more steeply dipping Pacific plate could help explain that proximity [6].
  • Plate-motion implications: Researchers say stretching of the forearc means the subduction zone must widen, a change that could affect plate motions in the western Pacific [5].

Q&A

Q: Could eruptions still be happening there?

A: Researchers have proposed placing seismometers nearby to detect small earthquakes and help determine whether eruptions are continuing [1].

Q: Why was volcanism in this location unexpected?

A: The deposits are in a forearc where the mantle is generally considered too cool to melt, and the Challenger Deep segment lacks the organized volcanic arc found elsewhere along the Mariana Trench [4].
