Iran seeks to jam Starlink as protests continue

Category: World

Iran has imposed a near-total internet shutdown during nationwide protests, with rights groups reporting hundreds killed and thousands detained amid an ongoing crackdown [1][2][3]. Several residents and monitors say a limited number of people have still managed to get online via SpaceX’s Starlink satellite service, which Iran bans, offering a narrow channel to communicate and share information with the outside world [2][3][5][6][7][8]. Australia’s ABC reports Iran is using “military-grade” jamming aimed at disrupting Starlink signals as the blackout stretches into a fifth day [1].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: The Guardian reports that even during widespread blackouts, a small number of Iranians still manage to send images and videos out via improvised, often risky technical workarounds [4].

Key Points

  • Smuggled connections: The Guardian describes an informal network of smuggled devices and improvised setups that some Iranians use to send photos, videos and messages out of the country despite the blackout [4].
  • Monitoring signal: NetBlocks founder Alp Toker said he heard Starlink still works in some areas, though access appears reduced, suggesting uneven connectivity across locations [5].
  • Sharing evidence: The Telegram channel Vahid Online posted images and video from places including Kahrizak and funeral gatherings, illustrating how small pockets of connectivity can still carry protest information outward [4].
  • How shutdown works: Reuters-sourced reports note Iran’s regular internet relies on fibre-optic cables and mobile towers, which authorities can restrict, while satellite links can bypass those choke points [6][2].

Perspectives

Iranian authorities: Authorities have moved to sharply restrict communications during the protests, including shutting down internet access and, according to ABC, using jamming intended to disrupt Starlink connections.

Sources: ABC News (Australia)

People inside Iran using Starlink: Three people inside Iran told Reuters that some Starlink access continues despite the communications blackout, allowing limited connectivity even where normal service is unavailable.

Sources: Al-Monitor (Reuters)

Internet monitoring community: NetBlocks’ founder said reports indicate Starlink remains available in parts of Iran but appears degraded, pointing to partial effectiveness of disruption measures.

Sources: The Hindu

Human rights groups and activists: Human rights groups cited by ABC estimate more than 500 deaths and over 10,600 arrests during the protests, and activists continue trying to document events despite the blackout.

Sources: ABC News (Australia)

Timeline

  • Thursday afternoon: Internet shut off for most of Iran in severe blackout [4].
  • Monday: Vahid Online posted images and videos shared from inside Iran [4].

Technical Details

  • Terrestrial internet shutdowns: Sources describe Iran’s regular connectivity as routed through fibre-optic networks and mobile towers, which authorities can disable or throttle to enforce a blackout [2][6].
  • Satellite internet bypass: Starlink connects users directly via satellites rather than local cables and towers, so it can keep some users online even when terrestrial networks go dark [6][2][common].
  • Signal jamming: ABC reports Iran is using “military-grade” jamming to disrupt Starlink, a technique that interferes with radio signals between user terminals and satellites [1][common].

Industry Impact

  • Digital rights and security: The reporting underscores how shutdowns and countermeasures shape the tools activists rely on—ranging from satellite links to an underground supply chain of devices—raising demand for secure communications and censorship-circumvention know-how [4][6].
  • Satellite communications sector: Claims of targeted jamming add real-world pressure for satellite providers and terminal makers to harden systems against interference, a capability that can become a competitive differentiator in high-risk environments [1][common].
  • Telecom infrastructure control: By focusing restrictions on fibre and mobile networks, authorities can quickly suppress broad connectivity while leaving only small, harder-to-control satellite pockets—reshaping how information flows during crises [6][2].

Historical Background

Iran has repeatedly imposed internet restrictions during periods of unrest, using control of domestic telecom infrastructure to limit online organising and the flow of images and reporting abroad [4][6]. In recent years, Starlink has increasingly appeared in geopolitical flashpoints as a way to maintain connectivity when governments cut or constrain terrestrial networks [2][3].

Q&A

Q: What practical barriers limit Starlink’s use inside Iran even when it works?

A: Sources describe Starlink as banned by Iranian authorities, and The Guardian reports reliance on smuggled tech, both of which can constrain access and increase personal risk for users [7][4].

Q: Who is documenting and distributing protest material when the internet is cut?

A: The Guardian points to channels such as Vahid Online, which has shared photos and videos from inside Iran during the blackout [4].

Q: How do observers verify whether connectivity is reduced or disrupted?

A: The Hindu cites NetBlocks founder Alp Toker discussing reports of reduced Starlink access, reflecting how monitoring groups rely on user reports and network measurements to assess availability [5].
