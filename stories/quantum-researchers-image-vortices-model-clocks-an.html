Quantum researchers image vortices, model clocks and control nuclear spin

Category: Science

Three separate quantum-physics studies report ways to examine microscopic behavior: a University of Cambridge team directly imaged tangled vortices dissipating in an ultracold gas, researchers led by Kyushu University proposed using trapped-ion clocks to detect quantum effects on time, and Delft University of Technology scientists controlled the nuclear spin of a single atom [1][2][3]. The studies cover an observed fluid process, a theoretical clock method and a single-atom experiment. The Cambridge experiment matched predictions for vortex decay, while the clock proposal still needs laboratory testing [1][2]. The Delft team demonstrated nuclear-spin control but said the mechanism that allowed electrical signals to affect nuclear spin remains unexplained [3].

Primary Source: View original article

We are also interested in exploring whether atomic clocks could eventually be used to probe the quantum realm of gravity, the other fundamental question in physics.

— Joshua Foo, associate professor at Kyushu University’s Institute for Advanced Studies (SciTechDaily)

Key Points

  • Decay pattern: Denser vortex tangles decayed faster as neighboring vortices interacted; later, decay slowed as the remaining vortices were lost at the trap walls [1].
  • Clock sensitivity: The trapped-ion method for controlling clock motion could increase sensitivity to the predicted effect by 100 to 1,000 times, the researchers said [2].
  • Nuclear-spin memory: Nuclear spin is better shielded from outside disturbances than electron spin, making it a candidate for preserving quantum information [3].

Technical Details

  • Vortex-density measurement: The Cambridge team repeatedly imaged thin slices of gas to estimate total vortex-thread length per unit volume, allowing it to compare the observed decay with Vinen turbulence predictions [1].
  • Trapped-ion clock proposal: In the proposed test, an ion's motion would be placed in a quantum superposition; the researchers predict that its motion could become entangled with its internal energy, leaving a detectable change in quantum properties [2].
  • Single-atom resonance: The Delft experiment applied separate signals to the electron and nucleus, enabling the team to manipulate nuclear spin independently of electron spin despite the nucleus's weaker magnetic response [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Cross-fluid comparison: After accounting for particle-mass differences, the vortex-decay rate closely matched measurements in superfluid helium despite the fluids’ different microscopic properties; it also did not depend on interaction strength [1].
  • Future direction: If tested successfully, trapped-ion clocks could help investigate the quantum behavior of time and possibly probe quantum gravity [2].
