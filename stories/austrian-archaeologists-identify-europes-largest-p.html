Austrian archaeologists identify Europe’s largest prehistoric iron cache

Category: Science

Archaeologists have identified at least 500 double-pointed iron bars recovered from gravel near the Danube at Deinham in Hartkirchen, Austria, as Europe’s largest known find of prehistoric iron bars. The objects weigh about one metric ton and date from roughly 300 to 100 B.C., during the La Tène period [1][2][4][5]. Workers found the material during gravel extraction in 2019 and reported it to Austria’s monument authorities. Researchers say the bars were semi-finished products for transport and later reworking; they suspect the cargo may have been lost in a shipwreck, although its origin, destination and intended use remain unknown [1][3][4][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: The recovered iron could have produced approximately 8,000 nails rather than only weapons [3].

Key Points

  • Bar design: The bars average nearly 30 centimeters long and have a uniform bipyramidal form, with both ends hammered to points [3][4].
  • Recovery process: The initial delivery included a box containing 200 bars, prompting systematic searches of the gravel heaps for additional material [4].

Perspectives

Peter Trebsche: The University of Innsbruck archaeologist characterized the discovery as a “minor miracle” [1].

Sources: ScienceAlert

Austrian monument authorities: The Bundesdenkmalamt investigated after workers and the gravel plant reported the unusual objects instead of sending them for scrap; archaeological divers later examined the find site [4][6].

Sources: ORF

Study researchers: Peter Trebsche and Marion Berranger collaborated on the analysis, with Berranger based at France’s Archaeomaterials Research Institute [3].

Sources: Phys.org

Technical Details

  • Bipyramidal form: Both ends were hammered into points, a design that allowed buyers or users to assess the iron’s quality visually [4].
  • Dating method: The Université de Technologie de Belfort-Montbéliard conducted radiocarbon analysis [4][5].

Scientific Significance

  • Iron Age commerce: Because iron corrodes readily, late Iron Age trade routes are difficult to reconstruct. The concentrated find offers rare material evidence of the scale of iron movement and exchange during the period [7].
