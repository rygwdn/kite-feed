British Columbia sues OpenAI over Tumbler Ridge shooting

Category: World

British Columbia filed a lawsuit in San Francisco federal court against OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman, alleging the company failed to alert law enforcement after its safety team flagged ChatGPT activity linked to the February 10 shooting in Tumbler Ridge. The province says the attack could have been prevented if OpenAI had warned police and is seeking damages for recovery costs, as well as court-ordered changes to how the company handles conversations that may signal violent threats [1][6][10]. The attack killed eight people, including six children. The 18-year-old shooter, Jesse Van Rootselaar, died from a self-inflicted gunshot wound, bringing the death toll to nine [10][1][6][27]. British Columbia alleges that OpenAI deactivated an account after internal safety reviewers identified violent content but did not contact authorities; the shooter then continued using a second account [18][29]. The province also says OpenAI rejected requests to disclose the conversations. The Guardian reported that Altman apologized but did not implement promised reforms [27][33][1].

Key Points

  • Separate claims: The provincial action follows more than 30 lawsuits filed in California by victims’ families, educators and students, while OpenAI has sought dismissal of the family cases [8][24].
  • Alleged chatbot role: The lawsuit says ChatGPT reinforced the shooter’s violent ideation [13].
  • Trial request: B.C. is asking for a jury trial [38].
  • Plaintiffs: The action was brought by B.C.’s attorney general and a local school board [29].
  • Product-liability claim: The province accuses OpenAI of negligence and describes the chatbot as a dangerous product in the lawsuit [18][38].

Technical Details

  • Account circumvention: Deactivation did not prevent continued access because the user allegedly created a second account, an enforcement gap central to the province’s claims [29][21].
  • Escalation workflow: The alleged process moved from automated detection to human review, where reviewers reportedly concluded there was a specific, credible risk of harm and recommended notifying police; management instead deactivated the account [29][18].
  • Reporting threshold: OpenAI said the flagged material did not meet its internal standard for notifying law enforcement, while the lawsuit claims reviewers had already identified a credible, specific risk [24][29].
  • Account escalation: According to the lawsuit, an automated monitoring system sent the account for human review; reviewers recommended police notification, but management instead deactivated it [18][29].
