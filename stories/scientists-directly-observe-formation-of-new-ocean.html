Scientists directly observe formation of new ocean crust

Category: Science

A French-led geophysics team directly recorded seafloor spreading at the Southeast Indian Ridge, where the Australian and Antarctic tectonic plates move apart beneath the Indian Ocean [1][3][5][7][10][12]. Instruments about 2,000 meters underwater near the Amsterdam–Saint Paul region recorded earthquake activity, seafloor deformation and magma movement during an April 26, 2024, event, according to accounts of the Nature study published July 8, 2026 [1][10][5]. Measurements showed the abyssal floor opened roughly 2 to 4 meters over a few days, while about 160 million cubic meters of lava or magma emerged to form new oceanic crust [7][10]. SciMex said the seafloor also sank 4 meters as magma stored beneath the ridge drained out, and Ars Technica reported that most spreading occurred in a short window, with some key changes showing no obvious seismic signals [1][12]. Researchers said the event provided a rare direct record of a process long inferred from plate tectonics: stress builds for decades at mid-ocean ridges, then releases in abrupt spreading episodes rather than only through steady centimeter-scale motion [1][5][11].

Primary Source: View original article

We did not dream of capturing such a massive event, and were hoping to at least measure the steady stretching of the ridge (maybe a few centimeters) that allows stresses to build up between events, like a loaded spring

— Jean-Yves Royer (ScienceAlert)

Key Points

  • Instrument array: The team used more than 20 measurement stations across a 100-kilometer section of the Indian Ocean ridge, with the observatory installed only about two months before the event [7][12].
  • Remote site: Ars Technica placed the study area roughly halfway between Australia and Madagascar, far south of India, near the Amsterdam–Saint Paul Plateau [12].
  • Global setting: Mid-ocean ridges extend about 65,000 kilometers across the seafloor, and ridge volcanism and spreading formed more than two-thirds of Earth’s surface [5][11].
  • Publication record: Nature published the findings under the title “Anatomy of a seafloor spreading event captured by in situ seismogeodesy” [6][5].

Perspectives

Scientific American: The rare eruption in the Indian Ocean gave researchers one of the clearest views yet of a seafloor spreading event.

Sources: Scientific American

Ars Technica: John Timmer focused on a gap in plate-tectonics research, writing that scientists knew more about the crust produced at spreading sites than about how its features form.

Sources: Ars Technica

Le Monde: The newspaper emphasized that the measurements came from instruments placed nearly 2,000 meters deep off Amsterdam Island and linked the direct observation to the April 26, 2024, tectonic event.

Sources: Le Monde

Timeline

  • February 2024: Researchers installed the underwater observatory on the Southeast Indian Ridge [11].
  • 26 April 2024: Instruments tracked the ridge expansion event near Amsterdam Island [10].
  • 8 July 2026: Nature published the study describing in situ seismogeodesy observations [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty and confidence: Instruments were already on the seafloor during the event, giving researchers direct measurements of a rare deep-ocean tectonic episode at one ridge segment rather than repeated observations across different spreading environments [1][12].
  • Limitations and future work: The event’s rarity limits immediate generalization. ScienceAlert quoted Royer describing it as a once-in-40-year event, and future records at other ridges would be needed to compare how spreading varies by tectonic setting [5][common].
