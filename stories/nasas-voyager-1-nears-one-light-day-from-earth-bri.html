NASA's Voyager 1 nears one light-day from Earth, bringing two-day round-trip signal delay

Category: Science

NASA’s Voyager 1 is expected to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth on Nov. 18, nearly 50 years after its launch. It has traveled farther than any other human-made object. At about 25.9 billion kilometers (16.1 billion miles), the spacecraft will be so distant that a radio command takes roughly 24 hours to arrive and a reply another 24 hours to return [1][2][8]. Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012 and still sends measurements from beyond the Sun’s protective bubble. Controllers must wait about two days to learn whether a command worked, complicating responses to faults [2][11]. The aging probe has only two science instruments operating, and its power supply continues to decline. Contact briefly dropped in October and resumed on Oct. 20 after backup thrusters activated, according to mission data [2][11]. NASA invited the public to flash “VGR” in Morse code after sunset on Nov. 17, ahead of the expected crossing [6][11].

Primary Source: View original article

The one-light-day distance is not a noteworthy location other than it is really far away

— Adam Szabo, principal investigator for the Voyager magnetometers (Scientific American)

Did you know: Voyager 2, despite launching 16 days before Voyager 1, is not expected to reach one light-day from Earth until 2035 because it followed a different trajectory [2][3].

Key Points

  • Remaining instruments: Voyager 1’s magnetometer and Plasma Wave Subsystem remain active; NASA shut down its Low-Energy Charged Particles instrument in April 2026 to conserve power [11][9].
  • Power trade-offs: The September 2026 power-saving change replaced three devices with lower-power alternatives and was intended to preserve the remaining science instruments [11].
  • Weak radio signal: Voyager 1’s transmitter uses just 23 watts, while NASA relies on Deep Space Network antennas as large as 70 meters to receive its faint signal [13].
  • Data from space: The probes report magnetic-field strength and direction, surrounding pressure, dust and galactic cosmic rays from a region no other spacecraft has explored [8].

Perspectives

Adam Szabo, principal investigator for the Voyager magnetometers, said the milestone illustrates the technical difficulty of operating a spacecraft in the outer heliosphere and interstellar medium.

Sources: Scientific American

Jamie S. Rankin, a Princeton University researcher and deputy project scientist for Voyager, said Voyager 1 may be nearing the point beyond which the heliosphere no longer significantly affects the surrounding interstellar medium. The spacecraft’s measurements can help investigate that question, Rankin said.

Sources: Digi24

Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, said the team must expect unusual behavior from the spacecraft because of its age. He described engineers as relieved after communications resumed.

Sources: Forskning.no, Digi24

Timeline

  • 5 September 1977: Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral, Florida [8][11]
  • 1979-1980: The spacecraft made close flybys of Jupiter and Saturn [8][11]
  • August 2012: Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space [11][12]
  • 20 October 2026: Communications resumed after missed sessions; backup thrusters had activated [11]
  • 18 November 2026: NASA expects Voyager 1 to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth [1][2]

Technical Details

  • Radioisotope power: Radioisotope thermoelectric generators convert heat from plutonium decay into electricity; their output falls by about four watts per year, forcing NASA to shut down equipment and reduce electrical demand [11][14].
  • Command-and-response delay: The roughly two-day round trip covers signal travel alone; preparing commands and processing returned data add time [11].

Historical Background

Voyager 1 launched during a rare alignment of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, which occurs about once every 176 years and made an outer-planets mission possible. The initial plan focused on Jupiter and Saturn; after those flybys, Voyager 1 continued outward [4].

Q&A

Q: How much longer might Voyager 1 remain in contact with Earth?

A: NASA hopes Voyager 1 will keep communicating into the early 2030s, before its power supply falls below the level needed to operate its systems [6].
