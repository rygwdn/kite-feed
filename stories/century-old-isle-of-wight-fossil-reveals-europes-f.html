Century-old Isle of Wight fossil reveals Europe's first megaraptoran

Category: Science

Researchers have identified a new predatory dinosaur, Invictibellatrix amberina, from a skull fragment found on the Isle of Wight, making it the first known megaraptoran found in Europe. The animal lived about 120 million years ago and was likely 3 to 4 metres long, with powerful forelimbs and curved claws estimated at 10 to 20 centimetres [4][6][9]. The Natural History Museum acquired the fossil in 1924, but it remained undescribed until a team led by University College London examined it. The discovery supports a proposed route in which megaraptorans spread from Asia through Europe to Gondwana, rather than reaching the southern continents by island-hopping. The researchers published the study in Royal Society Open Science on October 14, 2026 [6][2][4].

Primary Source: View original article

It was not the apex predator of its time, but for humans this dinosaur would have been more dangerous than a Tyrannosaurus rex. We would have been perfectly sized prey, about a third of their body weight. They definitely would have chased us. A Tyrannosaurus rex would not have bothered, as we would have been too small.

— Cassius Morrison (Sci.News)

Did you know: The fossil was part of a collection of more than 1,000 specimens discovered on the Isle of Wight by amateur paleontologist Reginald Walter Tooley [4][9].

Key Points

  • Size evolution: The researchers’ analysis suggests megaraptorans were widespread early in the Cretaceous and that some descendants later grew to 9 to 10 metres in length [4][6].
  • Northern decline: Megaraptorans apparently disappeared from northern regions by the Late Cretaceous; the study proposes climate or environmental shifts and competition from other large carnivores as possible causes [4][2].
  • Skull evidence: The skull fragment has an oval opening for the spinal cord and a distinctive hourglass-shaped crest, features used to distinguish it from other theropods [4][1].
  • Name meaning: The researchers said Invictibellatrix means “unconquered female warrior,” while amberina refers to amber found in the fossil-bearing rocks [9].

Perspectives

Paul Barrett, Natural History Museum paleontologist: The Isle of Wight continues to yield new dinosaur species despite more than 200 years of collecting, Barrett said. Carnivorous dinosaur fossils have been particularly rare and difficult to find.

Scientific Significance

  • Megaraptorans differed from tyrannosaurs in their relatively narrow skulls and reliance on powerful forelimbs and curved claws, rather than jaws, to seize prey [6][4].
  • Limits of reconstruction: The identification rests on a braincase fragment, while estimates of body length, height, feathers and hunting behaviour draw on comparisons with related species rather than a complete skeleton [6][9].

Historical Background

The best-known megaraptoran fossils come from South America and Australia, and the earliest Southern Hemisphere fossils are younger than the Isle of Wight animal [6].

Q&A

Q: What did the researchers infer about the dinosaur’s likely prey?

A: They think it hunted small dinosaurs, lizards and mammals, based on comparisons with its closest relatives [4][6].
