Rubin observatory team finds possible dwarf planet in archived images

Category: Science

A team led by University of Washington astronomer Mario Juric has identified a possible dwarf planet in early data from Chile’s Vera C. Rubin Observatory and estimates it is 1,000 to 2,000 miles across [16]. The object, provisionally designated 2026 ED-138, lies about 46 astronomical units from Earth; the team also found it in older observations, including photographic plates dating to the 1930s [16]. The estimated size would make it far larger than the asteroids the survey team usually detects, but astronomers have not established that it is round, a requirement for dwarf-planet status [16]. Because it is too distant for researchers to image its shape directly, they plan to study how it dims stars as it passes in front of them; one such event may occur within days, Juric said [16]. He expects confirmation to take about two years [16].

We went back 11 years. We found it in data that we just overlooked for over the past decade

— Mario Juric, University of Washington professor of astronomy (KING 5)

Key Points

  • Known candidates: Astronomers recognize five dwarf planets, while at least a dozen additional candidates lack confirmed evidence that they are round [16].
  • Survey potential: Rubin's long-term survey has only just begun; Juric anticipates millions of new asteroid detections and potentially dozens of interstellar comets over the next decade [16].

Perspectives

Mario Juric and the Rubin Observatory solar system team: Juric said the provisional discovery reflects the work of the broader observatory team and that naming the object would involve the wider project, not just the scientists who identified it [16].

Timeline

  • 1930s: Photographic plates from the 1930s recorded the object, Juric said [16].
  • 11 years before discovery: Researchers later found the object in data collected 11 years before its identification in 2026 [16].
  • 2026: The Rubin team identified the candidate in early observatory data [16].

Q&A

Q: What happens if the object is confirmed as a dwarf planet?

A: The Rubin team plans to propose a name to the International Astronomical Union, which sets naming rules for outer solar system objects [16].

Q: Why was it missed for so long?

A: Juric said it looks like an ordinary star against the crowded star field of the Milky Way, making it difficult to distinguish in older images [16].
