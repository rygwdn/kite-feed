UN Security Council set to meet on Somaliland

Category: World

Israel has formally recognised Somaliland as an independent and sovereign state, making it the first country to do so in more than 30 years [3][1]. The move drew swift objections from Somalia and a wide range of regional and international actors who say Somaliland remains part of Somalia and warn the decision could destabilise the Horn of Africa and the Red Sea corridor [1][2][5]. In response, the UN Security Council is expected to convene an emergency meeting to discuss the implications [2][5]. Somaliland’s president, Abdirahman Mohamed Abdullahi, said Somaliland intends to join the Abraham Accords, signalling a push for broader diplomatic engagement despite the backlash [6][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Somaliland has maintained its own currency and governing institutions despite limited formal recognition abroad [9].

Key Points

  • African blocs: Africa’s regional governance bodies rejected Israel’s move and reiterated support for Somalia’s territorial integrity, underscoring how strongly the African Union-aligned system resists changes to borders without broad consensus [3].
  • EU stance: The European Union urged respect for Somalia’s sovereignty, aligning with a wider chorus of governments and blocs pressing for de-escalation and diplomatic restraint [4][8][10].
  • OIC statement: Pakistan joined 20 other countries and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation in a joint rejection that called Israel’s recognition a violation of international law and warned of repercussions for regional peace and security [6].
  • Palestinian linkage: Pakistan’s Foreign Office also said it rejects any steps aimed at forced displacement of Palestinians, noting the Palestinian foreign ministry referenced Somaliland in the context of potential deportation discussions [7].
  • Why unrecognised: Somaliland has operated with its own government and currency yet has remained largely unrecognised internationally for decades, reflecting states’ reluctance to encourage secessionist precedents [9].

Technical Details

  • Recognition: In diplomacy, formal recognition is a state’s declaration that it accepts an entity as a sovereign state, often enabling official ties such as embassies and treaties [common].
  • De facto vs de jure status: Somaliland has operated with de facto self-rule since 1991, but most countries have withheld de jure recognition, leaving its international legal status contested [6][9].
  • Abraham Accords: Somaliland’s president said the territory intends to join the Abraham Accords, a set of normalisation agreements between Israel and several Arab states, as part of its bid for wider engagement [6][7].

Historical Background

Somaliland declared itself independent in 1991 as Somalia descended into civil war, and it has maintained de facto autonomy and comparatively stable governance since then, even as formal international recognition has largely not followed [6][9]. Many governments and regional bodies have traditionally prioritised Somalia’s territorial integrity, partly to avoid setting precedents for secession elsewhere on the continent [3][common].
