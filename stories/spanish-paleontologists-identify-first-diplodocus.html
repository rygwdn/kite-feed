Spanish paleontologists identify first Diplodocus fossils outside North America

Category: Science

Paleontologists have identified the first confirmed Diplodocus remains outside North America at the La Tejería site near El Castellar, in Spain’s Teruel province. The material includes 14 tail vertebrae and several chevron bones from an animal that lived about 150 million years ago during the Late Jurassic. Researchers estimate the individual measured about 25 meters long and say the remains are among the most complete diplodocid skeletons found in Europe [2][3][7][8][5]. Previously, confirmed Diplodocus remains had been found only in the Morrison Formation of the western United States. Anatomical comparisons and evolutionary analyses placed the Spanish specimen within the genus and closely related to Diplodocus hallorum. Researchers said its presence in Iberia indicates dinosaurs may have moved between North America and Europe across temporary land bridges as the Atlantic opened [3][9][2].

As we looked more closely at the anatomy, we began to identify a combination of features characteristic of Diplodocus species. Then, as we carried out different evolutionary analyses, they kept pointing in the same direction.

Key Points

  • Research team: The study was conducted by the Fundación Conjunto Paleontológico de Teruel-Dinópolis [9].
  • Body plan: Diplodocus was characterized by a small head, long neck and whip-like tail [18][11].

Timeline

  • 1877: First Diplodocus fossils were identified in Wyoming, United States [10][11].
  • 2008: A village resident found fossil bones in eastern Spain [16].
  • 15 September 2026: Researchers published the Spanish specimen’s identification in a paleontology journal [2][7].

Technical Details

  • Tail anatomy: The specimen’s vertebrae have pneumatic cavities, elongated centra and deep longitudinal grooves that aided anatomical identification [9][20].
  • Chevron morphology: Several bifurcated chevrons—bones attached beneath tail vertebrae—provided additional characters for assigning the specimen to Diplodocus [20][17].
  • Phylogenetic testing: Maximum-parsimony and Bayesian analyses independently placed the specimen within Diplodocus [9].

Scientific Significance

  • Regional significance: The find adds Diplodocus to Jurassic sauropods documented around Teruel [7][8].
  • Evidence limits: The discovery consists mainly of tail elements rather than a fully articulated skeleton, so the genus assignment rests on comparative anatomy and phylogenetic analysis of the preserved material [3][9].
