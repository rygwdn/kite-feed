Three men charged in alleged $129 million AI fraud targeting companies

Category: AI

Three men have been charged in Manhattan with allegedly using artificial intelligence to impersonate company executives and lawyers and persuade employees to transfer more than $129 million to accounts controlled by the group and co-conspirators [2][3][16]. Prosecutors say the alleged scheme ran from July 2024 to February 2026 and targeted thousands of companies worldwide. The defendants, Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit, were arrested in Spain and the United Kingdom between June and September 2026. U.S. extradition requests are pending [2][11][14]. The indictment describes a two-stage impersonation: someone posing as a CEO asked accounting staff to handle a confidential transaction, then a supposed lawyer reinforced the request and supplied wire instructions. The alleged losses include $11.5 million from a British financial technology company, $5.2 million from a Florida pet-food company and $4.8 million from a Missouri hospital system [11][5]. Prosecutors say the group moved funds through bank accounts in several countries, converted them into cryptocurrency and distributed the proceeds [1][2]. The defendants face charges including wire fraud, aggravated identity theft and money laundering; prosecutors say the counts carry a combined maximum sentence of 104 years in prison if they are convicted [11][14]. Sheetrit’s lawyers said he has no connection to the case and maintains his innocence. Lawyers for Soall and Sheetrit had not responded to the Associated Press when it reported the indictment, and it was unclear whether Hazan had an attorney [11][3].

The alleged scheme orchestrated by Soall, Hazan, and Sheetrit demonstrates how the use of advanced technology can be exploited to commit large scale fraud

Key Points

  • Targeting method: Prosecutors say the operation often began with an email sent through an electronic-signature service, followed by a nondisclosure agreement intended to keep accounting employees from consulting colleagues [6][11].
  • Scripted impersonation: Jacob Soall allegedly used a handwritten script titled “Lawyer Call” while posing as British attorneys, and prosecutors said the scheme also involved impersonating lawyers at some of the world’s 50 largest law firms [3][11].
  • International transfers: The alleged transfer destinations included accounts in the United States, Spain, Portugal, Greece, the United Arab Emirates, Singapore, Austria, Slovakia and China [1][14].
  • Prior conviction: Sharon Hazan was convicted in the Southern District of New York in 2008 of conspiracy to commit bank fraud, according to the U.S. Attorney’s Office [2][3].
  • Alleged laundering claims: Prosecutors say Hazan boasted he had laundered more than $400 million in the previous three years and called himself “number one all over the world” [2][11].

Perspectives

Jamie McDonald, U.S. attorney for the Southern District of New York: McDonald warned businesses and individuals to consider how to protect themselves from crimes enabled by AI, which the Justice Department referred to as “super intelligence” technology [2][15].

Sources: NBC News

James C. Barnacle Jr., FBI assistant director in charge of the New York field office: Barnacle said the FBI is adapting its capabilities to identify, investigate and disrupt criminals using emerging technology [3][7].

Guy Sheetrit’s lawyers, Sharon Nahari and Shay Shorer: They argued that Sheetrit’s cybersecurity expertise and advisory work do not establish involvement, and said he is entitled to the presumption of innocence [11].

Sources: CyberScoop

Timeline

  • July 2024: Prosecutors say the alleged scheme began, targeting companies worldwide [2].
  • 17 June 2026: Spanish authorities arrested Jacob Soall, according to court records [11].
  • 5 August 2026: Spanish authorities arrested Sharon Hazan, prosecutors said [11].
  • 7 September 2026: British authorities arrested Guy Sheetrit, according to prosecutors [11].
  • 5 November 2026: Manhattan prosecutors announced the charges and sought extradition [14][15].

Industry Impact

  • Corporate finance controls: The indictment says perpetrators targeted accounting employees authorized to initiate large transfers [11][6].

Historical Background

The alleged $129 million in proceeds exceeds the $25.6 million that engineering firm Arup lost in a 2024 deepfake video-call fraud, cited as an earlier AI-enabled business scam [14]. The FBI recorded just over $30 million in AI-linked business email compromise losses for all of 2025, but that tally included only cases in which victims recognized that AI had been used [14].

Action Items

  • Report suspected targeting to the FBI: The FBI asked companies that believe they were victims to submit information through its online tips page [16][14].
