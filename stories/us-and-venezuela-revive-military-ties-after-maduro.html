US and Venezuela revive military ties after Maduro’s ouster

Category: World

U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a military cooperation agreement with Venezuela during an unannounced visit to Caracas on Nov. 18, saying it would restore joint training, intelligence sharing and cooperation on defense equipment and capabilities [4][1][2]. Hegseth met acting President Delcy Rodríguez and signed the accord with Venezuelan Defense Minister Gustavo González López [1]. Neither government disclosed a timetable, resource commitments or specific mechanisms for implementing the agreement [12][11]. U.S. forces captured President Nicolás Maduro on Jan. 3. Washington has since expanded its dealings with Rodríguez’s government, including in the oil sector; an August arrangement gave a U.S.-linked producer access to 17 oilfields holding about one-fifth of Venezuela’s reserves [9][21]. Hegseth said the new military relationship would help counter drug-trafficking groups and foreign threats, while Rodríguez said it should address shared threats and strengthen Venezuela’s armed forces [4][1]. The agreement’s terms and scope remain unclear. Rodríguez’s court-authorized term expired in July, no date has been set for a presidential election, and hundreds of political prisoners remain in detention [11][2]. González López said cooperation must respect Venezuela’s sovereignty and preserve its armed forces’ command structure [1].

Venezuela has gone from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner.

Key Points

  • Sovereignty limits: Venezuelan Defense Minister Gustavo González López said military cooperation must adhere strictly to Venezuela’s constitution [1].
  • Oil terms: The United States secured a 35% stake in North American Blue Energy Partners’ parent, a guaranteed share of output and the right of first refusal on the remainder [21].
  • Opposition return: Opposition leader María Corina Machado returned to Venezuela in October after months in exile [18].
  • Regional scrutiny: At least 240 people have been killed in U.S. attacks on suspected drug boats since September 2025, while U.S. officials have publicly released little evidence that targeted vessels carried drugs [4][10].

Perspectives

Luz Mely Reyes, Venezuelan journalist: Reyes argued that Hegseth’s presence in Caracas signaled Washington’s direct influence over Venezuela’s decisions and operational sovereignty [13].

Venezuelan pro-democracy opposition: Opposition figures objected to Washington’s embrace of Rodríguez while much of the authoritarian state apparatus remained in place [2].

Timeline

  • 3 January 2026: U.S. forces captured Nicolás Maduro in a military operation [12][4]
  • August 2026: Venezuela and the United States reached an oil-sector agreement [14][21]
  • 18 November 2026: Hegseth announced restored military cooperation during his Caracas visit [4][2]

Industry Impact

  • Venezuelan oil production: North American Blue Energy Partners said it aims to raise output above 250,000 barrels per day by year-end, from 224,000 barrels per day in mid-October, with 10 well clusters planned for service [14][21].

Historical Background

Venezuelan President Hugo Chávez ended military cooperation with Washington in 2005, and Caracas turned to Russia, Iran and China as major arms suppliers [1][12]. Relations deteriorated further in 2019, when Nicolás Maduro cut formal diplomatic ties after the first Trump administration recognized opposition figure Juan Guaidó as interim president [9].

Q&A

Q: Had military cooperation already begun before the agreement?

A: Yes. U.S. equipment and intelligence reportedly supported a June operation against Tren de Aragua leader Héctor Rusthenford Guerrero Flores in Bolívar state and a drone strike on an ELN operations center in Apure; questions remain about those killed [12].
