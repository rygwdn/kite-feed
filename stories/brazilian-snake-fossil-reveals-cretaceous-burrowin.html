Brazilian snake fossil reveals Cretaceous burrowing anatomy

Category: Science

Researchers described Tametara mirim, an exceptionally preserved fossil snake from southeastern Brazil that lived about 75 million to 85 million years ago during the Late Cretaceous and measured about 42 centimeters, or 17 inches, long [4][5][3]. The fossil came from deposits near Presidente Prudente in São Paulo state and preserved a skull and much of the body in three dimensions, providing rare anatomical material from an early snake [3][7]. The skull let researchers reconstruct the animal’s brain anatomy, while skull bone microstructure helped identify a burrowing, or fossorial, lifestyle [4][5]. The finding adds evidence to a long-running debate over whether early snakes were mainly burrowers, swimmers or surface-dwellers; the new study points to ecological diversity among early snakes rather than a single origin story [3][6].

Key Points

  • Sparse fossils: Fewer than 10 early snake skeletons are known from this dinosaur-era interval, limiting previous comparisons [3].
  • Brazilian first: The specimen is described as the first articulated snake fossil ever found in Brazil [3].
  • Ancient habitat: Tametara lived in a lush ecosystem alongside long-necked herbivorous dinosaurs, crocodiles and birds [4][5].
  • Evolutionary age: Scientists think snakes evolved from lizard ancestors and first appeared roughly 170 million years ago in the Jurassic Period [5].

Historical Background

Scientists have debated snake origins for more than a century, but the fossil record has left the issue unresolved because well-preserved Jurassic snake fossils are absent and only a handful of Cretaceous specimens preserve enough anatomy for detailed study [5].
