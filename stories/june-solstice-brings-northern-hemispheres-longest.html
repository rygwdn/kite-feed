June solstice brings Northern Hemisphere's longest day

Category: Science

The June solstice occurred Sunday, June 21, at 08:24 GMT, or 4:24 a.m. ET, marking the start of astronomical summer in the Northern Hemisphere and astronomical winter in the Southern Hemisphere [15][9][29]. North of the equator, it brought the longest day and shortest night of 2026; south of the equator, it brought the opposite seasonal marker [6][7][10][30]. The event occurs because Earth orbits the sun on a tilted axis, causing sunlight and warmth to fall unevenly on the two hemispheres during the year [7][10][31]. At the Northern Hemisphere’s summer solstice, Earth’s northern half leans most directly toward the sun, the sun follows its longest and highest daily arc, and daylight begins decreasing until the December solstice [7][10][30].

Key Points

  • Daylight range: Day length varied widely by latitude: about 15 hours 32 minutes in Romania, 15 hours 36 minutes in Belgrade, about 12 hours 56 minutes in Bangkok and 12 hours 50 minutes to 13 hours 11 minutes across listed Philippine locations [13][26][31][29].
  • Heat timing: The hottest part of summer does not necessarily fall on the solstice because land and oceans take time to absorb and release solar energy, a pattern known as seasonal lag [22].
  • Calendar variation: The solstice can fall on June 20, 21 or 22 because the calendar year does not exactly match Earth’s orbital period; leap years help realign the calendar [15][25][27].
  • Stonehenge gathering: Thousands gathered before dawn at Stonehenge near Salisbury, England, for music, dancing and quiet reflection as the sun rose over the ancient stone circle [28][23].

Perspectives

PAGASA: The Philippine weather and astronomy agency said the June solstice is an astronomical event, not the start of the country’s season, and gave the exact local time as 4:24 p.m. Philippine Standard Time.

Historical Background

People have marked solstices for centuries with festivals and monuments, including Sweden’s midsummer celebrations and Stonehenge, which was designed to align with the sun’s paths at the solstices [10][15]. In parts of Europe after Christianization, midsummer customs became linked with the June 24 feast of John the Baptist, and some bonfire traditions took his name [11].
