Scientists test yeast-based material for Mars housing

Category: Science

Researchers at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology proposed a Mars construction material made from simulated Martian regolith or sand, gelatin and genetically engineered yeast. The team extruded the mixture through a 3D printer in conditions modeled on the planet’s cold, low-pressure environment [1][3][5][6][8]. The study was published Sept. 10 in the journal Chem Circularity [3][6][8]. The material, called Martian living building material, hardens through a process similar to freeze-drying: water in the printed mixture freezes and then turns directly into vapor under low pressure, leaving a light, porous structure [1][3][6][9][10]. Laboratory tests showed the material is as strong as low-grade concrete [1][6][8][4]. The researchers said the approach could reduce the need to launch building materials from Earth, since early Mars settlements would lack industrial infrastructure and payload space would be limited [1][5][8]. The tested structures remain small, about the height of a wine cork, and the proposed material would serve as a structural shell rather than a complete habitat [1][4][8].

Key Points

  • Habitat gaps: A finished Mars home would still require an airtight inner membrane, insulation, radiation shielding and life-support systems before people could live inside it [1].
  • Protein adhesive: The engineered yeast carries adhesive proteins, including proteins modeled on those mussels use to attach to rocks underwater [3][4][8][9][10].
  • Recycling claim: The team said the hardened material can be broken down, recycled and brewed again for new construction [4][7].

Perspectives

Euronews: Euronews linked the Mars biomaterials work to wider efforts to adapt 3D printing for off-Earth habitats, including lunar habitat planning under the Artemis program.

Sources: Euronews

Technical Details

  • Simulated Mars conditions: The printing tests were conducted in a chamber at about -30°C and 600 pascals, mimicking the cold, low-pressure Martian environment [1].
  • Yeast chassis: The team used Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a common model eukaryote that grows readily and has low pathogenicity for humans, to display adhesive proteins [8].

Scientific Significance

  • The study tests a biological binder strategy for in-situ construction, combining locally available granular material with engineered microbes rather than relying on high-temperature processing of minerals [1][8][9].
  • The results come from laboratory and simulated-environment tests, not a field test on Mars, and the material's performance under real Martian conditions remains unverified [1][4][8].
  • The researchers still need to scale up the printing process to produce larger structures and test them under more realistic Mars-like conditions before the approach can be evaluated for practical use [1][4].
