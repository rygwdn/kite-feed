Dinosaur coprolite reveals 66-million-year-old bird feathers

Category: Science

Paleontologists identified exceptionally preserved feathers inside a 66-million-year-old dinosaur coprolite from the Hell Creek Formation in Montana. David DeMar Jr. of the Burke Museum first found the fossil in 2016 [1][2][7]. The dung likely came from a large predatory theropod, possibly a Tyrannosaurus rex or Nanotyrannus, and also contained fish scales and bones of a hesperornithiform, an extinct loon-like diving bird [1][2][8]. The researchers said the feathers probably came from the same aquatic bird and described them as the first hesperornithiform feathers identified [1][8]. Their anatomy suggests modern-looking feathers had evolved in some non-modern bird lineages by the end of the Cretaceous, while some body feathers still retained smaller, fuzzier features associated with earlier dinosaurs and enantiornithine birds [1][10][8]. The study, published in Current Biology, uses the fossil to examine why neornithines, the ancestors of modern birds, survived the asteroid-driven extinction event 66 million years ago while hesperornithiforms and other bird lineages disappeared [8][10]. Jingmai O'Connor said the find indicates that living near water alone cannot explain survival because hesperornithiforms were aquatic yet went extinct [8].

It is, without a doubt, the best feather we've ever found from the age of dinosaurs

— Jingmai O'Connor (NPR)

Did you know: Well-preserved Mesozoic feathers are usually found in amber or fine-grained limestone, not in fossilized dung [9].

Key Points

  • Rare preservation: Researchers said bird fossils are uncommon and fossil feathers from the dinosaur age are rarer still, making the coprolite an unusual source of soft-tissue evidence [1][7].
  • Microscopic detail: The feather was preserved well enough for researchers to see barbs and barbules, the tiny structures that interlock feathers, under a microscope [7].
  • Survival hypothesis: O'Connor said differences in feather insulation may have mattered after the asteroid impact because primitive body feathers may have shielded hesperornithiforms from cold less effectively than modern down [8].

Perspectives

Gregory Wilson Mantilla: The University of Washington paleontologist said the feathers offer rare evidence of avian evolution and predator-prey interactions near the end of the Cretaceous.

Sources: Sci.News

David DeMar Jr.: DeMar said he found a dark, round object while crawling over the Hell Creek badlands and, after turning it over, realized it contained an embedded feather.

Sources: NPR

Jingmai O'Connor: O'Connor said that despite having modern feathers, hesperornithiforms still went extinct, suggesting that feather structure alone was not enough to survive the end-Cretaceous catastrophe.

Sources: NPR

Scientific Significance

  • First direct evidence: The coprolite provides the first known direct evidence of a non-avian dinosaur feeding on a bird, with feathers and bird bones preserved in the same dropping [1][7].
  • Unusual preservation: The find shows fossilized dung can preserve feathers with microscopic detail, expanding the range of fossil types that yield soft-tissue information beyond amber and limestone [9].
  • Methodological advance: Researchers used CT scanning to examine the coprolite's contents without damaging it, a technique that could be applied to other rare fossil droppings [7].
