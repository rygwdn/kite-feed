AI-generated political ads top 200 as Pennsylvania mandates labels

Category: AI

More than 200 AI-generated political ads have circulated during the 2026 U.S. midterm campaign, with spending on them exceeding $100 million, according to the Wesleyan Media Project, which tracks political advertising [6]. Both outside groups and candidates have used the ads. In Texas, an ad distributed by Republican Senate candidate Ken Paxton’s campaign depicts Democratic rival James Talarico rewriting the Bible; Talarico responded with an ad mocking Republican attack ads [6][3]. With the November election still ahead, project co-director Travis Ridout says researchers continue to add ads to the count [6]. Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro signed a law Thursday requiring labels on ads with content significantly modified or generated by AI, including still images, video, audio and text [4]. The law includes exceptions for parody, satire, commentary and criticism, but leaves terms such as “significantly modified” undefined [4]. Federal legislation has not advanced, while 31 states have adopted differing rules for AI-generated political ads [6].

What we don’t know is whether a voter sees that and knows it isn’t real, does it still have an impact on how they think?

— Travis Ridout, co-director of the Wesleyan Media Project (El País)

Key Points

  • Campaign spending: Spending on AI-generated ads remains a small portion of the more than $10 billion estimated for all campaign advertising; outside political groups appear especially often behind AI-generated spots [6].
  • Counting method: Researchers identify AI use manually, so the project’s tally is approximate and excludes unpaid material that unaffiliated individuals make and share on social media [6].
  • State-law gaps: Tennessee’s disclosure law took effect July 1, but two candidates in a congressional primary argued it did not apply to their federal race; neither candidate’s ad displayed an AI notice [6].
  • Unclear effects: In small experiments, most participants recognized deepfake ads, and one test found no apparent effect on trust in political advertising; researchers cautioned that the results are limited [6].

Perspectives

Travis Ridout, co-director of the Wesleyan Media Project, said campaigns would welcome a national rule clarifying what they can and cannot do [6].

Industry Impact

  • Political advertising production: Researchers say AI can help candidates with fewer resources create ads without the cost of a traditional production shoot, although the project estimates that spending on AI-generated ads accounts for a fraction of overall campaign advertising spending [6].
  • Disclosure compliance: Pennsylvania requires disclosures on television, print-style, video and audio advertisements, and the rule can apply to ads created elsewhere if they are expected to air in the state [4].

Q&A

Q: Could Pennsylvania’s law apply to ads beyond political campaigns?

A: The Verge says the law’s wording could cover commercial spots and restaurant flyers. It also questions whether disclosures apply only when synthetic content creates a false consumer perception about a product or service [4].
