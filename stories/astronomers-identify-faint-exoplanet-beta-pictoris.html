Astronomers identify faint exoplanet Beta Pictoris d

Category: Science

Astronomers have identified Beta Pictoris d, a third giant planet orbiting the young nearby star Beta Pictoris, after new observations and archived data showed it had appeared in telescope images for more than a decade without being recognized [8][4][6][14]. The planet is 100 times fainter than Beta Pictoris b, the first planet discovered in the same system, and researchers described it as the faintest exoplanet directly imaged from Earth [1][8][6][10]. The discovery emerged while researchers were studying the known planet Beta Pictoris b, not searching for a new world [4][6][14]. A team using the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile spotted the object and confirmed it in ESO archive observations spanning more than 10 years; a separate NASA account said Webb observations also revealed a hidden planet in the same well-studied system [8][20][19]. Beta Pictoris lies about 63 to 64 light-years from Earth in the constellation Pictor and was already known to host Beta Pictoris b and c, a debris disk of gas and dust, and many comet-like bodies [6][4][20]. The star is only about 20 million to 23 million years old, compared with the Sun's age of about 4.5 billion to 4.6 billion years, making the system a nearby site for studying young planetary systems [4][19][22].

Key Points

  • Planet properties: Based on infrared brightness and color, ESO-linked material describes Beta Pictoris d as a gas giant about 2.4 times Jupiter's mass [14][9].
  • Wide orbit: NASA said Beta Pictoris d has the widest orbit of the three known planets in the system, while Silicon Republic reported an orbital distance of about 30 astronomical units [20][19].
  • Rare system: The finding makes Beta Pictoris only the second planetary system known to contain at least three directly imaged planets [6][19][22].
  • Independent checks: Several accounts said two independent teams made the discovery within days of each other using different telescopes and reported their results in The Astrophysical Journal Letters [12][15][17].

Timeline

  • more than a decade ago: Beta Pictoris d appeared in archive observations [8][14]
  • late 2025: Two independent groups detected the cold giant [12][15]
  • 15 July 2026: NASA announced Webb's discovery of the hidden planet [20]
  • 15 July 2026: ESO-linked discovery coverage described VLT confirmation [8][14]

Scientific Significance

  • Comparison set: A three-planet directly imaged system lets astronomers compare multiple young giant planets around the same star rather than infer formation histories from unrelated systems [19][22].
  • Independent evidence: The identification gained support from ground-based and space-based observations and from archive detections across more than a decade, reducing the chance that a single transient image artifact explains the signal [8][20][12].
  • Formation context: Because Beta Pictoris is much younger than the solar system and still contains a substantial debris disk, the planet adds a measured object to a system where giant planets have formed while smaller rocky bodies may still be assembling [4][15].

Historical Background

Beta Pictoris b was one of the first exoplanets directly imaged, discovered in 2008, and Beta Pictoris c was already known before Beta Pictoris d was identified [20]. The system has been a benchmark for planet formation studies since the 1980s, when the Infrared Astronomical Satellite (IRAS) discovered its prominent debris disk [20].
