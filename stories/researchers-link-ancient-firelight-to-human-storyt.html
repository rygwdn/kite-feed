Researchers link ancient firelight to human storytelling origins

Category: Science

Researchers at the University of St Andrews and Dartmouth College have proposed that controlled fire may have helped shape early human language by extending social life after dark and creating a setting for sustained storytelling [1][2][3][4]. They argue that firelight gathered early humans when hunting, gathering and other practical work had stopped, potentially shifting communication from gestures tied to the immediate environment toward more flexible spoken narratives [2][4]. The researchers described the idea as speculative and outlined six testable predictions for future work, including whether storytelling differs from everyday speech in rhythm and structure, whether firelight reduces the usefulness of gestures, and whether fireside narratives synchronize behavior, heart rates and brain activity between storytellers and listeners [3]. They said the work is preliminary, with hints supporting the hypothesis but no claim that the evolutionary scenario has been proved [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Research team: Catherine Hobaiter, a primatologist at the University of St Andrews, and Nathaniel J. Dominy, an anthropologist at Dartmouth College, developed the hypothesis [4].
  • Ape comparison: The authors said other great apes already coordinate activities, learn tool traditions, form alliances and communicate intentionally, challenging explanations that language first evolved for those tasks [4].
  • Prior finding: A 2024 study in Current Biology found chimpanzee gestural exchanges show rapid turn-taking with timing similar to human conversation [4].

Perspectives

Catherine Hobaiter: Hobaiter said the study began with a question about what early humans needed language for, given that other great apes already handle many practical social tasks without human-like speech.

Sources: ZME Science

Scientific Significance

  • Research focus: The hypothesis examines language as a medium for narrative communication in a recurring nighttime social setting, rather than only as a tool for immediate coordination [1][2][4].
  • Future work: The proposed research program includes behavioral, acoustic and physiological tests rather than reliance on archaeological inference alone [3].
