Egypt desert fossils reveal seven ancient shark species

Category: Science

Paleontologists studying Egypt’s Abu-Tartur Plateau have identified fossilized shark teeth in the Duwi Formation, showing that at least seven shark species lived in waters that covered parts of northern Africa during the Cretaceous period [1][3][4][5]. The latest find includes 14 teeth attributed to five extinct species, adding to two shark species previously recorded from the same layer [1][4][5]. The fossils came from phosphorite-rich deposits, rocks associated with high marine productivity, and researchers linked the assemblage to a nutrient-enriched shelf ecosystem [1][5]. During the Cretaceous, about 145 million to 66 million years ago, warmer conditions and higher sea levels left much of northern Africa underwater [1][4][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • New records: Researchers recorded Serratolamna cf. serrata and Squalicorax bassanii in Egypt for the first time, while Scapanorhynchus cf. raphiodon may represent a first African occurrence and one of its youngest fossil records [3][4].
  • Named genera: The teeth include forms assigned to Cretalamna, Scapanorhynchus, Serratolamna and Squalicorax, genera known from extinct shark lineages [4].
  • Study authorship: Paleontologist Tarek Yassin of Cairo University led the research, which was published in Cretaceous Research [1][5].
  • Food supply: Researchers connected the shark diversity to nutrient-rich upwelling that supported plankton, small fish, invertebrates and top predators [3][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Ecosystem reconstruction: The range of tooth shapes suggests the sharks occupied different ecological niches rather than competing in the same way for the same prey [5].
  • Assemblage limits: Researchers cautioned against reading the site as a literal mass-death shark graveyard because slow sediment accumulation can gather remains from different periods and habitats into one layer [4][5].
