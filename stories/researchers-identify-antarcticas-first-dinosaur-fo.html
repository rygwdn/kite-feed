Researchers identify Antarctica's first dinosaur fossil as a titanosaur

Category: Science

Researchers have identified a fossil collected on Antarctica's James Ross Island in 1985 as a tail vertebra from a titanosaurian sauropod, making it the first known dinosaur bone found on the continent [1][2][10][11]. The specimen, cataloged as BAS D.8621.25, came from the Santa Marta Formation and dates to the Late Cretaceous Campanian; sources place its age at roughly 78 million to 83 million years [2][8][1]. British Antarctic Survey geologist Michael Thomson and German paleontologist Reinhard Förster collected the vertebra on December 9, 1985, while mapping rock layers and gathering fossils on the Antarctic Peninsula, but researchers at the time treated it as a large reptile or marine reptile specimen rather than a dinosaur [2][3][4][10]. The new analysis, published June 29 in Acta Palaeontologica Polonica, compared the bone's shape with better-known dinosaur remains and assigned it to Titanosauria, a group of long-necked, plant-eating sauropods that included some of the largest known land animals [1][2][7][12]. The fossil is only the second sauropod body fossil reported from Antarctica, where dinosaur remains are scarce because much of the continent is covered by ice [2][8][4]. Researchers said the animal lived when Antarctica supported temperate forests rather than today's ice sheet, and Paul Barrett of London's Natural History Museum noted the fossil's significance for the history of Antarctic exploration [1][2].

Key Points

  • Specimen size: The vertebra is about 10 centimeters wide; researchers estimate the individual was about six to seven meters long, possibly a juvenile or a small adult [3][12][6].
  • Sparse record: Antarctica has yielded only about 12 described dinosaur species, with many finds concentrated around James Ross Island [4][5].
  • Anatomical comparison: The vertebra shows features of advanced eutitanosaurs and resembles material known from Argentina, but its incomplete state prevented assignment to a species [8].

Perspectives

Paul Barrett, Natural History Museum: Barrett said Antarctica's Late Cretaceous forests would have provided food for large herbivores and added that retreating ice could expose more evidence of past biodiversity.

Historical Background

The fossil was collected in 1985, before later dinosaur finds gave scientists a fuller picture of Antarctica's prehistoric fauna. Scientists first reported additional Antarctic dinosaur traces in the late 1990s, but the continent remains the least sampled for dinosaur fossils because little rock is accessible [5][4].
