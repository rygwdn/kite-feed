Canada fast-tracks Alberta-B.C. pipeline as separatism debate continues

Category: Canada

Prime Minister Mark Carney designated the proposed Pacific Link oil pipeline from Alberta to British Columbia a project of national interest on Oct. 1, putting it on an accelerated federal review track. The 1,200-kilometre line is designed to carry one million barrels a day to a proposed export terminal on B.C.’s coast, with the federal government targeting completion of project conditions by September 2027 [1][12]. Carney and Alberta Premier Danielle Smith presented the project as a way to expand exports beyond the United States; the federal government estimates it could create about 140,000 jobs and generate more than C$20 billion in annual GDP [1][12]. Separatists say the announcement will not change their campaign ahead of Alberta’s Oct. 19 referendum, while some First Nations and environmental groups object to the project and consultation process [3][4]. The pipeline is not approved or fully financed, and producers’ willingness to commit supply remains an open question; an open season to test shipper interest is expected next spring [9][6].

Key Points

  • Ownership and cost: Alberta and Ottawa would each hold 45 per cent of the project, Pembina Pipeline has a 10 per cent interest, and a preliminary cost estimate ranges from C$35.2 billion to C$43.7 billion [6][9].
  • Indigenous opposition: Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation called the announcement a “foregone conclusion” and said loan guarantees or project conditions do not constitute free, prior and informed consent [4].
  • Supply uncertainty: The pipeline’s one-million-barrel daily capacity would join other planned additions of about two million barrels a day, while analysts question whether producers will expand output enough to fill the new capacity [6].
  • Labour constraints: Deloitte Canada’s Andrew Botterill said oilsands producers are more aligned than a decade ago and are trying to coordinate on labour and avoid driving up costs [11].

Perspectives

Let Alberta Decide: Co-leader Keith Wilson questioned why Ottawa made the announcement so close to the Oct. 19 referendum, calling it political messaging aimed at voters [4].

Industry Impact

  • Oil production: Oilsands companies have recently focused on increasing output at existing operations rather than launching new greenfield megaprojects; industry leaders have pointed to technology such as autonomous haul trucks as a way to grow production without the same pressure for a large influx of workers [11].
  • Construction workforce: Energy and infrastructure projects may compete for tens of thousands of workers, including engineers, welders and pipe fitters, according to EY Canada managing partner Lance Mortlock [11].

Historical Background

The previous Alberta oilsands expansion boom ran from the mid-2000s to the mid-2010s, but falling crude prices and then the COVID-19 demand collapse weakened the case for large new projects [11]. Fort Hills, the last oilsands mine built from scratch, received final approval in 2013 and began operating in 2018; its cost rose to more than C$17 billion from C$13.5 billion [11].

Q&A

Q: What is the planned route of Pacific Link?

A: The proposed 1,200-kilometre line would run from Bruderheim, Alberta, to Delta, British Columbia [12].

Q: What does the national-interest designation change?

A: Ottawa made its national-interest decision before detailed engineering and consultation, rather than leaving the go-or-no-go call until after years of preparatory work [9].
