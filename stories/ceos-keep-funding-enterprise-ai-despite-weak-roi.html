CEOs keep funding enterprise AI despite weak ROI

Category: AI

A new wave of reporting finds many enterprises are still pouring money into AI even as the financial payoff remains hard to prove, with CEOs describing AI adoption as necessary to stay competitive and relevant [1][2][3][4][5]. In a PwC survey of 4,454 CEOs, 56% said AI delivered neither revenue growth nor cost reduction over the past 12 months, while only 12% said it achieved both outcomes [2][3]. Coverage also highlights a common pattern: companies run pilots and proof-of-concepts but struggle to scale them into durable, organization-wide returns without clearer road maps and stronger “AI foundations” [3][1]. The upbeat note is that a smaller group of firms is already translating AI into measurable results, suggesting the gap may be more about execution maturity than the technology itself [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: PwC’s CEO survey referenced in the coverage included 4,454 respondents, making it a large snapshot of executive sentiment on AI ROI [2][3].

Key Points

  • Competitive pressure: Executives told PwC they worry about falling behind if they do not lean further into AI, even when ROI remains unclear [3][2].
  • Revenue signals: PwC’s results show 30% of CEOs reported increased revenue from AI in the last 12 months, indicating uneven benefits across companies and use cases [3][2].
  • Pilot-to-scale gap: PwC said many companies have not moved “beyond pilots,” which it linked to missing foundations like clearly defined road maps and sufficient investment levels [3].
  • Datacenter spending: The reporting notes tens of billions of dollars are being poured into data-center buildouts and related infrastructure tied to the AI boom [3].
  • Short-term fixes: Some commentary frames enterprise AI as a tool being used to patch efficiency problems, raising the question of whether the gains will persist once the easy automation opportunities are exhausted [4].

Technical Details

  • AI pilots: Limited-scope trials (proofs of concept) that test AI in one workflow; PwC’s survey commentary highlights that many firms struggle to move beyond pilots into scaled deployment [3].
  • AI foundations: Organizational prerequisites such as clear road maps and adequate investment levels that PwC said many companies lack, which can slow the path from experimentation to measurable ROI [3].
  • Data-center buildout: Large-scale expansion of compute infrastructure used to train and run AI models; reporting notes tens of billions of dollars flowing into these projects alongside the AI boom [3].

Industry Impact

  • Enterprise software: If many firms remain stuck at the pilot stage, vendors may see more demand for implementation support and governance tooling that helps convert experiments into scaled programs [3].
  • Cloud and data centers: Continued AI infrastructure spending means cloud and colocation operators face strong demand drivers, but the scale of buildouts increases the importance of keeping capacity utilized [3].
  • Corporate strategy: The reporting suggests AI is increasingly treated as a baseline capability for competitiveness, which can shift budgets toward AI even before clear payback is proven [2][1].

Historical Background

Enterprise software waves often start with experimentation, then consolidation around repeatable processes and governance before returns become widespread [common]. The current enterprise AI debate echoes earlier adoption cycles (for example, early cloud migrations) where many organizations saw limited payoffs until they standardized data, security, and operating models at scale [common].

Q: Does the PwC survey distinguish between cost savings and revenue gains from AI?

A: Yes; PwC’s CEO survey results reported separate outcomes, including CEOs who saw increased revenue from AI and those who saw neither revenue growth nor cost reductions [2][3].

Q: What is the main reason executives keep investing even without clear ROI?

A: Survey coverage indicates many CEOs believe AI adoption is required to remain relevant and fear falling behind competitors if they do not invest [2][3].

Action Items

  • Run a two-metric AI scorecard: For each AI use case, track one outcome metric (revenue, cycle time, error rate) and one cost metric (compute + labor), then sunset pilots that cannot show progress toward either after a fixed period—reflecting PwC’s “beyond pilots” gap [3].
  • Prioritize ‘foundation’ work: Create an AI road map with owners, data readiness checks, and a scaling plan, aligning with PwC’s emphasis on missing foundations like clearly defined road maps [3].
  • Ask vendors for ROI proof: Request case studies that quantify outcomes (not just demos) and specify what data and process changes were required, since many CEOs report no measurable payoff yet [2][3].
