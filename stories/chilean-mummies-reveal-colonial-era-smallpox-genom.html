Chilean mummies reveal colonial-era smallpox genomes

Category: Science

Scientists recovered and analyzed smallpox DNA from two naturally mummified people found at the Camarones 9 archaeological site in northern Chile, identifying the first reported ancient smallpox virus genomes from the Americas [7][10][14][12]. The remains date to the early Spanish colonial period, between 1492 and 1631, and the findings were published July 30 in Science [5][10][14]. The genetic analysis placed the virus in an extinct variola lineage that falls evolutionarily between known medieval European strains and later smallpox variants, providing direct molecular evidence that smallpox reached the Americas through European colonization [4][7][8][12][15]. Researchers named the lineage CAM9 and found the two viral genomes were nearly identical, suggesting the two people were infected around the same time [5][13]. Historians have linked post-1492 epidemics to Indigenous population losses, but the study provides genetic evidence from South America rather than relying only on documentary records [5][1][2]. The study involved researchers from institutions including Trinity College Dublin and the University of Chile [7][5][13].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Human adaptation: El País reported that the sampled lineage had already stopped losing genes, indicating the virus had become fully adapted to humans by the time it reached the Americas [15].
  • Population losses: Coverage cited estimates ranging from three to four million Indigenous smallpox deaths to 10 million to 100 million deaths from combined colonial-era epidemics over several centuries [10][8][13].
  • Disease mix: Popular Science identified diphtheria, influenza, typhus and measles among other infectious diseases that spread in Indigenous communities after European arrival [8].

Perspectives

Constanza de la Fuente Castro: The University of Chile biological anthropologist said low DNA concentrations and accumulated damage make ancient pathogen recovery and analysis difficult.

Sources: ScienceAlert

Lara Cassidy: The study co-author said the virus may have reached an evolutionary optimum by the 17th and 18th centuries and then spread with little further adaptation in populations without prior immunity.

Sources: Popular Science

Timeline

  • 1492: Europeans arrived in the Americas, beginning sustained contact [12][8].
  • 1518: The first documented smallpox outbreak in the Americas occurred on Hispaniola [12][10].
  • 30 July 2026: Science published the Chilean mummy genome study [14][10].

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty and confidence: The study rests on two sampled individuals from one northern Chilean site, but the genomes’ phylogenetic position and near identity support a colonial-era introduction rather than an independent American origin [5][15][13].
  • Limits of sampling: Available sources do not report a broader regional sample set, so the study identifies an early South American lineage without mapping the full spread of smallpox across the Americas [14][10].

Historical Background

Smallpox, caused by the variola virus, remained one of the world’s deadliest infectious diseases until eradication in 1980 [10]. Researchers have also reported evidence of smallpox from 3,000-year-old Egyptian mummies, placing the disease’s history far earlier than the colonial Americas [10].
