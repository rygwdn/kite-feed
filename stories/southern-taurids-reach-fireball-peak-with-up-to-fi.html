Southern Taurids reach fireball peak, with up to five meteors an hour

Category: Science

The Southern Taurid meteor shower’s expected surge in fireballs peaks around November 4-5, with maximum activity forecast for 3 p.m. ET on November 5. The best viewing is around midnight, when up to five meteors an hour may appear [1][2][3]. The shower is visible from nearly anywhere except the South Pole, and a crescent moon should leave relatively dark skies [1][9]. The meteors come from debris left by Comet 2P/Encke, which orbits the sun about every 3.3 years [1][2]. The Southern Taurids remain active through November 20; the separate Northern Taurid shower is expected to peak overnight November 11-12 [1][2]. Bill Cooke, chief of NASA’s Meteoroid Environment Office, said Earth is forecast to cross a denser Taurid debris swarm in 2032 [1].

Primary Source: View original article

What makes the Taurids spectacular is that they’re rich in bright fireballs.

— Bill Cooke, head of NASA’s Meteoroid Environment Office (CNN)

Key Points

  • Long trajectories: Taurid meteoroids enter the atmosphere at about 65,000 mph, slower than Orionid meteors, and often leave longer visible trails [1][2].
  • Fireball trails: Fireballs can outshine Venus; streaks of ionized air may fade within seconds, while some vapor-like smoke trails linger for several minutes [1][5].
  • Viewing advice: Experts recommend finding a dark location, looking broadly toward Taurus rather than directly at its radiant, and allowing 20-30 minutes for eyes to adjust to the dark without checking a phone [2][1].
  • Other showers: The Leonids are forecast to peak November 17-18 at about 15 meteors an hour, while December’s Geminids can produce up to 120 an hour near peak [3].

Timeline

  • 20 September 2026: Southern Taurid activity began for the 2026 season [2]
  • 14 October 2026: The shower reached its first activity peak of the year [2][3]
  • 4 November 2026: The predicted fireball-swarm peak began overnight [3][2]
  • 20 November 2026: Southern Taurid activity is expected to end [1][2]

Technical Details

  • Debris-size range: Most meteors come from dust- or pebble-sized material, but fireballs can come from pieces ranging in size from marbles to small boulders [1].

Historical Background

Scientists believe Comet 2P/Encke was once part of a larger body that broke apart about 20,000 to 30,000 years ago. The resulting debris complex is linked not only to the Southern and Northern Taurids but also to at least three other meteor showers [3][1].

Action Items

  • Watch with the naked eye rather than binoculars or a telescope, which limit how much sky you can see [2].
