T. rex had human-like body temperature, tooth study finds

Category: Science

A study published in Science Advances estimates that Tyrannosaurus rex had a body temperature of about 36.3 degrees Celsius, or roughly 97 degrees Fahrenheit, based on chemical analysis of fossilized tooth enamel. The estimate is close to the average human body temperature and supports researchers' conclusion that T. rex was warm-blooded rather than relying mainly on external heat [3][12][24]. Researchers analyzed rare bonds between carbon and oxygen isotopes preserved in three T. rex teeth from fossils found in Montana. The estimate provides a direct measure of body temperature and supports earlier evidence about tyrannosaurs' metabolism. T. rex lived roughly 68 million to 66 million years ago [2][13][16].

Key Points

  • Temperature comparison: The estimate places T. rex below modern birds, whose body temperatures generally range from 104 to 109 degrees Fahrenheit, but above cold-blooded reptiles and sloths [3].
  • Research reach: Robert Eagle said the chemical approach could be used to investigate body temperatures in many other species [21].
  • Ecological role: Researchers described T. rex as an energetic predator and scavenger [2].

Perspectives

Ariadna Tripati: The UCLA geoscientist and lead author said T. rex was thermally more comparable to elephants and ostriches than to crocodiles [25].

Sources: Naftemporiki

Technical Details

  • Clumped isotopes: Rare carbon and oxygen isotopes form temperature-dependent bonds. Their abundance in enamel allows researchers to estimate the temperature at which the tooth formed [2][15].
  • Enamel preservation: Tooth enamel's large, resilient crystalline structure preserves chemical signals better than many other fossil tissues over geological timescales [3][15].
  • Uncertainty range: One report gives the estimate as 36.3 degrees Celsius, plus or minus 2.5 degrees, so the result is an interval rather than an exact reading [24].

Scientific Significance

  • Evolutionary implication: The finding challenges the older view of dinosaurs as uniformly reptilian in physiology [1].
