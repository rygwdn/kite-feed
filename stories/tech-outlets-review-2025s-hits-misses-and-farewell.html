Tech outlets review 2025’s hits, misses, and farewells

Category: Technology

A cluster of year-end retrospectives is taking stock of what worked, what flopped, and what quietly disappeared from the tech landscape in 2025, spanning everything from consumer gadgets to long-running software staples [1][5][2][7][4][6]. Alongside “best of” lists, multiple outlets published “tech we lost” roundups that frame product shutdowns and retirements as a sign of how quickly the industry pivots—and how often old habits get replaced by newer defaults [2][7][4]. One clear throughline: the year featured both genuine progress and a healthy amount of trial-and-error, with some companies streamlining toward software and stronger account security while other ideas simply didn’t stick [2][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Mashable notes TiVo DVRs lasted into 2025—well past their peak—and only disappeared from TiVo’s website in October [2].

Key Points

  • TiVo pivots: Mashable notes TiVo stopped selling its DVR boxes after they were removed from its website in October, while the company continues by focusing on software used by TVs sold in Europe [2].
  • Passwords fade: Mashable reports Microsoft phased out its password manager in stages and pushed users toward passkeys, describing them as device-stored login credentials often tied to a fingerprint or PIN [2].
  • Old icons exit: Firstpost’s “goodbye” list highlights nostalgia-driven departures that included Skype, the iPhone’s home button era, and the Windows “blue screen of death” as familiar touchpoints that changed or vanished [4].
  • Flops cataloged: PCMag’s year-end “trainwrecks” roundup spotlights high-profile missteps and security/ops failures as reminders that not every launch or workflow scales safely [5].
  • Personal favorites: MobileSyrup frames its year-end picks as a celebration exercise, publishing an individual writer’s “top tech” list as part of broader retrospectives across phones, games, and gadgets [6].

Technical Details

  • Passkeys: Passkeys are a passwordless login method typically tied to a device (and unlocked with biometrics or a PIN), and Mashable reports Microsoft migrated users toward passkeys as it phased out its password manager [2].
  • DVR hardware sunset: A DVR (digital video recorder) stores TV recordings locally; Mashable reports TiVo ended sales of its DVR boxes and shifted emphasis toward software used by TV makers, reflecting a move away from dedicated recording hardware [2].
  • Product deprecation: Deprecation is the planned retirement of a feature or product—often via phased rollouts—so users can transition; Mashable describes staged changes as Microsoft moved users from password management to passkeys [2].

Industry Impact

  • Account security: The shift away from traditional password tools toward passkeys points to continued industry investment in reducing credential theft risk by changing how users authenticate day-to-day [2].
  • Consumer electronics lifecycle: TiVo’s exit from DVR box sales illustrates how legacy hardware categories can shrink or consolidate as companies pivot to software layers embedded in smart TVs [2].
  • Product-market feedback loops: Multiple outlets’ “fails” and “trainwrecks” lists show how publicly visible postmortems can pressure vendors to improve safety, reliability, and user experience, influencing what gets funded next year [5].

Historical Background

Consumer tech has long cycled through “platform shifts,” where new defaults (streaming, cloud services, mobile-first design) gradually replace older hardware-centric habits like physical media playback and set-top recording [common]. Microsoft and others have also spent years trying to move logins beyond reusable passwords because stolen or reused credentials are a common cause of account compromise, making passwordless approaches an industry-wide goal [common]. The 2025 retrospectives fit into that bigger pattern: products that match new distribution models and security expectations tend to survive longer than those built around yesterday’s workflows [2][4].
