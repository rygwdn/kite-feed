Oregon scientists grow potatoes in simulated lunar soil with compost

Category: Science

Oregon State University researchers grew potatoes through their full life cycle in simulated lunar soil mixed with organic worm compost. A blend containing 30% compost produced growth and yields comparable to those in Earth soil [2][14][7][1]. The NASA-funded study tested five soil simulants representing lunar highlands and plains. Pure simulant severely stunted plants, while lower compost levels produced only partial recovery [12][15]. The potatoes retained broadly similar nutritional profiles, and tests found no statistically significant increase in two naturally occurring toxins. Some tubers nevertheless contained higher levels of certain metals [2][14]. The experiment used Earth-based simulants in small pots in a greenhouse, not lunar soil or lunar conditions. Researchers say they used the harvested potatoes for lab analysis rather than tasting them [6][7]. The team is evaluating 10 potato varieties for a follow-up study [12][14].

Lunar regolith does not contain any nitrogen, so no crop would grow without supplementing it

— Aymeric Goyer, Oregon State University researcher and study senior author (TimesLIVE)

Did you know: Apollo missions returned 342 kilograms of lunar regolith, an amount too small to fill even a one-hectare greenhouse [1].

Key Points

  • Metal uptake: Potatoes grown in pure mare simulant accumulated more copper, while some crops grown in highlands simulants had elevated aluminum levels; one highlands type delayed emergence to about 24 days, and only three of five plants produced tubers [2].
  • Other crops: A University of Texas at Austin team grew chickpeas through a full life cycle in regolith simulant amended with vermicompost and root-associated mycorrhizal fungi, although the plants produced fewer and smaller chickpeas [1][13].
  • Closed loop: The European Space Agency’s MELiSSA project in Barcelona is linking waste-processing, nutrient-recovery and algae-growing compartments to recycle astronaut waste and respiration into fertilizer, food and oxygen [1].
  • Earth applications: Oregon State co-author David Handy said methods developed for lunar-style soils could also aid food production in urban and desert communities that depend on shipments and help remediate contaminated soils [4][7].

Perspectives

Francesc Gòdia, director of the MELiSSA pilot plant, said the team must first demonstrate that the life-support system works on Earth because astronauts’ lives depend on it. The team is now connecting its compartments after decades of testing [1].

Technical Details

  • Copper binding: Researchers propose that vermicompost increases the simulant’s cation-exchange capacity, binding copper ions and limiting their uptake by potato roots [1][13].
