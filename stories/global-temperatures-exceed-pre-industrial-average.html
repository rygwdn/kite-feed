Global temperatures exceed pre-industrial average by 2°C for a week as El Niño intensifies

Category: Science

Preliminary data from Copernicus, the European Union’s climate-monitoring service, show global temperatures stayed more than 2°C above the 1850–1900 average for seven consecutive days, reaching 2.25°C above that baseline on Monday [1]. These daily readings do not establish that the Paris Agreement’s temperature limit has been breached: that target is assessed over at least 20 years [1]. Scientists attribute the spike to long-term human-caused warming combined with a strong El Niño [1][5]. The record run coincided with COP31 climate negotiations in Turkey, where nearly 200 countries failed to agree on language about moving away from fossil fuels [1]. The UK Met Office forecasts an unusually sharp rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide in 2027 as drought weakens natural carbon sinks; one estimate puts the year-to-year increase at about 3.5 parts per million [1][3]. The current El Niño began in June and is expected to continue until at least March [1].

El Niño will come and go; the underlying warming from greenhouse gas emissions will not, unless we bring those emissions down.

— Samantha Burgess, strategic lead for Climate at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (Financial Times)

Key Points

  • Autumn records: October 2026 was the warmest October worldwide since records began, while Toronto set an October heat record dating back 80 years [2].
  • Pacific conditions: The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration put the chance of a historic El Niño event by January at 60% [1].
  • Coastal hazards: Storms prompted flood warnings along the California coast and as far north as Oregon and Washington; New York postponed its Thanksgiving Day parade until Saturday [1].
  • Emissions trajectory: A climate researcher writing for The Conversation said global emissions may have peaked in 2026, but stressed that a peak does not amount to the steep cuts needed to reduce warming [5].

Perspectives

Samantha Burgess, climate strategic lead at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, said governments face two tasks: preparing communities for higher temperatures and accelerating cuts in greenhouse gas emissions. She cited heat-related damage to roads and railways and pressure on hospitals [3].

Technical Details

  • El Niño signal: Sea-surface temperatures more than 4°C above average across the eastern equatorial Pacific reflect the event’s exceptional strength. The warm water affects global average temperatures [1][5].

Scientific Significance

  • Scale of the drivers: El Niño events can raise global average temperatures by tenths of a degree, while cumulative human emissions have warmed the planet by almost 1.5°C [5].
  • Regional heat risks: Major El Niño events increase the chance of heat in Australia and Indonesia, and this event is expected to bring particularly humid, dangerous heat to the tropics [5][1].

Historical Background

The 2015 Paris Agreement set a goal of limiting global warming to 1.5°C and keeping it well below 2°C compared with pre-industrial levels [1][3].

Q&A

Q: How unusual is the forecast rise in carbon dioxide?

A: The forecast year-to-year increase is about one-third faster than in recent years and nearly twice the rate consistent with limiting warming to 1.5°C [3].

Q: When might the current El Niño’s effects be most pronounced?

A: The event is forecast to peak between November and January, but scientists expect some of its effects to be felt several months after the peak [3].
