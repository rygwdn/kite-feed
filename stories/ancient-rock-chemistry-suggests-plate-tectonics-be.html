Ancient rock chemistry suggests plate tectonics began 2.5 billion years ago

Category: Science

A chemical signal in ancient rocks disappears from the geological record about 2.5 billion years ago, a shift researchers say may mark the start of modern plate tectonics [2][1]. The findings suggest Earth’s early continental crust formed through repeated remelting of ancient material before new crust began forming from a mixture of old and fresh sources, a process the researchers compare with activity at present-day plate boundaries [2]. The study examined more than 1,200 rocks from ancient landmasses in Australia, Africa, North America and Europe, spanning nearly four billion years [2]. The researchers said the chemical signal’s disappearance coincided with other changes in rock chemistry and crustal composition, but they do not know what triggered the tectonic shift [2].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Early Earth: For much of its early history, Earth may have been mostly covered by oceans, without the shifting, colliding plates that build tall mountains [2].
  • Earlier crust: Rocks older than about 2.5 billion years bore a chemical signature linked to ancient crust that appears to have formed about four billion years ago [2].

Technical Details

  • Mineral record: Researchers used chemical traces preserved in feldspar and some sulfide minerals, which can remain largely undisturbed for billions of years [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Planetary role: Plate tectonics builds mountain ranges, triggers earthquakes, shapes oceans and continents, and may help regulate climate and Earth’s magnetic field over deep time [2].
  • Supercontinent formation: Earth’s first supercontinent also formed during the era when the chemical fingerprint disappeared [2].
