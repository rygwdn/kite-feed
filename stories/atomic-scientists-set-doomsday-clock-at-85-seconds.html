Atomic scientists set Doomsday Clock at 85 seconds

Category: World

The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists set its symbolic “Doomsday Clock” to 85 seconds to midnight, the closest it has ever been, saying catastrophic global risks are rising [6][7][3][5][1][4][2]. In explaining the move, the group cited intertwined threats including nuclear tensions and conflict, climate change, and the fast-growing role of artificial intelligence in amplifying disinformation [6][3][1][4][5]. While the clock is a metaphor rather than a prediction, the scientists urged leaders and the public to push for practical steps—especially renewed arms-control and risk-reduction—to pull the world back from the brink [1][3][6].

Key Points

  • Trump policies cited: Some coverage noted the clock update came during President Trump’s second term and reported the Bulletin’s criticism of proposed US missile-defense plans and of administration energy policy, arguing both could worsen strategic and climate-related risks [2][5].
  • How it works: The Doomsday Clock was created in 1947 by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists as a metaphor meant to communicate the likelihood of human-caused global catastrophe, rather than to provide a predictive model [8][7].
  • Disinformation focus: Nobel Peace Prize laureate Maria Ressa argued that social media and generative AI have helped drive an “informational armageddon” that is not “anchored in facts,” a dynamic the Bulletin highlighted as a rising systemic risk [3].
  • Mirror life: Live Science highlights “mirror life” as an emerging biosecurity concern discussed alongside AI, referring to the idea of life forms built from opposite-handed biological molecules and the potential implications for ecosystems and biodefense [10].
  • Geopolitical context: The Guardian reports the Bulletin linked the clock’s move to rising international aggression and nationalism among major powers including Russia, China, and the United States [11].
  • Public leverage: The scientists emphasized civic pressure as a constructive lever—calling on the public to demand quick action from leaders on nuclear and climate threats as a way to reduce the risks the clock symbolizes [1].
  • AI governance: Several articles emphasize concerns about “unregulated AI,” framing governance, safety standards, and misuse prevention as key factors the Bulletin weighed when setting the clock [10][7][11].
  • Arms control gap: The Bulletin warned that the New START treaty is nearing expiration, which it said could leave the United States and Russia without any arms-control agreement and prompted calls to resume dialogue on limiting nuclear arsenals [2][3].
  • Public pressure: Al Jazeera reports the Bulletin urged the public to demand faster action from leaders on nuclear and climate risks as part of a broader strategy to reduce the chance of catastrophe [1].
  • Major-power posture: The group described major countries as becoming more “aggressive, adversarial, and nationalistic,” framing that shift as a driver of heightened danger alongside existing nuclear and climate risks [6].

Technical Details

  • New START treaty: The last major US-Russia agreement limiting deployed strategic nuclear forces; the Bulletin warned its expiration would remove a key constraint and urged renewed arms-control talks [3][2].
  • Doomsday Clock: A metaphorical “time” set by the Bulletin to communicate how close it believes the world is to catastrophe; it is updated each January and is not a literal forecast [3][2].
  • Doomsday Clock metaphor: A symbolic clock whose “midnight” represents a human-made global catastrophe; it is designed to communicate risk, not to predict exact events [8][7].
  • AI-driven disinformation: The Bulletin highlighted how AI can amplify disinformation at scale, which it argues can undermine decision-making during crises and increase broader systemic risk [3][6].
  • Mirror life concept: A proposed form of biology built from opposite-handed molecules (a ‘mirror’ version of familiar biochemistry), discussed as a potential biosafety and ecological-risk topic [10].
  • Risk drivers: The Bulletin’s update reflects a multi-factor assessment that includes nuclear tensions, climate disruption, and fast-evolving technologies such as AI [7][11][1].

Scientific Significance

  • Governance focus: Rather than presenting new experimental findings, the update underscores a research-to-policy gap: several articles stress that the most actionable lever is governance—rules, oversight, and safety practices—especially for AI and other emerging technologies [10][1].
  • Interacting risks: Across the coverage, the throughline is that risks can compound—geopolitical conflict can raise nuclear danger while also slowing climate cooperation, and rapid AI development can introduce new security vulnerabilities [11][7].
  • Scientific meaning: The update reflects a risk-communication judgment by a science-oriented advocacy group rather than a new measurement; it bundles multiple interacting hazards—nuclear, climate, and technological—into a single public signal intended to spur policy action [6][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Because the clock is a symbolic assessment, it does not come with statistical error bars; its “seconds” are a communicative device based on expert deliberation about trends the Bulletin describes as worsening [3][4].
  • Science communication: The clock functions as a science-to-public communication device: it compresses diverse, hard-to-compare risks (nuclear, climate, and technology) into a single, attention-grabbing indicator that can prompt broader discussion of mitigation [8][7].
  • Limitations and future work: The clock’s simplicity can’t show how each risk contributes or which interventions would reduce danger most; the Bulletin’s accompanying statements instead point to concrete areas—arms-control dialogue, climate action, and managing AI-disinformation—as priorities [1][3][2].

Historical Background

The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists created the Doomsday Clock in 1947 as a symbolic gauge of how close humanity is to self-inflicted catastrophe, originally shaped by the Cold War nuclear arms race [3]. The clock has since been updated annually to reflect a broader set of risks, with nuclear weapons remaining central while issues such as climate change and disruptive technologies increasingly feature in the Bulletin’s assessments [3][4].

Q&A

Q: Who decides the clock’s time each year?

A: The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists sets the clock through its experts and advisers as part of its annual assessment process [3][2].

Q: What does “midnight” represent in the clock metaphor?

A: In the Bulletin’s framework, midnight symbolizes a theoretical global catastrophe caused by disruptive human-made threats [3].

Q: Why do the scientists include climate change alongside nuclear weapons?

A: The Bulletin describes climate change as a catastrophic risk that interacts with geopolitical instability and other threats, so it factors into the clock setting alongside nuclear dangers [1][6][4].

Q: Who decides the Doomsday Clock time each year?

A: The setting is determined by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists and its experts, who review global risk factors and then announce the updated time [8][7].

Q: Does the clock measure the probability of catastrophe numerically?

A: No; sources describe it as a symbolic metaphor meant to communicate urgency, not as a predictive or statistical model [8][7].

Q: What practical changes could lower the clock’s risk assessment?

A: Articles point to faster action on nuclear and climate threats and stronger governance for emerging technologies like AI as the kinds of measures the Bulletin argues could reduce overall risk [1][7][10].
