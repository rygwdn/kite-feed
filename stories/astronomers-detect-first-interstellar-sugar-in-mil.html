Astronomers detect first interstellar sugar in Milky Way

Category: Science

An international team led by Izaskun Jiménez-Serra of Spain’s Centro de Astrobiología reported the first confirmed detection of a sugar molecule in interstellar space in a Nature Astronomy study published July 13 [7][8][10][11][12][13]. The molecule, erythrulose, is a four-carbon sugar found on Earth in raspberries and self-tanning products. Researchers identified it in the molecular cloud G+0.693−0.027 near the center of the Milky Way, about 26,000 to 27,000 light-years from Earth [5][7][10][23][29]. The team used radio telescopes and matched spectral signals from the cloud to laboratory measurements of erythrulose [7][8][10][13][32].

This is the very first sugar to be detected in interstellar space and it is important because it tells us that these sugars are more common than we previously thought

— Dr. Izaskun Jiménez-Serra (The Guardian)

Did you know: The same general region of the Milky Way previously yielded ethyl formate, a compound that contributes to the flavor and aroma of raspberries [4][46].

Key Points

  • Rich molecular cloud: G+0.693−0.027 is one of the most chemically rich molecular clouds in the galaxy and has yielded numerous prebiotic molecules in recent years [22].
  • Unexpected abundance: The team measured at least eight times more erythrulose than comparable three-carbon sugars in the cloud and reported no detections of those three-carbon variants [7].
  • Possible delivery: The study estimated that 5 million to 50 million tons of erythrulose may have reached Earth during the Late Heavy Bombardment 4.1 billion to 3.9 billion years ago [22].
  • Chemical route: Researchers proposed that erythrulose can form on interstellar dust grains when glycolaldehyde and ethylene glycol react, according to the Nature Astronomy study [12][39][37].

Perspectives

Izaskun Jiménez-Serra, Centro de Astrobiología: Jiménez-Serra said the team had not expected success after it failed to find simpler three-carbon sugars in G+0.693−0.027, before the erythrulose signature appeared.

Sources: The Guardian

Erika Hamden, University of Arizona: Hamden, who was not involved in the research, described the molecule as one of the most complex sugars spotted so far and called it a pristine example of material floating in the galaxy.

Sources: ABC News

Brett McGuire, Massachusetts Institute of Technology: McGuire, who was not involved in the study, said the molecule qualifies as a genuine sugar rather than only a sugar-like compound.

Sources: Folha de S.Paulo/The New York Times

Technical Details

  • Molecular line matching: The identification rests on matching 12 emission features in the cloud’s radio spectrum to laboratory spectra of erythrulose, a standard method for assigning molecules in chemically crowded astronomical sources [7][10][32].
  • Four-carbon ketose: Erythrulose has the formula C4H8O4 and is described as the only possible four-carbon ketose, placing it in the same broad chemical family as biologically important sugars rather than sugar-like two-carbon molecules [10][23][15].
  • Chirality: Erythrulose is chiral, meaning it can occur in mirror-image molecular forms, a property relevant to prebiotic chemistry because living systems use specific handedness in many biomolecules [29][common].

Scientific Significance

  • Origin-of-life research: The result connects interstellar chemistry to origin-of-life research by showing that a formal sugar can exist in a star-forming molecular cloud, rather than only in meteorites, asteroids or laboratory mixtures [10][11][23].
  • Detection method: The detection is observational and spectroscopic, not a returned sample; confidence comes from multiple matching radio lines and comparison with laboratory measurements rather than from physical extraction of the molecule [7][13][32].
  • Limits of the finding: The finding does not detect life, and AP reported that erythrulose itself is not essential for life, although it can convert to a form thought to be important for early life chemistry [13][14][1].

Historical Background

Sugars such as ribose and glucose had previously been identified in meteorites and asteroid samples, including material returned from asteroid Bennu by NASA’s OSIRIS-REx mission [10][11][13][1]. Scientists spotted a cousin to table sugar near the center of the Milky Way about 25 years before this study, but direct detection of a formal sugar in the interstellar medium had remained unconfirmed [13][16].
