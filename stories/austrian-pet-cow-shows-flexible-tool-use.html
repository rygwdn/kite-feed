Austrian pet cow shows flexible tool use

Category: Science

Researchers in Austria report the first experimentally verified tool use in cattle after observing a pet Brown Swiss cow, Veronika, deliberately manipulating a broom or sticks to scratch herself [6][5][4]. The work, published in Current Biology, describes her using the object as a multipurpose tool and adjusting how she handled it to match the task—behavior the team says meets scientific definitions of tool use [6][8]. The finding is a cheerful reminder that familiar farm animals may have more problem-solving capacity than scientists have documented so far, especially when their environments allow exploration [3][12].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: One report notes Veronika is 13 years old and has been kept as a companion rather than for meat or milk [11].

Key Points

  • Ten trial design: In a series of 10 trials, researchers tossed a deck broom in random orientations and recorded which end Veronika selected and how she used it [8].
  • Careful gripping: Video analysis described Veronika gripping the broom with her mouth by positioning it under her tongue and wedging it between her teeth for stability [8].
  • Sensitive-area control: Reports describe her using the bristled end more forcefully on tougher-to-reach areas while switching to the stick end more gently for sensitive areas such as the udder [12][11].
  • Environment matters: Researchers and outside commentators noted that animals in more "impoverished" or restrictive farm settings may have fewer chances to express such behaviors, which could help explain why cattle tool use has gone undocumented [12][3].
  • Call for sightings: The authors invited the public to contact them if they have observed cows or bulls deliberately using objects, to help determine how widespread the behavior might be [12].

Perspectives

Study authors (University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna): They interpret Veronika’s repeated, end-selective broom handling as deliberate, multipurpose tool use that meets scientific definitions and suggests cattle cognition may be under-observed.

Sources: Sci.News

Science journalists highlighting novelty: Coverage emphasizes that this is the first documented tool use in cows and notes that flexible selection of tool parts has previously been reported mainly in a small set of species.

Sources: Science

Animal welfare and cognition commentators: Some writers argue the observation strengthens the case for taking farm-animal minds seriously and may influence how people think about the mental lives of livestock.

Sources: New Scientist

Technical Details

  • Tool use (biological definition): Several articles define tool use as manipulating an external object to achieve a goal through a mechanical interface—here, using a broom or stick to produce scratching against the body [8][11].
  • Multipurpose tool selection: The study reports Veronika used the same object in different ways, choosing different ends for different body areas, which the authors frame as versatile tool use [6][8].
  • Experimental verification: Rather than relying only on anecdotes, researchers structured repeated trials (including random broom orientations) and recorded consistent handling choices to support an intentional-use interpretation [8][6].

Industry Impact

  • Animal welfare debate: Commentary across outlets suggests findings like this may intensify public discussion about how housing and enrichment affect the behavioral expression of livestock cognition, even if the study itself focuses on a single cow [3][9].
  • Research and enrichment practices: The researchers’ call for additional sightings and the emphasis on opportunity-rich environments could encourage more systematic observation of cattle behavior and experimentation with safe enrichment objects in research or husbandry settings [12].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The report expands the list of species with experimentally supported tool use by adding cattle, suggesting that complex object manipulation may not be limited to a small set of “usual suspect” animals and may be easier to miss in species that researchers observe less often in this context [5][6][13].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The evidence centers on a single, closely observed individual, but the study design described in coverage includes repeated trials and consistent end selection, which strengthens the claim that the behavior was not accidental [8][6].
  • Limitations and future work: Because Veronika is a pet cow with unusual opportunities for exploration, the authors and commentators highlight the need for broader observations and additional animals to learn how common this capacity is across cattle and under different living conditions [12][3].

Historical Background

Scientists have long used “tool use”—manipulating an external object to achieve a goal—as one signal of advanced problem-solving in animals [11]. Documented examples include chimpanzees using sticks to access food and crows using tools to probe, while archaeologists often point to early hominin stone tools as a landmark in human prehistory [11][common]. Against that backdrop, multiple outlets note that cattle had not previously had experimentally verified tool use reported, making Veronika’s case notable mainly because it fills a gap in what researchers have formally documented [6][5].

Q&A

Q: Do scientists think Veronika learned this from humans or figured it out herself?

A: The articles describe observations and trial results but do not provide a definitive conclusion about social learning versus individual discovery [6][3].

Q: Have any other cows shown similar behavior outside this study?

A: Coverage says this is the first experimentally verified case, and the authors explicitly asked readers to report additional sightings, implying more examples may exist but remain undocumented [12][5].

Q: What would convince scientists that tool use is common in cattle?

A: Reports point toward the need for more observations across different animals and settings, ideally with repeated, structured tests similar to those used with Veronika [12][8].

Action Items

  • Share credible observations with researchers: If you have witnessed deliberate object use by cattle (e.g., repeated, goal-directed manipulation), consider documenting it (date, setting, video if safely possible) and contacting the study team as invited in coverage [12].
  • Visit the primary study for details: Look up the Current Biology paper referenced in coverage to review methods, trial structure, and definitions used before drawing broader conclusions [6][5].
