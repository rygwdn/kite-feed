Broccoli grown under magenta solar panels yields comparable harvests

Category: Science

Researchers in Sweden tested semi-transparent magenta solar panels over broccoli and found yields comparable to those of open-field plants while the panels generated electricity [1][2][5]. Broccoli under the panels took 25 days longer to mature but used available light more efficiently—up to 4.5 times as efficiently under the less-transparent panels [3][4]. The trial used two 20-square-meter panel plots and a full-sun comparison plot during one growing season. The researchers say the results are specific to those conditions and require testing across more seasons, crops and panel designs. Team leader Silvia Ma Lu said current configurations may suit community gardens or greenhouse roofs before large-scale farm use [3][5].

There is no single agrivoltaic design that will work optimally everywhere

Did you know: The magenta panels were originally developed for architectural use to give building interiors a rosy glow while generating electricity, and researchers reused commercially available units for the broccoli trial [4].

Key Points

  • Land efficiency: Under an assumption that panel structures leave 40% of a hectare uncultivated, researchers calculated a land-equivalent ratio of 1.18, or a theoretical 18% land-use advantage over separate farming and solar sites [5].
  • Power output: The 50%-transparent setup was estimated to produce 447.6 kilowatt-hours during cultivation, with a photovoltaic conversion efficiency of 7.2% [5].
  • Energy trade-off: The panels generated about 224.7 megawatt-hours per hectare over the growing period, roughly 100 megawatt-hours less than conventional panels; energy-systems expert Ramesh Rayudu estimated 1.2 to 3.2 additional tonnes of broccoli per hectare could offset the electricity-value gap, depending on prices [4][6].
  • Nutrient profile: Broccoli grown under the panels had similar calcium levels but lower amounts of micronutrients including iron and zinc, according to the study summary [2].

Perspectives

Silvia Ma Lu and the research team: The team has begun further tests of magenta panels alongside red and blue versions. It has also tested the panels in controlled lab settings that exclude unfiltered light [3].

Sources: Popular Science

Technical Details

  • Cadmium telluride strips: The panels use opaque cadmium telluride photovoltaic material arranged in strips within glass; denser spacing captures more sunlight for electricity but reduces light reaching crops [4][6].
  • Light transmission: The tested panels transmitted 13.2% and 19.2% of photosynthetically active radiation; the magenta filter passes red and blue wavelengths, while the cells do not capture the blocked green light [5][4].
  • Unresolved growth effects: The experiment could not determine whether broccoli growth reflected the filtered spectrum, the overall reduction in light or a greenhouse effect from the glass, so further controlled tests are needed [4].

Scientific Significance

  • Panel comparison: Broccoli performed similarly beneath panels with both tested transparency levels [3].

Q&A

Q: Why did the researchers choose broccoli?

A: Broccoli suited the Swedish test site and belongs to the same species as cabbage, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi, making those crops potential subjects for future testing [3][4].
