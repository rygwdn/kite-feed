Ancient Peruvians carried live parrots across Andes

Category: Science

A new multi-method study of centuries-old feathers from a major coastal Peruvian ceremonial center found that people carried live Amazonian parrots and macaws across the Andes around 1,000 years ago—far beyond the birds’ natural rainforest range [2][3][4][6][8][1]. Researchers traced the birds to the Amazon and identified multiple species, pointing to an organized long-distance trade that linked rainforest, highlands, and desert regions well before the Inca Empire [2][6][5][7]. The feathers and related burial finds show the birds were prized for their vivid plumage, used in elite adornment and mortuary practices—an unusually tangible glimpse of how pre-Inca societies built wide exchange networks and moved living animals across difficult terrain [3][8][1]. The work also shows how modern tools—ancient DNA, isotope chemistry, and landscape modeling—can reconstruct ancient human-animal relationships with surprising precision [4][5][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Species identified: Genetic analysis found at least four Amazonian parrot species in the feather remains, including scarlet macaw, blue-and-yellow macaw, red-and-green macaw, and mealy amazon [6].
  • Distance moved: The likely trade route ran more than 300 miles [4].
  • Site context: Researchers analyzed feathers from Pachacamac, a major religious center on Peru’s central coast—far from the rainforest habitat these birds need [4][8].
  • How traced: The team paired ancient DNA with isotope chemistry and computational landscape modeling to narrow down origins and likely transport paths [4][5][7].
  • Cultural use: Sources describe the birds’ feathers being used for elite adornment—such as headdresses—and to decorate the dead in mortuary contexts [3][8][1].

Perspectives

Study co-authors (methods emphasis): The researchers say that combining ancient DNA sequencing, isotope chemistry, and computational landscape modeling let them reconstruct how—and from where—the birds moved across the landscape.

New York Times reporting (interpretation): The reporting frames the discovery as an Andes-crossing trade in parrots, pieced together from old feathers found in a coastal tomb and traced back to Amazonian origins.

Sources: The New York Times

LiveScience reporting (animal handling): The reporting highlights that people carried the parrots alive across the mountains and kept them in captivity on the coast, supplying vivid feathers for mortuary decoration.

Sources: Sci.News

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: By combining biomolecular evidence with environmental modeling, the study strengthens the case that pre-Inca societies ran organized, long-distance networks that could transport—and sustain—live animals across extreme ecological barriers [4][7][5].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The core conclusions come from converging evidence (genetic species IDs, isotopic signals, and modeling that shows the coast was unsuitable habitat), reducing reliance on any single proxy for origin and movement [4][5].
  • Limitations and future work: Because the evidence comes from feathers and burial contexts at one major site, broader sampling across more sites and time layers would help clarify how widespread, frequent, and standardized these live-bird exchanges were [2][3].

Historical Background

Pachacamac was inhabited from about 200 BCE and later grew into a major religious center on Peru’s central coast, becoming a long-lived hub where offerings and burials accumulated over many centuries [4]. The new findings place the long-distance movement of live rainforest birds centuries before the Inca Empire, sharpening what archaeologists already infer about pre-Inca exchange networks spanning distinct ecological zones [7][5].
