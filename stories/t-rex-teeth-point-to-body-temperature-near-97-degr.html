T. rex teeth point to body temperature near 97 degrees Fahrenheit

Category: Science

Researchers estimated Tyrannosaurus rex’s body temperature at about 97 degrees Fahrenheit (36 degrees Celsius), close to human body temperature, by analyzing three fossilized teeth [2][6][5]. Science Advances published the study on Wednesday, Sept. 16, 2026 [2][6]. The estimate is similar to the body temperature of modern elephants and lower than typical temperatures in modern birds, the living descendants of dinosaurs [2][8].

Primary Source: View original article

We figured out how warm the most famous animal of all, a T. rex, was

— Aradhna Tripati (PBS NewsHour)

Did you know: The same tooth-based temperature technique has also been used on other ancient species, including woolly mammoths [6].

Key Points

  • Research process: After more than a decade of lab work, researchers persuaded a museum to allow them to sample T. rex teeth for the first time [1].
  • Climate range: Researchers said a body temperature near 36 degrees Celsius is consistent with T. rex living across most of prehistoric North America, including colder environments [8][6].

Perspectives

Robert Eagle, UCLA geobiologist: Eagle called the method the most direct and least complicated constraint on T. rex’s actual body temperature.

Sources: WPBF

Jasmina Wiemann, Johns Hopkins paleobiologist: Wiemann, who was not involved in the study, said she had expected a higher temperature based on previous research and comparisons with modern birds.

Sources: PBS NewsHour

The research team: UCLA researchers plan to apply the tooth-based method to other dinosaurs to compare body temperatures among species.

Sources: The Week

Timeline

  • 2022: An Alaska fossil footprint provided evidence of T. rex activity in cold climates [6][11]
  • 16 September 2026: Science Advances published the tooth-temperature study [2][6]
  • 17 September 2026: Researchers reported an estimate of approximately 36 degrees Celsius [2][5]

Technical Details

  • Enamel clumped isotopes: The method measures how atoms in tooth enamel clump together. Those patterns vary with the temperature at which the mineral formed, allowing researchers to estimate ancient body temperature [2][5].
  • Sampling limitation: Researchers derived the estimate from only three teeth, so testing additional specimens will be important in determining how broadly the reading applies [2][6].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The measured temperature supports the conclusion that T. rex was an internally regulated, warm-bodied predator rather than a typically cold-blooded reptile [2][6].
  • Physiological implications: The body-temperature estimate provides data for reconstructing the dinosaur’s metabolism, behavior, ecological interactions and likely energy requirements [6][11].
