Filmmaker Darren Aronofsky releases AI-made 1776 series

Category: AI

Filmmaker Darren Aronofsky has released an almost entirely AI-generated drama series about the American Revolution titled “On This Day… 1776,” produced through his AI studio Primordial Soup and partnered with Salesforce, TIME Studios, and Google’s DeepMind [1]. The first two episodes run about three minutes each and are available on YouTube, alongside a teaser trailer [1]. Reviews and reactions in the coverage are largely negative, focusing on the visual artifacts commonly associated with generative video (such as uncanny faces and scrambled text) and raising concerns about taste, craft, and the implications for human creative labor [1][2][3]. Even so, the project is being treated as a high-profile test of what “high-end” AI production can and can’t yet do—and it is prompting renewed public discussion about quality standards, historical responsibility, and how AI should be used in entertainment [1][3].

Key Points

  • Industry partners: The series is described as a partnership involving Primordial Soup, Salesforce, TIME Studios, and Google’s DeepMind, signaling that major tech and media organizations are experimenting with AI-native entertainment formats [1].
  • Public rollout: Futurism reports that two early episodes and a teaser are already posted on YouTube, suggesting the project is being tested with real-time audience feedback rather than held back for a traditional premiere [1].
  • Historical details: Historian Mateusz Fafinski pointed to an apparent error involving the depiction of Thomas Paine’s “Common Sense,” saying the AI bungled the pamphlet’s front page [1].
  • Viewer concerns: The coverage describes audience complaints that the project may sidestep paying actors and could involve AI regurgitating existing art, reflecting ongoing disputes about compensation and consent in generative media [1].
  • Critical framing: The Hollywood Reporter’s critic’s notebook characterizes the project as evidence that even expensive, polished AI output can still read as ‘AI slop’ to viewers, emphasizing a gap between technical novelty and perceived artistry [3].

Historical Background

The American Revolution (1775–1783) led to the independence of the Thirteen Colonies and the creation of the United States [common]. Thomas Paine’s pamphlet “Common Sense,” published in 1776, helped popularize arguments for independence among colonial readers [common].

Q&A

Q: What do viewers typically mean by ‘uncanny’ AI faces in video?

A: It usually refers to faces that look almost human but have subtle inconsistencies (eyes, teeth, skin texture, motion) that signal something is off—a phenomenon commonly called the ‘uncanny valley’ [common].

Q: Why is historically accurate text harder for generative models than scenery?

A: Many generative image/video systems struggle to render coherent typography and fine detail consistently, which can lead to jumbled letters even when broader visual style looks plausible [1].

Q: How might studios use AI responsibly without replacing performers?

A: One approach is to use AI for previsualization, concepting, or effects while still hiring performers and clearing rights—issues raised indirectly by audience complaints about actor pay and reuse of existing art [1][common].
