ESA reports software-related thruster glitch during BepiColombo's Mercury arrival

Category: Science

The European Space Agency (ESA) said a software issue shortened one of BepiColombo’s thruster burns during its Mercury orbit insertion. The joint European-Japanese spacecraft entered orbit successfully on Nov. 21 and remains safe. Mission teams at the European Space Operations Centre are investigating the cause, and ESA said it would provide an update on Monday, Nov. 23 [5][15][4]. BepiColombo carries two orbiters that have traveled together for eight years: ESA’s Mercury Planetary Orbiter and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, called Mio. They are scheduled to separate on Dec. 9–10, with routine science operations planned to begin in April 2027 [5][2][4]. The mission will investigate Mercury’s interior, magnetic field and surface, including the origins of its polar ice and unusual surface hollows. ESA said teams were investigating the issue and would provide further information on Monday [5][4].

Key Points

  • Different orbits: After separation, ESA’s orbiter is scheduled to pass as close as 480 kilometers from Mercury, while Japan’s Mio will follow an elliptical path reaching about 11,000 kilometers away [20].
  • Instrument payload: The European orbiter carries spectrometers, a surface altimeter and gravity-measuring instruments; its radio-science experiment will help determine the size of Mercury’s core [6].
  • Swiss contribution: Neuchâtel company Micro-Cameras & Space Exploration built cameras to monitor spacecraft components and record flybys; University of Bern teams led development of the BELA laser altimeter and STROFIO mass spectrometer [19].
  • Thermal challenge: Mercury’s surface can exceed 400°C, so mission engineers must keep the spacecraft and instruments cool while they operate in intense solar radiation [1][11].
  • Surface terrain: Mercury has expanses of smooth terrain as well as cliffs reaching about 1.6 kilometers high [5].

Perspectives

Professor Emma Bunce, a University of Leicester principal investigator on the mission, said its scientific goals range from understanding how Mercury formed and evolved to studying its interaction with the Sun and extreme space weather [3].

Sources: BBC News

Geraint Jones, BepiColombo’s lead project scientist, highlighted the mission’s ability to compare simultaneous measurements from two spacecraft at Mercury, describing the data as unprecedented [1].

Sources: Associated Press, published by Phys.org

Timeline

  • 2018: BepiColombo launched from French Guiana aboard an Ariane 5 rocket [7]
  • 2024: A thruster-power problem forced a route adjustment and delayed arrival at Mercury [6]
  • 21 November 2026: A braking maneuver put the spacecraft into orbit around Mercury [5][4]
  • 22 November 2026: ESA disclosed that a software issue had shortened the orbit-insertion thruster burn [5][4]
  • 9 December 2026: The European and Japanese orbiters are scheduled to separate [5][2]

Scientific Significance

  • Planetary interior: Measurements from the orbiters’ instruments are intended to constrain the structure of Mercury’s unusually large iron core and test explanations for how the planet acquired it [7][6].
  • Core origin: Professor Emma Bunce said Mercury may have lost much of its mantle in a major collision or a series of impacts, though it may instead have formed in a metal-rich region of the early solar system [3].

Historical Background

NASA’s Mariner 10 made three flybys of Mercury in the 1970s, and NASA’s MESSENGER spacecraft became the first to orbit the planet in 2011 before its mission ended in 2015 [1][5]. BepiColombo is the first spacecraft to return to Mercury orbit since MESSENGER [7].

Q&A

Q: What work remains after the orbiters separate?

A: Mio is expected to settle into its final orbit, while the European orbiter must make additional engine burns before reaching its own target orbit around March 10, 2027 [4].
