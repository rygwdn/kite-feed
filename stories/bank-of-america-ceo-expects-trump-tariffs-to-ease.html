Bank of America CEO expects Trump tariffs to ease

Category: Business

Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan said he expects President Trump’s tariff-driven trade tensions to start de-escalating in 2026, after a year in which shifting import duties sent “shockwaves” through the US economy [2][3]. In interviews aired on CBS’s “Face the Nation,” Moynihan said Bank of America’s outlook now points to “de-escalation, not escalation,” with tariffs clustering around a clearer, more predictable range for many countries [4][3]. He described this emerging clarity as a potential bright spot for planning, even as some trade relationships remain more complex than others [4][3].

Key Points

  • Tariff baseline: Moynihan said President Trump announced a baseline 10% tariff on all exporters to the US in April [3][4].
  • Midyear escalation: He noted the administration unveiled additional tariffs in July that were expected to push the average rate to about 15.2% for major trading partners if implemented as announced [3].
  • Rate jump estimate: Bloomberg Economics estimated the average US tariff rate rose to about 14% from 2% after President Trump returned to the White House [3].
  • Small business hit: Moynihan said higher tariffs and uncertainty weighed on small businesses in the second quarter, with some relief as rates later eased [3].
  • Labor uncertainty: He added that small businesses are currently more focused on labor availability, as some Trump administration immigration policies “haven’t settled in yet” [3].

  • July: Administration unveiled new tariffs projected to lift averages [3].

Technical Details

  • Baseline tariff: A broad import duty applied widely across countries; Moynihan said the administration set a 10% baseline rate in April [3][4].
  • Non-tariff barriers: Policies other than explicit tariffs that can restrict trade; Moynihan said higher rates could apply to countries that don’t lower such barriers [3].
  • Effective tariff rate: The overall average rate implied across imports; Bloomberg Economics estimated it rose to about 14% from 2% after Trump returned to office [3].

Industry Impact

  • SME planning: Moynihan said small and medium-sized businesses were “shocked” by early uncertainty, suggesting that clearer tariff endpoints could reduce planning friction even if duties remain in place [4].
  • Autos and products: The coverage notes product-specific duties (including automobiles), which can shift costs and sourcing incentives more sharply in targeted sectors than a broad tariff would [4].
  • North America trade: A USMCA review next year could raise compliance and scenario-planning needs for firms that rely on tightly integrated US-Mexico-Canada supply chains [3].

Historical Background

The US, Mexico and Canada replaced NAFTA with the USMCA in 2020, keeping most North American trade tariff-free while adding updated rules in areas like autos and labor standards [common]. In 2025, President Trump introduced a new wave of US import tariffs, including a broad baseline and additional, country- and product-specific duties, which businesses described as disruptive while they tried to forecast final rates [4][3]. Moynihan said that earlier uncertainty affected small and mid-sized firms’ planning, which is why a clearer “endpoint” for tariffs would matter for day-to-day operations [4][3].
