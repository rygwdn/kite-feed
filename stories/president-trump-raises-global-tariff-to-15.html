President Trump raises global tariff to 15%

Category: Canada

U.S. President Donald Trump says he will raise a worldwide tariff to 15 per cent, up from the 10 per cent global tariff he announced after a U.S. Supreme Court ruling struck down key parts of his earlier tariff program [6][4]. The court decision—and the speed of the policy pivot—has renewed uncertainty about which tariffs can stand, what legal authority the White House will lean on next, and how durable any side agreements with trading partners may be [3][11][8]. Several outlets also note that some of the U.S. duties that have hit parts of Canada’s economy hardest fall outside the Supreme Court case and remain in place, so the ruling may change less for Canada than it first appears [9][10]. For Canadian exporters, the White House’s CUSMA carveout means many cross-border shipments can still avoid the global tariff—so long as the goods qualify under the agreement’s rules [2][7][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Fast pivot: Trump moved quickly from a 10 per cent worldwide tariff to 15 per cent, implementing the change by executive order as the administration adjusted after the Supreme Court ruling [2][6].
  • What the ruling didn’t do: The Supreme Court struck down only a specific set of tariffs. Other duties on Canadian goods remain in place, along with other trade measures [3][10].
  • Trade-deal leverage: Some understandings with partners were reached under the threat of now-invalidated tariffs, raising questions about whether those arrangements still hold—and under what terms [11][8].
  • CUSMA is not automatic: The White House carveout applies only if goods qualify under CUSMA rules, putting the focus on origin requirements and the paperwork needed to prove compliance [2][7].

Historical Background

North American trade has been governed by NAFTA since 1994 and by its successor agreement (USMCA/CUSMA) since 2020. The newer pact provides preferential treatment for qualifying goods through rules-of-origin and related certification requirements [common]. In February 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court struck down a set of President Trump’s tariffs, prompting fresh questions about the legal footing for broad duties and the status of trade understandings reached with partners [3][11].
