Anthropic alleges 24,000 fake accounts used Claude to copy capabilities

Category: AI

Anthropic says it uncovered what it calls an “industrial-scale” effort to copy capabilities from its Claude chatbot by abusing both its API and consumer access, and it names three Chinese AI companies: DeepSeek, Moonshot, and MiniMax [3][4][11][25]. Anthropic alleges the operation used roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts and produced more than 16 million Claude queries and exchanges designed to tease out model behavior and performance—an approach often described as model “distillation” via outputs rather than stealing model weights directly [3][4][11][2][25]. The claims arrive amid rising US-China competition over AI leadership and a renewed US debate about tightening export controls on advanced AI chips. Several outlets note that tougher controls could raise the incentive to copy frontier model capabilities through access-based methods [11][25][4]. Anthropic casts the alleged activity as both a business threat and a safety risk, arguing that copying outputs can transfer capabilities without the original model’s safeguards. Critics and commentators respond that the industry already trains on massive external datasets, and that the line between scraping, reverse engineering, and legitimate benchmarking can look blurry without more public evidence—or responses from the accused firms [39][5][43].

Key Points

  • Uneven footprint and timing: Industry roundups say Anthropic’s description suggests wide variation across the named firms (with MiniMax described as much larger than Moonshot and DeepSeek), and note that an active extraction run is often easier to detect than a campaign that has already tapered off [42][5].
  • What wasn’t alleged (and what wasn’t answered): Commentary notes that other prominent Chinese model makers (e.g., Qwen and Z.ai/GLM) weren’t named in Anthropic’s account, and early reports said the accused firms hadn’t publicly responded at the time of publication [42][3].
  • Export-controls wrinkle: Some coverage links distillation worries to a broader point: copying model behavior through APIs could blunt chip export controls by letting capabilities spread even when access to cutting-edge hardware is limited [46][25].
  • Why policing it is difficult: When attackers extract only input/output behavior (not weights), large-scale querying can resemble legitimate evaluation. Providers may need behavioral monitoring, stronger identity checks, and stricter throttling beyond basic per-key rate limits [4][5].
  • Different scales: Anthropic broke out the alleged activity by company, saying DeepSeek accounted for about 150,000 interactions with Claude, compared with more than 3.4 million for Moonshot and about 13 million for MiniMax [50][49].
  • Not the first output-extraction accusation: Community summaries point out that other US labs, including OpenAI, have raised similar (if less public) concerns about competitors using API outputs to improve models—signaling a broader industry worry about “capability transfer” through access rather than breaches [42].
  • Terms and access restrictions: Anthropic and follow-on coverage describe this as more than ToS abuse, saying it also violated regional access restrictions—suggesting the campaigns used workarounds beyond routine account creation [52].
  • How it worked: Anthropic says “sprawling networks” of fraudulent accounts automated high-volume querying—more like credential farming paired with scripted prompt pipelines than normal developer use [3][39][thehackernews.com#1].
  • How identified: Anthropic says it linked the activity to specific firms with “high confidence,” and says other AI industry players have seen similar patterns [49][52].

Perspectives

Historical Background

The US expanded restrictions on exporting advanced chips to China starting in 2022, aiming to limit access to cutting-edge compute tied to military and surveillance uses [common]. In parallel, frontier AI labs have increasingly kept leading models behind hosted APIs rather than broadly releasing weights, making access—and the rules that govern it—a key point of competition and dispute [5][4].
