NASA investigates Voyager 1 data outage slowed by 48-hour command cycle

Category: Science

NASA engineers are investigating why Voyager 1 has stopped returning science and engineering data. The interstellar probe reached one light-day from Earth on Nov. 18. Its carrier signal remains strong, but it has returned no data since Nov. 14. NASA confirmed the distance milestone when a signal arrived at Earth 24 hours after the spacecraft sent it [10][6][3]. At a Nov. 19 briefing, Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd said the spacecraft lacked “frame sync,” preventing the team from receiving its science and engineering readings. Engineers are investigating whether the fault resembles an earlier onboard-computer problem. The carrier signal indicates that the transmitter is still operating and the spacecraft remains pointed toward Earth [10][15]. Each command now takes about a day to reach Voyager 1, and a response takes another day to return. The team said the long signal delay and low data rates slow diagnosis [10][6].

Primary Source: View original article

We do have a very strong carrier signal. We currently don't have a frame sync, so we're not getting the science or engineering data currently.

— Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager (Space.com)

Did you know: Voyager 1’s signal arrived about two seconds later than a simple 24-hour estimate because Earth had moved farther from the spacecraft while the radio waves were in transit [13].

Key Points

  • Remaining instruments: Voyager 1 has two science instruments still operating: a magnetometer that measures magnetic fields and a plasma-wave instrument [9][15].
  • Power limits: Each probe began with about 470 watts from plutonium-powered generators, which lose roughly 4 watts of output annually; engineers have switched off equipment to conserve power [15][14].
  • Twin spacecraft: Voyager 2 is operating with three science instruments and is expected to reach the one-light-day distance in November 2035 [10][11].
  • Golden Record: Both Voyager spacecraft carry a record of sounds and images from Earth, intended as a message for any advanced civilization that might encounter it [3].

Perspectives

Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager: Dodd said she is confident Voyager 1 will resume sending data soon [10].

Sources: Space.com

Joe Westlake, director of NASA's Heliophysics Division: Westlake said the mission’s value goes beyond its outer-planet encounters, describing Voyager as a source of heliophysics insights about the Sun [3].

Sources: ScienceDaily

Bill Nye, chief ambassador of The Planetary Society: Nye used the milestone to argue for continued support of NASA science, saying, “The scientific exploration of space is a bargain” [6].

Sources: SpaceQ

Timeline

  • 5 September 1977: Voyager 1 launched to explore the outer planets after a planetary alignment [14][3]
  • August 2012: Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause and entered interstellar space [14][3]
  • October 2026: Voyager 1 lost contact for about two days, then recovered on its own [14][6]
  • 14 November 2026: The spacecraft stopped returning science and engineering data to Earth [10][6]
  • 18 November 2026: Voyager 1 crossed the one-light-day distance from Earth [3][10]
  • 19 November 2026: A signal reached NASA's team, confirming the distance milestone [6][11]

Scientific Significance

  • Sampling interstellar conditions: Voyager 1's position beyond the heliosphere allows scientists to observe interstellar conditions directly in a region no earlier spacecraft had explored [15][3].
  • Reach of the Sun's influence: Mission scientists are using measurements of the interstellar magnetic field and plasma to investigate how far the Sun's influence extends beyond the heliosphere and where interstellar space begins [14].

Historical Background

The Voyagers were designed to take advantage of an unusual alignment of the outer planets that enabled a sequence of close encounters [3]. Both spacecraft visited Jupiter and Saturn; Voyager 2 went on to make the first close-up explorations of Uranus and Neptune [3].
