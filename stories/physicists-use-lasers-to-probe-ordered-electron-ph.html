Physicists use lasers to probe ordered electron phases

Category: Science

Physicists reported new laser-based observations of ordered electron behavior in quantum materials, including a Wigner crystal study by researchers at the University of Basel and the Technical University of Munich and a separate MIT study of coexisting electron phases in erbium tritelluride [1][2][3]. In the Wigner crystal work, researchers shone light on an atomically thin material cooled close to absolute zero to detect optical signals tied to the positions and collective motion of electrons arranged in a crystal-like pattern [1]. The MIT study, published in Nature Physics, used pump and probe laser beams and photoemitted electrons to track how two charge-density-wave phases form in erbium tritelluride, a rare-earth material [2][3]. The experiments found that two nearly identical electronic patterns can rebuild through fundamentally different mechanisms, even though both involve the same underlying type of electron order [2].

Key Points

  • Wigner challenge: Researchers have had difficulty studying Wigner crystals internally because they could not directly observe how the electrons move collectively, interact and respond to external disturbances [1].
  • Phase geometry: Quantum Zeitgeist described the two electron orders in erbium tritelluride as a dominant stripe pattern and a perpendicular subdominant pattern [3].

Perspectives

University of Basel and Technical University of Munich researchers: The teams used optical measurements to probe a Wigner crystal, rather than relying only on earlier detection methods that showed the state existed but offered limited access to its internal dynamics.

Sources: ScienceDaily

MIT co-author Alfred Zong: Zong said the study provides a new method for probing coexisting phases, which could eventually allow control over electronic behavior for high-performance quantum devices.

Sources: Quantum Zeitgeist

MIT researchers led by Nuh Gedik: The team treated erbium tritelluride as a model system for studying why some materials host multiple phases and how those phases interact when they appear together.

Sources: Quantum Zeitgeist

Historical Background

Eugene Wigner proposed in 1934 that electrons at sufficiently low density could minimize repulsion by forming an ordered lattice now called a Wigner crystal [common]. Charge-density waves have been studied for decades as collective electron-lattice states in low-dimensional materials, making erbium tritelluride a useful platform for probing competing electronic orders [common][3].
