Fossil study finds giant kangaroos could hop

Category: Science

New biomechanical analyses of Ice Age kangaroo fossils suggest several extinct giant species could still hop, challenging the long-held idea that their bulk forced them into mainly bipedal walking [1][4][8][9]. Researchers compared foot and ankle bones from fossil and modern kangaroos and wallabies, concluding the structures were robust enough to handle hopping forces—likely in short, powerful bursts rather than marathon bounds [2][5][10]. The result offers a cheerfully simple takeaway: evolution can “re-engineer” familiar moves in surprising ways, even at megafauna scale [4][9].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: One report describes Procoptodon goliah as reaching about 6.5 feet tall and weighing roughly 550 pounds, making it far larger than today’s biggest kangaroos [9].

Key Points

  • Bone dataset: The team examined hindlimbs from 94 modern specimens and 40 fossil specimens spanning 63 species, including the extinct genus Protemnodon, to link bone geometry with locomotion limits [2][10].
  • Stress testing: They focused on the fourth metatarsal—an elongated foot bone central to jumping—and used its size and shape alongside mass estimates to evaluate whether fossil feet could withstand hopping stresses [2][10].
  • Tendon attachment: By comparing heel bones, the researchers assessed whether giant kangaroos had room for a sufficiently large tendon attachment to tolerate hopping loads without rupture [2][10].
  • Scaling rethink: Several outlets note earlier “too-heavy-to-hop” conclusions often came from simply scaling up modern kangaroo anatomy, which can miss structural differences in extinct lineages [9][10].
  • How they moved: Coverage emphasizes a mixed locomotion picture: even if hopping was possible, the largest forms likely used it sparingly, alongside other ways of getting around [5][8].

Perspectives

Study authors (as described in reports): The fossil hindlimbs appear mechanically capable of hopping, but the biggest animals likely limited it to short bursts.

Sources: ScienceAlert

Prior research view (as summarized): Earlier work widely suggested extinct giant kangaroos were too massive to hop efficiently and instead relied more on bipedal walking.

Sources: New Scientist

Press-briefing summary: Bone strength and heel anatomy indicate tendons and metatarsals could have coped with hopping forces, though sustained hopping seems unlikely at the largest sizes.

Sources: SciMEX

Technical Details

  • Fourth metatarsal: An elongated foot bone associated with kangaroo hopping; researchers used its dimensions to infer whether fossil feet could tolerate hopping stresses [2][10].
  • Calcaneus (heel bone): A key ankle/heel element where major tendons attach; fossil heel size can indicate whether a tendon large enough for hopping loads could plausibly anchor there [2][10].
  • Biomechanical scaling: A method that predicts performance by scaling relationships (like body mass vs. bone strength); reports highlight that direct scaling from modern kangaroos can be misleading if extinct animals had different limb architecture [9][10].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The study refines understanding of locomotion in extinct marsupial megafauna by showing that “too big to hop” was not necessarily true when fossil-specific anatomy is considered [4][9][2].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Confidence in the inference comes from cross-species comparisons using a relatively large mixed dataset (modern plus fossil) and multiple anatomical checks (metatarsals and heel/tendon attachment) rather than a single metric [2][10].
  • Limitations and future work: Because the work infers behavior from bones, it cannot directly measure real gait choices; outlets note the likely outcome is occasional hopping alongside other movement styles, inviting more modeling and additional fossil sampling [5][8][4].

Historical Background

Australia’s Pleistocene hosted multiple lineages of giant kangaroos that later went extinct, leaving modern kangaroos as the largest living hoppers [9][10]. Previous studies often inferred locomotion limits by scaling up modern kangaroo proportions, which these reports say may have underestimated how differently some extinct forms were built [9][10].

Q&A

Q: Which extinct kangaroo lineages were highlighted besides Protemnodon?

A: Coverage mentions multiple Pleistocene giant lineages, including Sthenurinae forms such as Procoptodon goliah, alongside Protemnodon [9][10].

Q: Why do some articles say giant kangaroos might have walked bipedally?

A: Reports describe a previous view that their size made sustained hopping inefficient or mechanically difficult, leading to suggestions of more upright walking—an idea this new analysis revisits using fossil anatomy [8][5].

Q: What time period do the articles associate with these giant kangaroos?

A: Popular accounts place several of the giant forms in the late Pleistocene, with examples discussed around roughly 45,000–50,000 years ago in Australia [9][3].
