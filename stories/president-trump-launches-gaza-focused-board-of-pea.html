President Trump launches Gaza-focused Board of Peace

Category: World

President Trump signed a charter in Davos on Jan. 22 to formally launch a new “Board of Peace,” an international body he says will help oversee a ceasefire and broader peace efforts related to Israel’s war with Hamas, while also aiming to address other conflicts over time [1][3][13]. Leaders and representatives from 19 countries appeared as founding members at the World Economic Forum signing ceremony, with much of the participation coming from the Middle East, parts of Europe, and the Americas [1][9][15]. Several traditional U.S. allies, including the United Kingdom, held back from joining at the launch, citing concerns about the body’s mandate and possible Russian President Vladimir Putin involvement [17][14][15]. Trump presented the board as a potentially influential venue that would work alongside the United Nations, though reporting also noted skepticism and uncertainty about how it will operate in practice [15][3][5].

We’ll work with many others, including the United Nations,

— Donald Trump (Time)

Did you know: Founding members named at the launch included Bahrain, Morocco, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Hungary, Indonesia, Jordan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey and the United Arab Emirates, among others [1].

Key Points

  • Membership terms: Reporting on the draft charter says countries receive three-year membership terms, while states that contribute $1 billion in cash within the first year can obtain permanent membership [15][8].
  • UN linkage: The board grew out of President Trump’s 20-point Gaza ceasefire plan that sources say was endorsed by the UN Security Council, and Trump has framed the new body as working “with many others, including the U.N.” [13][15].
  • Leadership model: Several outlets highlighted that the board’s structure concentrates decision-making authority with President Trump personally, including reported provisions that would let him retain a leadership role beyond his presidency [5][7].
  • Gaza redevelopment pitch: At the Davos event, Jared Kushner presented a pitch for rebuilding Gaza that included ideas such as coastal resorts and energy infrastructure, alongside Trump’s public discussion of “New Gaza” plans [10][6].
  • Russia fee dispute: RFE/RL and Time reported that Putin signaled openness to contributing $1 billion but said Russia would need access to assets frozen in the U.S. to fund such a payment [12][15].

Perspectives

Trump administration / President Trump: The White House presented the Board of Peace as a new mechanism to help maintain a Gaza ceasefire and potentially take on broader conflict-resolution work, with Trump saying many countries want to participate and that it can work alongside the UN.

Sources: NPR

United Kingdom: UK Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper said Britain would not sign on at the launch, citing concern about possible involvement by Russian President Vladimir Putin and questioning his commitment to peace in Ukraine.

Sources: BBC

European leaders (non-participants at Davos): Coverage noted a conspicuous absence of many Western European leaders from the on-stage launch ceremony, reflecting hesitation about the board’s membership and direction.

Sources: Semafor

Skeptical analysts/observers: Some reporting described the initiative as part of a broader effort to reshape post-World War II international arrangements and raised doubts about legitimacy and accountability given the centralized leadership model.

Sources: The New York Times

Timeline

  • November: UN adopted U.S.-penned resolution establishing Board of Peace [15].
  • January 22: Trump signed Board of Peace charter at Davos ceremony [1][14].

Technical Details

  • Founding charter: The Board of Peace launched via a signed charter that establishes its initial membership framework and governing rules [1][4].
  • Paid permanent membership: Reports describe a structure in which permanent membership may require a $1 billion cash contribution within the first year [15][8].
  • Expanded remit: Although tied to Gaza ceasefire efforts at the outset, sources say the board has expanded beyond Gaza to address other conflicts [13][11].

Industry Impact

  • Multilateral diplomacy: The board’s design and rhetoric about potentially rivaling or working alongside the UN have fueled debate over whether it would complement or bypass existing international institutions [2][15].
  • Conflict mediation market: By proposing a paid, invitation-based membership model, the initiative could create incentives for states to seek influence through contributions, a departure from universal-membership bodies highlighted in reporting [15][5].
  • Reconstruction planning: The Davos “New Gaza” redevelopment presentations and proposals indicate continued interest in large-scale rebuilding concepts, though outlets emphasized skepticism and lack of operational detail so far [6][3].

Historical Background

The Board of Peace concept emerged from the Trump administration’s Gaza ceasefire plan, which multiple reports said helped seed the organization’s initial focus on Israel-Hamas-related diplomacy [13][4]. Several outlets describe the board’s broader ambitions as part of a shift away from traditional post-World War II multilateral structures and toward new, U.S.-led arrangements [7][5].

Q&A

Q: How are countries selected or invited to join the Board of Peace?

A: Coverage describes the board as invitation-based, with membership terms and a paid pathway to permanency outlined in reporting on the charter draft [15][8].

Q: Does the Board of Peace have a clear enforcement mechanism for ceasefire compliance?

A: Some outlets noted that President Trump offered limited detail on how the panel would work in practice or implement its mandate [3][4].

Q: Which Western European countries explicitly declined participation, and why?

A: Reporting cited France, Norway and Sweden as saying no, amid wider hesitation from some U.S. allies about the board’s direction and membership [7].
