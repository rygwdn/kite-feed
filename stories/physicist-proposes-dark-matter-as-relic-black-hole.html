Physicist proposes dark matter as relic black holes

Category: Science

A new Physical Review D study, highlighted by Futurism, suggests that dark matter could consist of primordial black holes that survived a previous cosmic cycle rather than some unknown particle [1]. The idea combines a "Big Bounce" model — in which the universe contracts and then expands again — with the possibility that primordial black holes formed under the extreme conditions of the early universe [1]. In this picture, some black holes would predate our universe’s current expansion and could help explain the unseen mass that seems to shape galaxies today [1]. Futurism presents the proposal as a theoretical attempt to explain dark matter within a cyclic-universe scenario [1].

Primary Source: View original article

The universe may not have begun once, but may have rebounded. And the dark structures shaping galaxies today could be relics from a time before the Big Bang.

— Enrique Gaztanaga (Futurism)

Did you know: Some primordial black hole scenarios allow objects that begin at roughly atomic size, far smaller than the star-born black holes most people picture [1].

Key Points

  • Why it appeals: Black holes draw interest as a dark matter candidate because they would let scientists explain the missing mass without invoking a new particle species [1].
  • Primordial route: Unlike stellar black holes, which form after massive stars die, primordial black holes are hypothesized to have formed seconds after the Big Bang and could initially have been as small as an atom [1].

Technical Details

  • Big Bounce cosmology: The Big Bounce is a class of cosmological models in which cosmic expansion follows an earlier contraction phase, replacing a one-time beginning with repeated cycles; Gaztanaga's proposal uses this framework to allow compact objects to survive across cycles [1].
  • Primordial black holes: These hypothetical black holes would form from density fluctuations in the very early universe rather than from stellar collapse, making them a dark matter candidate grounded in gravity and early-universe conditions instead of new particle physics [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The proposal is scientifically interesting because it tries to connect two unresolved problems in one framework: the nature of dark matter and the possibility that the Big Bang was not the universe's only beginning [1].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The coverage describes the idea as theoretical and hypothetical, leaving the central claim unconfirmed [1].

Historical Background

Dark matter became a central problem in astronomy after observations showed that galaxies and galaxy clusters moved as if they contained far more mass than visible matter alone could supply [common]. The Big Bang model became the standard framework in 20th-century cosmology, while cyclic or "bounce" alternatives remained minority ideas that researchers explored as ways to address what, if anything, came before the hot early universe [common]. Primordial black holes have been discussed for decades as one possible dark matter candidate, but they have not displaced particle-based explanations in mainstream research [common].
