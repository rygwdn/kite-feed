COP31 final draft omits fossil fuels, leaving deal at risk

Category: World

COP31 negotiators in Antalya were still seeking consensus on Sunday after a proposed final agreement again omitted an explicit commitment to move away from fossil fuels, the central dispute at the U.N. climate summit [12][32][10]. The draft refers indirectly to a 2023 agreement on the transition, recognizes that the world is on course to exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius warming threshold, and backs a goal of raising electricity’s share of global final energy use to 35% by 2035. It also calls for faster development of carbon-removal technologies [12][2]. The text followed overnight consultations among summit co-presidents Turkey and Australia and delegations from nearly 200 countries. It does not restore the proposed $120 billion annual adaptation-finance target that developing countries had sought, and it sets no requirement that new power for the electricity goal come from renewables [38][4]. Countries still needed to approve the proposed text. The scheduled session was delayed, and reports did not confirm that a final agreement had been adopted [4][10]. Departing delegations could also reduce attendance below the two-thirds quorum needed for a decision [5].

Key Points

  • Carbon capture dispute: The latest text scaled back its commitments to carbon capture, use and storage, which may be enough to ease the EU’s concerns [31].
  • Finance dispute: The latest draft replaces the $120 billion target with further discussions on a pathway to triple adaptation finance, while leaving countries’ contributions voluntary [38].
  • Electricity claims: A document sent anonymously to some delegations from poorer countries portrayed the proposed electrification goal as binding national quotas, although the UN described it as a global target, with countries free to choose their own pathways [1].
  • Import savings: The International Energy Agency estimated that electrification could save developing countries about $100 billion in fossil-fuel imports by 2035 [1].
  • Roadmap gap: The draft also omits a Brazil-led global roadmap for transitioning away from fossil fuels, a demand of several countries and campaign groups [38].
  • Global stocktake: The 2028 global stocktake is intended to assess countries’ climate efforts and shape subsequent emissions-cutting plans [1].
  • Carbon capture: The draft retains an aspirational aim to scale carbon capture, use and storage toward one gigatonne by 2035, despite opposition from delegates and campaigners [38][10].
  • Science timetable: Countries also disagree over when the next Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change assessment reports should appear: several want them before the 2028 global stocktake, while India and Saudi Arabia favor 2029 [14][36].

Perspectives

COP31 President Murat Kurum, Turkey’s environment minister, said he believed the new text could win agreement and urged delegates to weigh it carefully, saying people outside the negotiating hall were already experiencing climate impacts [2][4].

Technical Details

  • CCUS mechanism: The technology captures emissions as they are released and stores them underground [31].
  • Carbon capture record: Critics of the draft’s carbon-capture language point to its high cost and limited record of reducing emissions, and warn that reliance on it could prolong fossil-fuel use [2][26].
  • Power-mix caveat: The proposed 35% electricity-share objective does not require the added electricity to come from renewable sources rather than coal or gas [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Assessment timing: If the next IPCC report arrives after the 2028 global stocktake, that review would lack the panel’s latest synthesis of climate science [1].

Historical Background

At COP28 in Dubai in 2023, governments agreed to transition away from fossil fuels, but did not set targets or deadlines for doing so [8][11]. At COP30 in Brazil last year, the hosts pledged to develop a global roadmap for phasing them out [8].

Q&A

Q: What would happen if delegates cannot approve the text?

A: If consensus fails, negotiations could be suspended until next year; such a breakdown has occurred only once since COP meetings began in 1995 [4].
