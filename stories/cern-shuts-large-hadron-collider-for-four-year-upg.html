CERN shuts Large Hadron Collider for four-year upgrade

Category: Science

CERN switched off the Large Hadron Collider’s beams for Long Shutdown 3, a roughly four-year overhaul of the 27-kilometer accelerator complex beneath the French-Swiss border near Geneva [2][3][4]. The work will convert the machine into the High-Luminosity Large Hadron Collider, with operations scheduled to resume in June 2030 [2][4]. The upgrade focuses on producing far more collisions rather than substantially increasing collision energy: Berliner Zeitung reported that the collision energy will remain about 14 teraelectronvolts while denser proton beams should allow the revamped collider to collect about ten times more data [4]. CERN’s Oliver Brüning said the existing LHC had “exceeded every expectation” and described the shutdown as a transition from the LHC “as we have known it” to its successor [2]. The LHC’s best-known result was the 2012 discovery of the Higgs boson by the ATLAS and CMS detector teams. Particle physicists are still debating what should follow the upgraded LHC, including whether a larger future collider would be worth building [3][4]. The Hindu framed the question as whether the world’s largest science machine can justify a next step after the Higgs boson [3].

Key Points

  • Prior pauses: The collider was previously shut down for upgrades and recalibrations in 2013–2015 and 2015–2018 [2].
  • Scientific record: Popular Science reported that the LHC has also highlighted the behavior of muons and measured antimatter, beyond its Higgs boson work [2].

Timeline

  • 2008: LHC recorded its first beam operations as a subatomic collider [2]
  • 2010: Regular LHC operations began, Berliner Zeitung reported [4]
  • 2012: ATLAS and CMS detected the Higgs boson [4]
  • 2013: The first cited long shutdown period began [2]
  • 29 June 2026: CERN announced the start of Long Shutdown 3 [2]
  • June 2030: High-Luminosity LHC restart is scheduled [4]

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific purpose: Collecting about ten times more data lets researchers test rare processes and small deviations from Standard Model predictions with greater precision, because larger event samples improve many particle-physics measurements [4][common].
  • Limits: A luminosity-focused upgrade does not guarantee the discovery of new particles; it mainly improves the ability to confirm or constrain subtle effects in known channels and rare processes [4][common].

Historical Background

The LHC began after first beam operations in 2008 and proton-proton collision operations in 2010, depending on whether the milestone is defined by commissioning or regular running [2][4].
