Study links mild heart dysfunction to brain microdamage

Category: Science

A study published July 6 in The Journal of Neuroscience linked mild heart dysfunction with microscopic signs of degradation in brain regions involved in memory and associated with Alzheimer’s disease [3][2][4]. Researchers followed 73 patients with an average age of 55 for about three and a half years and found that subtle reductions in heart-pumping function predicted later microstructural changes in the brain [2][4]. Study co-author Dr. Xia Zhang, a doctoral researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, said routine heart-function testing could help clinicians identify people at risk of memory problems earlier [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Brain regions: Neuroscience News said the observed tissue changes clustered in structural networks and temporal-lobe areas tied to memory consolidation and Alzheimer’s disease pathology [4].
  • Imaging gap: The researchers used microstructural integrity measures that Neuroscience News said can detect tissue-level changes not captured by standard scans focused on brain volume or major strokes [4].

Perspectives

Dr. Jan Scheitz: Scheitz, a consultant stroke neurologist and head of the Brain-Heart Lab at Charité University Hospital who was not involved in the study, said the results add to evidence that brain-heart communication matters for healthy aging.

Sources: Live Science

Scimex: Scimex described the work as an international study finding that heart issues may be linked to brain function.

Sources: Scimex

Technical Details

  • Microstructural integrity: The study focused on microscopic tissue degradation rather than gross brain shrinkage, using measures sensitive to gray- and white-matter network changes [4].
  • Subclinical cardiac dysfunction: The cardiac changes described were mild enough to appear in people without a clinical heart-failure diagnosis, making them relevant to risk screening before overt disease [2][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific finding: The study linked reduced pumping function with later microstructural changes in memory-related brain regions associated with Alzheimer’s disease [2][3][4].
  • Uncertainty: The evidence remains associative because the study did not prove that heart dysfunction directly caused memory problems, and the cohort included 73 patients [3][2].
  • Future work: Neuroscience News said the Max Planck team plans trials to integrate specific neural measures after the 3.5-year longitudinal study [4].
