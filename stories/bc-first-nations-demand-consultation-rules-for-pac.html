B.C. First Nations demand consultation rules for Pacific Link pipeline

Category: Canada

First Nations leaders in British Columbia called on Ottawa to set national standards for Indigenous consultation on major projects, telling Major Projects Office CEO Dawn Farrell on Oct. 14 that they had only 11 working days to review the proposed Pacific Link pipeline’s national-interest designation [2][1][3]. The federal government initially allowed one month for consultation on the designation, then extended it to two months after Indigenous leaders raised concerns; Prime Minister Mark Carney and Alberta Premier Danielle Smith designated the pipeline a project of national interest on Oct. 1 [2]. Farrell said she supported the idea of national standards but questioned how they would fit with accelerated project approvals. She acknowledged that First Nations would judge the Major Projects Office by its actions [2]. Six Métis communities in Alberta’s Wood Buffalo region separately said Carney had not met with them before the announcement and that they were excluded from it. They said they had concerns but had not declared opposition to the pipeline [5]. The federal and Alberta governments aim to secure construction approvals by September 2027 [5].

Key Points

  • Pipeline scale: The proposed line would carry one million barrels of oil a day from Alberta’s oilsands to tankers at a deepwater port near Delta, B.C.; it could begin operating in 2032 or 2033 [5].
  • Legislative opposition: B.C. First Nations passed a resolution calling on Carney to stop the Building Canada Act from advancing and repeal Bill C-39 if Parliament passes it; the bill would shorten project reviews and create national-interest zones [2].
  • Rights and process: Lyackson hereditary Chief Shana Thomas said faster approvals must not come at the expense of protections for Aboriginal title, rights, governance, and lands and waters [2].
  • Information gap: The six Métis communities said they were not told about Indigenous ownership stakes before the announcement [5].

Perspectives

B.C. First Nations: Tk’emlúps te Secwépemc Chief Rosanne Casimir said communities receive large volumes of documents late and lack time and funding to assess them, making meaningful consultation difficult [2].
