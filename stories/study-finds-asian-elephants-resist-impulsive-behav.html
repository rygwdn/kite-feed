Study finds Asian elephants resist impulsive behavior

Category: Science

Researchers studying 16 Asian elephants at Thailand’s National Elephant Institute found that the animals could suppress an immediate but ineffective response when trying to reach food inside a transparent box, according to a study [1][2][4][5]. The elephants could see and smell fruit through small holes in the box but usually used an opening on another side instead of trying to reach straight through the blocked front [1][2][4][5]. The study tested inhibitory control, a cognitive ability linked to decision-making and behavioral flexibility in humans and other animals [1][2]. When researchers rotated the box so the opening moved to a new side, the elephants initially used the old route but then changed course; older individuals took longer to switch, and the researchers cited possible age-related cognitive decline as one explanation [2][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Research team: Researchers from the CUNY Graduate Center and Hunter College worked on the study, with Sydney Hope identified as lead author by several sources [1][5][6].
  • Training phase: Before the transparent-box test, researchers taught the elephants to reach around a small solid box to obtain food through an opening [4][5].
  • Human conflict: Study authors said understanding elephant inhibitory control could help guide efforts to reduce conflict between elephants and humans [4].

Perspectives

Sydney Hope: Hope said studying inhibitory control in elephants could help explain how this aspect of cognition evolved across species and how elephants respond to environmental and human-driven changes.

Sources: Popular Science

Study authors: The authors linked the ability to suppress ineffective action to elephant social interactions and problem-solving, not only to food access in the box task.

Sources: Scimex

Scientific Significance

  • Comparative cognition: The findings add Asian elephants to evidence that inhibitory control appears across distantly related birds and mammals, a pattern relevant to studies of how similar cognitive skills evolve in different lineages [2].
  • Scope of evidence: The study tested 16 elephants at one Thai institute, so the results describe performance in a managed setting and do not show how wild elephant populations would perform on comparable tasks [1][2][4].
