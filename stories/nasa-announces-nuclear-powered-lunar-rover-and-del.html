NASA announces nuclear-powered lunar rover and delivery contracts

Category: Science

NASA is developing PROMISE, a nuclear-powered rover designed to map the Moon’s south-polar surface and subsurface and assess resources, including ice, before future crewed missions. Announced Nov. 7 at Kennedy Space Center in Florida, the six-wheeled rover is scheduled to launch in late 2028 for an 18-month mission [2][16][4]. PROMISE will drill about 3 feet (1 meter) below the surface, analyze soil chemistry and measure temperature and radiation. Its nuclear power system is intended to keep it operating through long lunar nights and in permanently shadowed areas, where ice may be present. NASA says its findings will help evaluate landing sites and plan future lunar infrastructure [2][14]. The Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) will build the rover using spare flight hardware and engineering models from the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers. NASA says reusing proven components and operating methods can reduce costs and shorten development. The agency announced the rover alongside lunar science and cargo-transport awards [2][3].

Every piece of equipment we learn to operate there brings that enduring presence a little bit closer to reality.

— Jared Isaacman, NASA administrator (Scientific American)

Key Points

  • Contract ceiling: NASA selected eight companies to compete for future lunar deliveries under a 10-year Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) framework with a combined maximum value of $10 billion. The ceiling is not an award of the full amount [2][3][14].
  • Transport tests: NASA issued six transportation task orders worth about $1.3 billion combined to Arrow Science and Technology, Blue Origin and Impulse Space for cargo integration and transport demonstrations [2][3].
  • Mission leadership: JPL planetary scientist James Keane will lead PROMISE as its project scientist [4].
  • Cameras: PROMISE will carry visible-light cameras to observe the lunar surface [2].
  • Science purpose: NASA science chief Nicky Fox said lunar payloads are intended to help determine how people could survive and operate on the Moon. PROMISE will scout resources near the south pole [4].

Perspectives

Nicky Fox, NASA associate administrator for science: Fox said lunar science should prioritize payload quality as well as quantity [4].

Sources: Scientific American

Carlos García-Galán, Moon Base Program manager: García-Galán said NASA has combined separate efforts into a lunar campaign that develops in stages and is using new contracts and partnerships to speed up technology development [2].

Sources: NASA

Timeline

  • May 2026: NASA awarded Blue Origin a task order for two lunar terrain vehicles [2]
  • 7 November 2026: NASA announced PROMISE and lunar transportation awards at Kennedy Space Center [2][4]
  • 2028: NASA targets a late-year launch for PROMISE’s planned 18-month lunar mission [2][14]

Industry Impact

  • Communications demonstration: NASA selected SpaceX under an existing lunar-logistics contract to demonstrate the deployment of communications satellites from Starship into the region between Earth and the Moon in 2028. The satellites would support networks for crews and lunar infrastructure [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Mission limits: NASA’s plans describe a mission to assess resource potential but do not establish how much ice or other usable material the rover will find [2][14].

Q&A

Q: What happens to the current lunar-delivery contract?

A: NASA’s existing CLPS 1.0 contract remains in place through November 2028, with a further five-year period to complete already awarded deliveries through November 2033 [2][14].

Q: What other lunar transport tests are planned?

A: The task orders cover communications and navigation tests between Earth orbit and the Moon, robotic drones to survey the south-polar surface, and demonstrations of orbital-transfer capabilities in cislunar space [2].
