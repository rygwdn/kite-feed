El Niño fuels Pacific storms as Atlantic lacks hurricanes

Category: Science

An exceptionally strong El Niño is driving sharply different hurricane seasons in the Pacific and Atlantic. By Sept. 25, the Pacific had recorded 41 named storms, including 18 hurricanes, while the Atlantic had produced no hurricanes [1]. Scientists cited El Niño as a key reason for the contrast [1][7]. Hurricane Polo rapidly intensified off Mexico, while Hurricane Nolo strengthened south of Hawaii. Nolo threatened the Big Island with extreme rain, flash flooding and mudslides. Forecasts called for 15 to 30 inches of rain in parts of the Big Island, with higher local totals possible; Nolo was not expected to make landfall [4][13][12][15].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Polo intensity: Polo registered 892 millibars on Sept. 22, making it the eastern Pacific’s second-most intense hurricane on record, with sustained winds near 150 mph [11].
  • Season benchmark: By late September, the Atlantic would typically have produced about four hurricanes, including two major hurricanes, according to the seasonal comparison cited by Grist [6].

Perspectives

Brian McNoldy, a University of Miami hurricane expert, said El Niño was strengthening and could become the strongest on record [7].

Sources: ABC News / Associated Press

Timeline

  • June 2026: El Niño conditions emerged as the Pacific season began [7].
  • 23 September 2026: Nolo strengthened into a tropical storm south of Hawaii [13].
  • 24 September 2026: Nolo rapidly intensified and reached hurricane strength [13].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientists cited El Niño's effects on atmospheric circulation as the likely explanation for the sharp difference in storm activity between the Pacific and Atlantic, while cautioning against attributing individual storms to the climate pattern [1][7].
  • The reported sea-surface-temperature anomaly above 3°C is based on satellite-era measurements dating back roughly half a century; the projected rise toward 4°C was a model expectation, not an observed peak [6].

Humanitarian Impact

The Big Island was under a tropical storm warning and hurricane watch. Maui was under a tropical storm watch [12][15].

Q&A

Q: How close did Nolo come to the Big Island?

A: On Sept. 25, one report placed the storm about 175 miles south of the Big Island, moving generally north [10]. Another report said it was moving at roughly 5 mph [12].
