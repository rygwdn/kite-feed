New Jersey meteorite reveals prebiotic asteroid chemistry

Category: Science

Researchers reported that the Hillsborough meteorite, which crashed through a home in Hillsborough, New Jersey, on July 16, 2024, contains amino acids, carbon compounds and other prebiotic molecules tied to chemistry relevant to the origin of life [3][8][1]. The study, published in Science Advances, identified preserved material from a primitive asteroid and classified it as a rare CM1/2 carbonaceous chondrite, with evidence of extensive water alteration and concentrated salty fluids [6][8]. Scientists said further work on the specimen could clarify how water transformed primitive asteroids and shaped early solar system materials [5][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: The homeowner kept the impact quiet and contacted NASA before researchers began analyzing the bedroom sample [10].

Key Points

  • Quick preservation: The homeowner used disposable gloves, aluminum foil and glass jars to preserve fragments after the impact, helping limit contamination from Earth materials [3][8][1].
  • Rare fall: SETI described Hillsborough as only the second observed fall of this meteorite type and one of the most scientifically valuable specimens recovered [8].

Perspectives

Peter Jenniskens, SETI Institute and NASA Ames: Jenniskens said the fragments preserved material from near the surface of a primitive asteroid where concentrated salty fluids acted, a process he called previously unknown from this type of protoplanet world.

Sources: Slashdot

Mike Zolensky, NASA Johnson Space Center: Zolensky said water on the parent asteroid altered the Hillsborough specimen more extensively than typical CM2 carbonaceous chondrites.

Sources: Slashdot

Queenie Chan and Nana Ogawa: The researchers said isotope evidence in primitive carbonaceous chondrites, including CM types, points to the delivery of organic matter to early Earth.

Sources: Slashdot

Technical Details

  • CM1/2 classification: Hillsborough sits between CM1 and CM2 petrographic types, indicating an intermediate degree of aqueous alteration in a carbonaceous chondrite instead of a typical CM2 specimen [6][8].
  • Brine chemistry: Researchers said high-salt fluids can keep phosphate in solution, catalyze reactions between organic molecules and precipitate minerals, creating chemically active settings relevant to prebiotic synthesis [6].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The finding adds a direct meteorite example of salty-fluid alteration and organic chemistry occurring together in primitive asteroid material, a combination relevant to models of how prebiotic ingredients reached early Earth [6][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The sources report a published laboratory classification and chemical analysis, but they do not provide quantitative uncertainty values, replication status or concentration ranges for the detected amino acids and organic compounds [3][9].
  • Future work: Researchers still need to identify the specific salt minerals in the Hillsborough fragments and compare them with returned asteroid samples from Ryugu and Bennu [6].

Historical Background

Carbonaceous chondrites are primitive meteorites that preserve early solar system material, making them important records of asteroid chemistry [common]. NASA’s OSIRIS-REx mission returned samples from asteroid Bennu to Earth in 2023, and Japan’s Hayabusa2 mission returned samples from Ryugu in 2020 [common][6].
