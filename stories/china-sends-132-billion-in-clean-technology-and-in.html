China sends $132 billion in clean technology and investment to developing countries

Category: Science

China sent $132 billion in clean technology and investment to developing countries last year, by far the largest contribution to carbon dioxide reduction by any country, according to a Reuters analysis. Chinese technology exports alone could help avoid 257 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions annually once deployed; this year’s exports are on track to raise that estimate to 337 million tons [3]. China’s total is close to the $137 billion in climate finance wealthy countries provided to poorer nations in 2024, but the figures are not directly comparable: China’s total largely reflects commercial clean-energy deals, while climate finance also covers adaptation and disaster recovery. Nations meet this month at the U.N. COP31 climate conference in Antalya, where China’s role in climate diplomacy is expected to draw attention [3].

Key Points

  • Export sectors: Subsidies and a price war among Chinese automakers helped expand exports across solar panels, batteries, battery minerals, electric vehicles and wind turbines [3].
  • Fuel economics: Ethiopia banned combustion-engine car imports in 2024. Its deputy transport minister told Reuters that fuel-import costs, rather than climate targets, drove the decision [3].
  • Diplomacy: Former climate negotiators said Beijing may hesitate to lead without Washington and could face pressure to provide more climate aid or curb its own fossil-fuel emissions if it takes a stronger stance [3].

Perspectives

Li Shuo, head of the China Climate Hub at the Asia Society Policy Institute: China’s manufacturing scale lowers clean-technology prices, allowing developing countries to adopt the products for economic reasons, he said [3].

Historical Background

The climate-finance pledge rests on the premise that industrialized nations contributed disproportionately to the climate crisis and have greater capacity to help poorer countries address it [3]. The OECD estimates that private-sector funding accounted for one-fifth of wealthy countries’ climate finance in 2024 [3].
