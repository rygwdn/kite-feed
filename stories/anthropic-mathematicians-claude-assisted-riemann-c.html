Anthropic mathematician's Claude-assisted Riemann claim prompts math departments to adapt

Category: AI

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge says the company’s Claude assistant helped him prove the Riemann hypothesis, a longstanding mathematical claim about prime-number distribution. The claimed proof remains subject to human verification [8][18]. Johns Hopkins University’s mathematics department has issued guidance for Ph.D. applicants and job candidates, saying it expects transparency about AI use and will not give written work positive weight unless candidates demonstrate they understand it [18]. Professor Yiannis Sakellaridis said the guidance was prompted by the risk that candidates could submit AI-generated proofs without learning the underlying mathematics, not by a specific applicant case [18]. Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei rejected the idea that people should hand governance to AI, while David Krueger, founder of the nonprofit Evitable, argued that concern about losing control calls for a halt to advanced AI development [23]. Researchers and technology leaders have debated whether the mathematical advances signal progress toward systems that exceed human capabilities [23][26].

Did you know: More than 12 trillion zeros of the Riemann zeta function have been checked and found on the line specified by the hypothesis, but that does not prove the claim for all zeros [38].

Key Points

  • AGI forecast: Google DeepMind co-founder Shane Legg said he sees a greater-than-50% chance of minimally capable artificial general intelligence arriving before 2028 [23].
  • New computation claim: On Nov. 25, OpenAI said an internal model had established a matrix-multiplication exponent of 2.25. The preprint has not undergone independent review, and the claim does not immediately make computers faster [36][44].
  • AI research batch: OpenAI released 722 AI-generated mathematics papers in October, then retracted some after errors were found [35][39].
  • Department policy: Johns Hopkins professor Yiannis Sakellaridis said the guidance was not prompted by a specific applicant case, but by the risk that candidates could submit AI-generated proofs without learning the underlying mathematics [18].
  • Research costs: OpenAI estimated that its Navier–Stokes effort cost $15 million, well above the $1 million Clay prize [33].
  • Physics benchmark: Theoretical physicist Suvrat Raju said GPT-6 Astra answered 49 of 50 unpublished theoretical-physics problems correctly, saturating the benchmark before its release [5].
  • Physics frontier: Theoretical physicist Edward Witten, who has credited Claude as a research assistant, said physicists are a couple of years behind mathematicians because physics is harder for AI to understand [26].
  • AI-free research: The Association for Human Mathematics, whose members commit to conducting research without AI, has more than 1,000 members [33].

Technical Details

  • Navier–Stokes search: OpenAI described assigning AI agents to separate searches for proofs and counterexamples. One reported approach first removed fluid viscosity, found a singularity in that simplified case, then tested whether a similar result could hold with viscosity included [48].
  • Matrix multiplication exponent: The exponent, written ω, describes how the number of operations needed to multiply large square matrices scales with their size. OpenAI’s claimed value of 2.25 is a theoretical bound, not a demonstrated speedup in deployed computing [44].

Industry Impact

  • Academic publishing: Science-integrity journalist Dalmeet Singh Chawla said publishers would need substantially larger integrity-review teams and more review work to check AI-generated research, warning that current capacity appeared inadequate [41].
  • Mathematics training: Mathematicians are considering shifting doctoral work and classroom assessment toward explaining and contextualizing results, with oral exams and presentations among the proposed approaches [35][39].

Scientific Significance

  • Number theory and applications: The hypothesis bears on estimates of how many primes occur below a given number; prime numbers also underpin digital cryptography [42][47].
  • Conditional results: Some mathematical theorems have already been established on the assumption that the Riemann hypothesis is true; a verified proof would turn those conditional results into unconditional ones [47].

Historical Background

Bernhard Riemann formulated the hypothesis in 1859 as a claim about the zeros of a function linked to the distribution of prime numbers [34][38]. David Hilbert included it among his 23 mathematical problems for the 20th century in 1900 [38]. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute included it among seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million award for an accepted solution [34][38].
