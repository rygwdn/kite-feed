Study finds water triggers reactive chemistry on microplastics

Category: Science

Researchers from Columbia University and the University of California, Berkeley, found that micro- and nanoplastics can react with surrounding water to produce highly reactive molecules, identifying a possible mechanism for biological harm [1][7]. The study, published in Nature on Oct. 14, challenges the assumption that plastic fragments remain chemically inert after breaking down. Because blood and cells contain substantial amounts of water, the team says particles reaching those environments could remain chemically active [1][8]. Experiments and computer simulations suggest that water at the plastic surface helps generate radicals capable of damaging biological molecules. Laboratory tests also found changes in proteins and red blood cells, but the work does not establish the extent of any health effects in people [7][3]. Researchers say the findings could guide efforts to design less reactive plastics. A Columbia team is also studying how particles move through human tissues and enter fetal and offspring tissues in animal models [1].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Tested materials: Researchers examined polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, PVC, PMMA and PET, and found that radical production varied among plastics [7].
  • Cell experiments: In laboratory tests, polystyrene particles altered the structure of the protein lysozyme and promoted aggregation; some red blood cells exposed to the particles deformed or fragmented [7].
  • Caffeine result: Adding caffeine, which can capture free radicals, strongly suppressed protein aggregation in the experiments [7].
  • Possible sources: Researchers identify laundering, microwave heating and sunlight exposure of bottled water as activities that can generate micro- or nanoplastics [1][7].

Technical Details

  • Interfacial electron transfer: The Berkeley team's theoretical calculations and dynamical simulations indicate that hydroxide ions in water layers next to hydrophobic plastic can lose an electron, forming highly reactive hydroxyl radicals [1].
  • Particle surface area: As a plastic fragment becomes smaller, its surface area increases relative to its volume, creating more contact with water where reactions can occur [1][7].

Industry Impact

  • Emissions reduction: Yan is investigating engineering measures to reduce micro- and nanoparticle emissions, including removing microfibers from washing machines and dryers [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Prior research context: The Columbia article cites later studies reporting links between micro- and nanoplastic exposure and heart attack, stroke, dementia and brain tumors; the current study proposes a mechanism but does not establish that particles caused those conditions [1].

Historical Background

In a 2024 study, some of the same researchers used laser-based microscopy to find an average of about 240,000 detectable plastic fragments per liter of bottled water, most of them nanosized. The count was 10 to 100 times earlier estimates [1][7]. The mechanism behind the particles' possible biological effects remained unclear [1].

Q&A

Q: Did the researchers detect radical formation only in pure water?

A: No. The researchers also reported radical formation in more complex environments, including serum [7].

Q: What healthcare-related activity did Columbia identify as a potential source of microplastics?

A: The article lists IV injections in healthcare settings among activities that can generate microplastics [1].
