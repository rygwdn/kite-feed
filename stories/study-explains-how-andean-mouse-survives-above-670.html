Study explains how Andean mouse survives above 6,700 meters

Category: Science

A study published in Science reports that the Andean leaf-eared mouse, Phyllotis vaccarum, lives above 6,700 meters in the Andes, making it the mammal with the highest documented habitat and one of the broadest known elevational ranges, from sea level to high volcanic summits [6][5][2]. The work focuses on mice found near 7,000 meters, including those on Llullaillaco volcano on the Chile-Argentina border, where air pressure gives each breath about 44% of the oxygen available at sea level and temperatures stay below freezing for most of the year [8][10][9]. Researchers found that high-altitude populations combine physiological and genetic adaptations that help them produce heat and function in low-oxygen conditions [2][11][5]. The study describes enhanced thermogenic capacity in highland mice, changes tied to oxygen use and mitochondria, and adaptations for surviving in a cold, rocky environment with limited food, including poisonous plants [6][9][4].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Lab measurements: The international research team collected Andean leaf-eared mice from sea level to above 6,700 meters and exposed them in the lab to low-oxygen conditions, measuring metabolic rate, body temperature, and gene expression [5].
  • Past evidence: Researchers captured a live mouse on Llullaillaco in 2020 and found 13 naturally mummified specimens on three nearby volcanic summits [8].
  • Human contrast: Well-trained, acclimatized climbers can tolerate those low-oxygen summit conditions only briefly, while the mice live there [8][10][9].

Perspectives

Grant McClelland: Study co-author Grant McClelland said adaptation to severe environments involves multiple challenges, not just the most obvious stressor.

Sources: RTVI

Technical Details

  • Thermogenesis under hypoxia: Highland populations preserved heat-production capacity when researchers exposed them to oxygen levels equivalent to elevations near 7,000 meters, a key distinction from lowland mice tested across the same species gradient [2][11].
  • Mitochondrial adaptation: The study links survival to changes in mitochondria, the cellular structures that support energy production needed for sustained heat generation in cold, oxygen-poor environments [9].
  • Elevational gradient design: Because Phyllotis vaccarum occurs from sea level to summits above 6,700 meters, researchers could compare highland and lowland populations within one species instead of comparing only unrelated mammals [6][11].

Historical Background

Archaeologists in the 1970s and 1980s found mouse remains among Inca ruins on Andean summits and initially thought people had carried the rodents there because scientists considered mammal life at those elevations impossible [8]. The Andean leaf-eared mouse later displaced the long-eared pika of Central Asia as the highest-dwelling mammal, exceeding that previous benchmark by at least 500 meters [8].
