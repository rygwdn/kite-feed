Researchers unveil Lumina simulation of universe’s first two billion years

Category: Science

Researchers at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian have developed Lumina, a large-scale computer simulation of how ordinary matter, dark matter and radiation interacted during the universe’s first 2 billion years [1][2][3]. It covers a region about 1.5 billion light-years across, modeling more than 100 times the volume of comparable earlier simulations [1][2]. The model follows the emergence of early galaxies and the changes they caused in surrounding gas, producing predictions astronomers can compare with observations from the James Webb Space Telescope and other instruments [1][3]. The research team plans to use Lumina to study gas flows between galaxies and the development of intense star-forming regions [1].

Primary Source: View original article

Lumina does everything from small-scale star and galaxy formation to large-scale re-ionization of hydrogen and helium, in a volume that is 100 times larger than previous best simulations

— Mark Vogelsberger, professor of physics at MIT (MIT News)

Key Points

  • Hydrogen and helium: Lumina indicates that ultraviolet light from early stars and galaxies likely reionized hydrogen, while energetic radiation from actively accreting supermassive black holes drove helium reionization [2].
  • Computing scale: The calculation tracked about half a trillion dark-matter particles and gas cells on the Frontier supercomputer, using the equivalent of more than 100,000 CPU cores and around 15,000 graphics processors at peak scale [2].
  • Starting point: Lumina begins about 45 million years after the Big Bang, during the cosmic dark age, with initial conditions based on physicists’ theories of what existed then [2].

Technical Details

  • Radiation hydrodynamics: Lumina combines a galaxy-formation model derived from IllustrisTNG with radiation transport to calculate how ultraviolet light moves through and alters gas as galaxies form [2].
  • Multi-scale resolution: The simulation resolves structures ranging from a cosmic region approximately 500 megaparsecs wide to individual developing galaxies, with about 30 times the resolution of IllustrisTNG [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Reionization milestone: Lumina is the first simulation to capture helium reionization as it occurred while allowing its effects to influence the evolving universe immediately [1].

Historical Background

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, neutral hydrogen absorbed light and left the universe in a period known as the cosmic dark age [2]. Later, radiation from the first stars and galaxies began ionizing the hydrogen, allowing light to travel more freely [2].
