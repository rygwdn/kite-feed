NASA images SpaceX Falcon 9 crater on the moon

Category: Science

NASA released before-and-after images showing a new lunar crater created when a SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage hit the moon on Aug. 5, 2026 [11][12][15][16][17]. Images taken by NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter on Aug. 11 and 12 show the crater is about 60 feet, or 18 meters, wide and less than 10 feet, or 3 meters, deep [3][5][9][11][12][15][13][16][17][22][24][26]. The upper stage was part of a Jan. 15, 2025, launch carrying private lunar landers, including Firefly Aerospace’s Blue Ghost 1. It later remained in a moon-crossing orbit before its trajectory shifted toward the lunar surface [5][6][9][12][13][16][38]. The rocket stage struck near Einstein Crater at about 5,400 mph, or 8,700 kph, after drifting through space for more than a year [11][15][13][9][22][24]. NASA said darker streaks radiating from the crater consist of near-surface dust and rock altered over time by solar wind, cosmic rays and micrometeorite impacts, while brighter rays mark fresher material excavated from deeper layers [15][12][16][17][22][21][31]. South Korea’s Danuri lunar orbiter published earlier images of the site on Aug. 6, before NASA released the sharper LRO views on Aug. 18 [12][22][24][28][38].

Did you know: ESO’s Very Large Telescope detected lithium in the impact plume, which FAZ reported is scarce in lunar soil and likely came from the rocket stage [27].

Key Points

  • Imaging challenge: NASA engineers tilted LRO toward the crater during passes roughly 60 miles, or 96 kilometers, above the surface while the spacecraft traveled about 1 mile per second [8][15][12][13][26].
  • Timing precision: NASA said a camera exposure 10 seconds early or late would have shifted the target about 10 miles, or 16 kilometers, off center [12][28][26][41].
  • Earlier tracking: NASA worked with South Korea’s aerospace agency using Danuri observations to aim LRO’s narrow-angle camera at the impact region [38][44].
  • Orbit cause: Solar radiation and lunar gravity helped push the discarded upper stage away from Earth and onto a collision path with the moon [5][13][29].

New York University Abu Dhabi researcher: A principal investigator at NYU Abu Dhabi’s Centre for Astrophysics and Space Science said the episode highlights risks astronauts could face during extended stays at future lunar bases.

Timeline

  • 15 January 2025: Falcon 9 launched private lunar landers [5][9].
  • 5 August 2026: Upper stage struck near Einstein Crater [9][17].
  • 6 August 2026: Danuri published early images of the crash site [12][22].
  • 11 August 2026: LRO began imaging the crater during targeted passes [3][12].
  • 12 August 2026: LRO completed the image series used for measurements [5][16].
  • 18 August 2026: NASA released the before-and-after image set [11][17].

Scientific Significance

  • Impact physics: The event gave researchers a rare recent artificial impact in which the incoming object, trajectory and approximate mass were constrained before imaging, allowing direct comparison between predicted impact zones and the observed crater [6][9][21].
  • Operational limits: LRO’s imaging campaign required days of geometry, pointing and timing alignment to inspect a known debris impact site [12][28][41].

Historical Background

NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has operated at the moon since 2009, making it one of the main spacecraft for repeated high-resolution surveys of changes on the lunar surface [12][28]. The Falcon 9 mission that produced the discarded upper stage supported NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services program by launching private lunar payloads in January 2025 [11][15][22][24].
