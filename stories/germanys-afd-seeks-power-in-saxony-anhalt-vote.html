Germany’s AfD seeks power in Saxony-Anhalt vote

Category: World

Voters in Saxony-Anhalt are choosing a new state parliament on September 6 in an election that could give the Alternative for Germany, or AfD, Germany’s first far-right state government since World War II [10][11][14][1][29]. Recent surveys put the AfD at about 40% to 43%, ahead of Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s Christian Democratic Union, or CDU, at roughly 22% to 23% [5][14][1][17]. The AfD could govern only by winning an absolute majority or finding a coalition partner, because mainstream parties have maintained a “firewall” against cooperation with it [14][20][52][61]. The outcome depends partly on whether smaller parties clear the 5% threshold to enter the Landtag, and weeks of coalition talks could follow if no party wins a majority [14][20][36]. Incumbent CDU state premier Sven Schulze remains the likely route to another government if the AfD falls short of a majority [14]. The CDU has led Saxony-Anhalt since 2002. The AfD is the biggest opposition party in the Bundestag, and the result could put pressure on Merz’s federal coalition with the Social Democrats [2][3][29]. Saxony-Anhalt, west of Berlin, has about 2.1 million residents, and roughly 1.7 million people are eligible to vote [29][2][17].

Key Points

  • Lead candidate: Ulrich Siegmund, 35, the AfD’s top candidate, was previously a CDU member and predicted an absolute majority at a final campaign event [14][33][7].
  • Security classification: Saxony-Anhalt’s state Office for the Protection of the Constitution has classified the local AfD branch as a confirmed far-right extremist organization, a label the party rejects [1][29].
  • Campaign issues: The AfD campaigned on restricting immigration, restoring ties with Russia and leaving the euro. Merz has taken a tougher line on migration and security to counter the party’s rise [25][55][4].
  • Opposition protest: Police said about 6,000 people demonstrated peacefully in Magdeburg on September 5 for a Saxony-Anhalt “open to the world” [34][41][37].

Perspectives

AfD national co-leaders: Alice Weidel and Tino Chrupalla said the party wants to apply existing law on illegal migration and argued that an absolute majority was almost the only way to change policy.

Historical Background

The AfD began as a euro-skeptic protest party and later shifted to an anti-immigration platform, gaining particular strength in eastern Germany [1]. Saxony-Anhalt, with about 2.1 million residents, is one of Germany’s smaller states; its economy accounts for less than 2% of national GDP [21].
