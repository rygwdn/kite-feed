NASA sets Roman telescope launch for Aug. 30

Category: Science

NASA is preparing to launch the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, its next flagship space observatory, at 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday, Aug. 30, from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center in Florida aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket [9][4][15]. The roughly $4 billion mission is scheduled nine months ahead of its original schedule, according to NASA officials cited in Reuters-based reports [8][11][13][6]. Scientists plan to use Roman to investigate dark energy and dark matter, test gravity at large cosmic scales, search for planets beyond the solar system, and study how galaxies formed and evolved over cosmic time [2][8][11][15]. After separating from the rocket, Roman will travel toward Lagrange Point 2, about 1 million miles, or 1.6 million kilometers, from Earth [8][11][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Roman’s 2.4-meter primary mirror was donated to the project by the U.S. National Reconnaissance Office [16].

Key Points

  • Survey scale: Roman’s 2.4-meter mirror matches Hubble’s, and its 300-megapixel Wide Field Instrument can capture at least 100 times more sky at once with comparable sensitivity and sharpness [4][9][2].
  • Planet census: Scientists expect Roman to discover thousands of new planets and produce a statistical census of planetary systems that resemble the solar system [7].
  • European hardware: ESA is providing Roman’s star trackers, batteries, coronagraph detectors and communications support, including data downloads through a 35-meter antenna in New Norcia, Australia [15].
  • Mission duration: Roman’s planned mission lasts five years, and NASA says the observatory may carry enough fuel for five additional years [7].

Perspectives

NASA Ames Research Center: NASA Ames scheduled virtual media interviews for Aug. 26, from 10 a.m. to 1 p.m. PDT, on its Roman-related software, image processing and mission operations work.

Sources: NASA

European Space Agency: ESA said its Mission of Opportunity status gives Europe a role in science planning in addition to hardware and ground-station contributions.

Sources: ESA

North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences: The museum in Raleigh planned a free launch party from 7 to 8:30 a.m. Sunday, with NASA volunteers guiding visitors through the livestream on a 40-foot-tall screen.

Sources: WRAL

Timeline

  • 1990: NASA launched the Hubble Space Telescope into orbit [8][7].
  • 2021: NASA launched the James Webb Space Telescope [8][7].
  • 7 August 2026: Technicians attached Roman to payload adapter hardware [11].
  • 30 August 2026: Roman is targeted to launch from Kennedy Space Center [9][15].

Technical Details

  • ROSALIA stray-light software: NASA Ames’ ROSALIA tool predicts and removes unwanted light from Roman images after photons scatter inside the telescope’s optics, reducing glints that can mimic planets or nebulae [2].
  • Multi-Star Wavefront Control: The technology is designed to suppress overlapping glare from multi-star systems so Roman’s coronagraph can reveal otherwise hidden exoplanets close to nearby stars [3].
  • Ground data pipelines: NASA’s Advanced Supercomputing Division is advising on Roman’s ground-based systems and operations to support efficient processing and preserve science-data quality [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Cosmic expansion tests: Roman will conduct fast infrared scans that ESA says can track the early universe and its expansion over time, adding a survey dataset alongside ESA’s Euclid mission [15].
  • Survey speed: Roman can map the sky roughly 1,000 times faster than Hubble, allowing it to complete large-area surveys in months that would take Hubble millennia [16].
  • Pre-launch limitation: The mission’s cosmology and exoplanet findings depend on launch, commissioning and science-data processing; NASA Ames is focused partly on maintaining the quality and integrity of resulting science data products [3].

Historical Background

Roman is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA’s first chief of astronomy, who is often called the mother of Hubble [4][16]. The observatory follows the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope, both of which are still operating. Hubble received five servicing missions from 1993 to 2009, a maintenance model not available to more distant observatories at Lagrange Point 2 [9][common].
