Researchers link 40,000-year-old symbols to proto-writing

Category: Science

Multiple research teams say repeated geometric markings carved on Ice Age bone, ivory, and stone objects in Europe may have carried information—closer to “proto-writing” than simple decoration [1][7][6]. The work focuses on artifacts dated to roughly 40,000 years ago, including finds from cave sites in Germany’s Swabian Jura, where lines, crosses, dots, and notches show up in recurring sequences across many objects [3][4][5]. To see whether the marks show structure beyond attractive patterning, researchers built a database of thousands of signs and ran computational and statistical tests that compare repetition and measured “complexity” with later writing-like systems such as proto-cuneiform [2][3][4]. The coverage lands on a hopeful idea: long before full writing, early Homo sapiens may already have been experimenting with durable ways to store and share meaning—adding depth to what we know about creativity and communication in deep prehistory [7][6][1].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Objects studied: Reporting describes researchers analyzing 260 artifacts and logging more than 3,000 individual geometric engravings, including marked figurines and other carved items from the Swabian Jura [3][4][5].
  • Notable examples: Popular Science points to a small mammoth figurine carved from mammoth tusk and the well-known ivory “Adorant” figure—both among the objects that also carry repeating engraved motifs [3].
  • Corpus approach: Instead of interpreting any single object in isolation, the researchers looked for short sign sequences that recur across many portable cave-site artifacts—repeated arrangements of lines, crosses, dots, and notches [3][5].
  • Headline statistical result: Some coverage highlights that the engravings’ measured sequence “complexity” resembles the structure seen in later proto-writing datasets used as benchmarks, including proto-cuneiform [2][6].
  • Interpretive range: Articles note that even if the markings don’t encode full speech, they could still have worked as practical memory aids—for tracking repeated information such as quantities or identities—which is why researchers treat them as potentially information-bearing [4][7].

Perspectives

Study team (Ewa Dutkiewicz, Museum of Prehistory and Early History, Berlin): Dutkiewicz said the artifacts come from a period “tens of thousands of years before the first writing systems,” and tie to the era when Homo sapiens spread into Europe and encountered Neanderthals.

Sources: Popular Science

Study team (Christian Bentz, linguist): Bentz framed the work as an empirical test of “basic, measurable characteristics of the signs,” rather than an attempt to read specific messages.

Sources: Popular Science

New Scientist analysis: New Scientist describes the engravings as possible “proto-writing” that predates a “full writing system” by tens of thousands of years, reflecting cautious wording about what the evidence can support.

Sources: New Scientist

Scientific American coverage: Scientific American emphasizes that the markings may have been more than “doodles,” while treating the claim as suggestive rather than settled.

Sources: Scientific American

Scientific Significance

  • Cognitive significance: Science notes the findings fit with the idea that early European Homo sapiens already had cognitive tools relevant to language and symbol use when they engraved the objects [7].
  • Methodological significance: Several articles frame the work as a corpus-style, quantitative approach to an archaeological problem—testing whether ambiguous marks behave more like an organized sign system than like ad hoc decoration [7][1].
  • Limitations and future work: Articles emphasize the need for more datasets and broader comparisons across regions and time periods, to see whether similar statistical signatures show up elsewhere and whether the patterns hold as the corpus grows [4][7].

Historical Background

The earliest widely accepted full writing systems—such as cuneiform in Mesopotamia and Egyptian hieroglyphs—appear in the late 4th millennium BCE [common]. Archaeologists have long recognized “proto-writing” systems: symbol sets that convey limited information without encoding full spoken language [common]. Because proto-writing can take many forms, debates often hinge on whether a sign repertoire shows consistent, repeated use across many objects and contexts—even when the signs can’t be “read” as language [6][7][1].
