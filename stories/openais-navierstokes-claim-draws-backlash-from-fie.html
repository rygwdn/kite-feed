OpenAI’s Navier–Stokes claim draws backlash from Fields medalists

Category: AI

OpenAI said on September 8 that an unreleased model had found a solution to the Navier–Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems. The company said the effort involved thousands of autonomous AI agents working over 88 hours. The problem concerns whether three-dimensional fluid equations can develop singularities, and the claimed result has not yet completed the mathematical community’s scrutiny [2][18][22]. The announcement prompted 25 Fields Medalists, including Terence Tao, June Huh and Peter Scholze, to issue a September 11 declaration warning that AI companies’ race to use difficult problems as performance benchmarks conflicts with mathematics’ emphasis on conceptual understanding, attribution and human discussion [31][18]. NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also accused OpenAI of pressuring him over credit for related work involving Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpoge and questioned whether OpenAI had benefited from research shared through its Codex tool. OpenAI denied accessing or using Buckmaster’s work [1][6][17].

The goals of the AI companies and the goals of the mathematical community are severely misaligned.

— Terence Tao and 24 other Fields Medalists (Terence Tao’s blog)

Did you know: Before OpenAI’s announcement, a chatbot from the company reportedly refuted an 80-year-old conjecture associated with mathematician Paul Erdős; that result had not been verified [37].

Key Points

  • Compute scale: OpenAI said its agents exchanged 2.7 million messages and generated about 130 billion tokens during the effort [28][26].
  • Research credit: Buckmaster said OpenAI project lead Sébastien Bubeck offered authorship, computing resources and a possible share of the $1 million prize if Buckmaster excluded Alpoge; Bubeck denied threatening him and OpenAI rejected the allegations [33][19].
  • Research record: After the dispute, Buckmaster published three papers describing his interactions with OpenAI [33].
  • Scope of warning: The Fields signatories said the mismatch between AI companies and mathematicians also threatens intellectual work beyond mathematics [18][21].
  • Caltech dispute: OpenAI withdrew sponsorship from Caltech’s Mathathon after Caltech researchers warned that the event could harm the mathematical community [6][1].
  • Formal checking: Quanta reported that the claimed proof was checked in Lean, while noting that further scrutiny remains necessary before mathematicians accept it [2].
  • Human research path: Buckmaster and Alpöge’s related work built on earlier strategies developed by Spanish mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa [53][33].

Technical Details

  • Incompressible flow: The disputed problem concerns fluids with constant density, including water and blood, rather than compressible fluids such as air. The mathematical question is whether a smooth solution can later develop infinite velocity or pressure [64].
  • Lean formalization: OpenAI’s reported proof was formalized and checked in Lean, a proof-assistant system that can verify whether specified logical steps follow from encoded definitions and prior theorems [2].
  • Computational scale: OpenAI’s reported run used millions of dollars’ worth of computing power to search for the claimed result [2][46].
  • Agent orchestration: The project used up to 10,000 autonomous agents working in parallel rather than a single model producing one uninterrupted proof [2][22].
  • Formal verification: Lean is a proof-assistant environment that can verify formally stated logical steps, but formal checking alone does not explain the proof or show how it relates to earlier work [2][1].

Industry Impact

  • Open research: Researchers have raised concerns that labs with access to tens of millions of dollars in computing power could foster secrecy and discourage scholars from sharing unfinished ideas [1][33].
  • Signatory breadth: The warning was signed by Fields Medalists spanning nearly five decades, from Pierre Deligne, who won in 1978, to Yu Deng, honored in 2026 [18][21].

Scientific Significance

  • Integration: They warned that AI-generated ideas need mathematicians to develop and incorporate them into established knowledge; without that work, the field’s human chain of transmission could break [31].
  • Educational role: The signatories said problem-setting helps students develop skills, making students and ideas central resources of the mathematical community [31].
  • Potential mathematical advance: If independent scrutiny confirms the argument, it would be the most consequential proof attributed to an AI system and would establish a new computational route into a problem that has resisted mathematicians for generations [2].
  • Prize status: The Clay Mathematics Institute requires independent acceptance of a submitted proof, so the announcement does not itself establish that the prize criteria have been met [2][64].
  • Historical role: The declaration describes famous problems as landmarks and lighthouses for measuring progress in mathematical understanding; solving one traditionally signals new insights or methods for the community to study [31].

Historical Background

The Clay Mathematics Institute established the seven Millennium Prize Problems in 2000, offering $1 million for each solution [2]. Until OpenAI’s announcement, the Poincaré conjecture was the only solved problem, proved by Grigori Perelman in 2002–03 after years of work [37]. The Navier–Stokes problem concerns equations arising from 19th-century work by Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes [55].
