Alberta chief justices defend court independence after Smith remarks

Category: Canada

Alberta’s three chief justices issued a rare public statement emphasizing that judicial independence is foundational to democracy, after Premier Danielle Smith said she wished she could “direct” judges [2][4]. The justices said democracy works only when the legislative, executive and judicial branches operate independently and respect each other’s roles [2][4]. The exchange has focused attention on how elected governments communicate with courts, and on the guardrails meant to keep decisions in individual cases free from political direction [2][1].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Alberta’s provincial court system includes three levels: the Alberta Court of Justice, the Court of King’s Bench, and the Court of Appeal [4].

Key Points

  • Rare signatories: The statement was signed by Acting Chief Justice Dawn Pentelechuk (Court of Appeal), Chief Justice Kent Davidson (Court of King’s Bench) and Chief Justice James Hunter (Alberta Court of Justice), underscoring that all three provincial courts spoke together [4].
  • Respect message: The chief justices framed their intervention as a call for mutual institutional respect, saying the three branches must each fulfill distinct responsibilities for the system to function [2].
  • Smith wording: The judges’ response followed Smith’s comment that she wished she could “direct” judges, a phrasing that prompted questions about boundaries between elected leaders and the courts [1][2][4].
  • Civic reassurance: By speaking publicly, the justices signalled to Albertans that courts view independence as a practical safeguard for fair hearings, not an abstract principle [2][4].

Technical Details

  • Judicial independence: A constitutional principle that judges must be free from improper influence by the executive or legislature when deciding cases, which the Alberta chief justices described as essential to democracy [2][4].
  • Separation of powers: The division of responsibilities among the legislative, executive and judicial branches, which the justices said requires independence and mutual respect to function well [2].

Historical Background

Canada’s courts are designed to be independent from elected governments so that judges can apply the law without political pressure [common]. In practice, this means governments make laws and administer policy, while courts interpret laws and decide individual cases; tensions can surface when political leaders criticize rulings or comment on how judges should decide matters [common]. The Alberta chief justices’ joint statement reflects a long-standing convention that judges speak publicly only rarely, typically to protect confidence in the justice system [2][4].

Q&A

Q: What does “directing” judges mean in Canadian governance terms?

A: In Canada, elected governments can set policy and pass legislation, but they are not meant to instruct judges on how to rule in specific cases because courts must decide matters independently [common].

Q: How often do chief justices issue public statements like this?

A: The articles describe the Alberta statement as a rare public intervention by the province’s top judges, indicating it is not a routine response to political debate [2][4].
