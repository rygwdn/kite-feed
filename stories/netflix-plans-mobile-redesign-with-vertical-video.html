Netflix plans mobile redesign with vertical video feed

Category: Technology

Netflix says it will redesign its mobile app and add deeper integration of TikTok-style vertical video clips, following months of testing the swipeable format for show and film promos [8][6][3]. Co-CEO Greg Peters described the new mobile UI as a platform the company can "iterate, test, evolve" as Netflix expands its business, including newer formats like video podcasts [2][5][8]. The changes aim to improve discovery on phones and help Netflix compete for attention with social platforms such as Instagram and TikTok, which executives cited as key rivals [2][3][8]. Rollout timing varies by report, with some outlets saying later in 2026 and others describing it as coming later this year [2][8][3][4][5].

Primary Source: View original article

We’re gonna roll this out later in 2026. And just like our TV UI, it then becomes a starting point. It becomes a platform for us to continue to iterate, test, evolve, and improve our offerings.

— Greg Peters (Mashable)

Did you know: Engadget reports Netflix pulled in $1.5 billion in ad revenue in 2025 from its lower-priced subscription tier [8].

Key Points

  • Video podcasts: Peters said the vertical feed could surface clips tied to "new content types, like video podcasts," as Netflix expands into that format [8][2].
  • Podcast lineup: Engadget reports Netflix’s early video podcast push includes originals featuring Michael Irvin and Peter Davidson, plus hosted shows such as The Bill Simmons Podcast and other Ringer programs [8].
  • Business scale: Engadget says Netflix ended 2025 with 325 million paid subscribers worldwide and reported $45.2 billion in revenue, including $1.5 billion in ad revenue from its lower-priced tier [8].
  • Industry echoes: PCWorld notes Disney+ has also discussed adding vertical videos, framing the format as a way to become a more habitual daily destination for viewers [7].
  • User concerns: Several outlets flag that some subscribers worry a new mobile UI could feel cluttered or drive more "doomscrolling," even as Netflix positions the feature as a discovery tool [1][9].

Perspectives

Netflix leadership: Netflix says the mobile redesign will support long-term business expansion and create a foundation for ongoing experimentation and improvements, including integrating clips for new formats like video podcasts [2][8].

Sources: Mashable

Tech critics/commentators: Some tech outlets argue the move risks importing social-style addictive scrolling into a subscription streaming app, and they question whether users want vertical shorts in the Netflix experience [1][4][7].

Sources: PCWorld

Mobile users/subscribers: TechRadar characterizes the planned redesign as something some subscribers are "bracing" for, reflecting skepticism after prior interface changes [9].

Sources: TechRadar

Timeline

  • May last year: Netflix began experimenting with a vertical video clip feed [6].
  • The last six months: Netflix tested vertical videos for promos in its mobile app [8].
  • Tuesday (January 20th): Netflix discussed the mobile redesign during its earnings call [6].
  • Later in 2026: Co-CEO Greg Peters said Netflix will roll out the new mobile UI [2].

Technical Details

  • Vertical video feed: A full-screen, portrait-oriented clip stream that lets users swipe between short previews, similar to TikTok or YouTube Shorts [8][3][6].
  • Discovery funnel: Netflix uses short clips as a "top-of-funnel" discovery tool to nudge viewers from previews into full episodes or films, according to descriptions of how the feature promotes shows and movies [8][6].
  • Iterative UI platform: Netflix frames the redesign as a modular interface it can A/B test and evolve over time, rather than a one-off cosmetic refresh [2][5].

Industry Impact

  • Streaming UX: Short-form feeds may push streamers to redesign around mobile-first discovery, especially as Netflix explicitly names Instagram-style competition for attention [2][3].
  • Creator economy: By promoting video podcasts and clip-friendly programming, Netflix could compete more directly with YouTube’s creator-led discovery model [8].
  • Advertising products: If more engagement happens in a feed, streamers may gain new surfaces for promos and potentially ad formats, reinforcing the importance of Netflix’s ad revenue stream mentioned in earnings coverage [8].
  • Competitor responses: PCWorld notes Disney+ leadership has discussed vertical video experimentation too, suggesting broader competitive pressure to adopt the format [7].

Historical Background

Vertical, swipeable short-form video became a dominant mobile format through apps like TikTok and Instagram Reels, which trained users to discover content via rapid feeds [common]. Several outlets note Netflix has already been testing vertical clips in its mobile app for months as a discovery and promotion mechanism, and the upcoming redesign formalizes and expands that direction [8][6][4].

Q&A

Q: Will Netflix let users turn off the vertical video feed?

A: The articles describe the feature and redesign but do not mention an on/off toggle or an opt-out setting [8][2][3].

Q: Will the vertical feed include content beyond Netflix originals?

A: Reports say the clips have promoted Netflix films and TV shows so far, and Peters suggested they could expand to clips for video podcasts; the coverage does not specify licensing-based inclusion rules [8][2].

Q: Does this mean vertical videos will appear on TVs too?

A: The sources focus on a mobile redesign; commentary about "living room" vertical video appears as an industry concern, not as a confirmed Netflix TV-app plan [7][2].

Action Items

  • Take control of notifications: In the Netflix app, review notification settings so promos from new mobile features do not interrupt your routine [common].
  • Use discovery intentionally: If the vertical feed rolls out to you, treat clips as a shortlist tool—add items to My List rather than endlessly swiping, then watch from the list later [common].
  • Compare app experiences: Try the TV interface and the mobile interface side-by-side to see whether the redesign improves finding something you actually want to watch [common].
