Global study finds around 40% of cancers preventable

Category: Science

An international team led by the World Health Organization’s cancer agency has estimated that roughly four in ten new cancer cases worldwide in 2022 were linked to modifiable risk factors such as tobacco use, infections, alcohol, excess body weight and air pollution [1][3][4]. Drawing on data from 18.7 million diagnoses across 185 countries, the researchers mapped how 30 changeable risk factors relate to 36 types of cancer, producing the most extensive global picture so far of where prevention efforts could have the biggest impact [1][2][4]. They estimate that about 7.1 million cases—around 2.7 million in women and 4.3 million in men—could likely have been avoided if these risks had been reduced, with lung, stomach and cervical cancers making up a large share of these preventable cases [2][3][4]. The findings highlight major regional differences, from smoking-driven cancers in many high‑income countries to infection‑related tumours in many lower‑income regions, and point to practical opportunities for governments to cut future cancer burdens through vaccination, smoking reduction, and better infection control [2][3][4].

Did you know: In Australia, melanoma—not lung cancer—is estimated to be the most common preventable cancer type, reflecting how intense sunlight and sun‑exposure habits shape national cancer profiles [2].

Key Points

  • Risk factor patterns: The analysis found tobacco as the top preventable driver in men globally, while infections such as HPV and Helicobacter pylori were the leading contributors to preventable cancers in women in many regions [3][4].
  • Cancer types affected: Nearly half of the estimated preventable cases were lung, stomach or cervical cancers, underscoring how smoking control and infection prevention could sharply reduce the incidence of these particular tumours [2][3].
  • Country differences: The most common preventable cancer type varied by country, with melanoma standing out as the leading preventable cancer in Australia, in contrast to lung cancer in many other nations [2].
  • Modifiable exposures list: Researchers evaluated 30 modifiable exposures—including diet, physical inactivity, alcohol, air pollution, and various infections—linking them with 36 cancers to quantify preventable shares for each pairing [1][4].
  • Data scale and scope: The work synthesised information from 185 countries for the year 2022, covering 18.7 million new cancer cases and creating a geographically detailed map of where primary prevention could avert the most disease [1][2][4].

Perspectives

World Health Organization / IARC researchers: They emphasise that a significant portion of the global cancer burden could be avoided if countries strengthened policies to reduce tobacco and alcohol use, expanded vaccination against cancer‑linked infections such as human papillomavirus, and improved control of environmental and occupational carcinogens [1][3][4].

Public health commentators: Experts commenting on World Cancer Day note that the study offers concrete evidence to support scaling up prevention programmes—especially in low‑ and middle‑income countries where infection‑related cancers are more common—arguing that these measures could be more cost‑effective than treating advanced disease [3][4].

Technical Details

  • Population attributable fraction: The study estimates, for each risk factor–cancer pair, the proportion of cases that would not have occurred if exposure had been reduced to a specified lower level, a metric known as the population attributable fraction that is widely used in public health to quantify preventable disease burden [1].
  • Modifiable risk factors: Researchers defined 30 modifiable risk factors—including tobacco smoking, second‑hand smoke, alcohol use, specific infections, obesity, air pollution and occupational exposures—meaning that, in principle, policy or individual behaviour changes can reduce people’s contact with these cancer‑causing agents [1][2].
  • Global cancer mapping: By combining cancer incidence data from 185 countries with exposure estimates for each risk factor, the team produced geographically resolved estimates of which cancers and risk factors contribute most to preventable burden in different regions, allowing targeted prevention planning rather than one‑size‑fits‑all recommendations [1][4].

Industry Impact

  • Health systems planning: Quantifying the proportion of cancers attributable to specific behaviours and infections gives health systems empirical guidance for investing in vaccination, tobacco control, and screening programmes that can prevent cases before they reach already stretched oncology services [1][4].
  • Pharmaceutical and vaccine sector: The prominent role of infections such as HPV in preventable cancers underscores sustained demand for vaccines and antiviral interventions, potentially encouraging companies and public–private partnerships to expand access to cancer‑preventing immunisations in low‑ and middle‑income countries [2][3].
  • Workplace and environmental policy: By including air pollution and occupational exposures among the modifiable risks, the analysis adds scientific backing for stricter environmental regulations and workplace safety standards that could shift industry practices toward reducing carcinogenic exposures [1][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: This work provides the strongest global evidence so far that a substantial fraction of current cancer incidence arises from exposures that scientists can already identify and measure, reinforcing decades of research linking behaviours and environmental factors to cancer and showing where prevention could yield the greatest health gains [1][2][4].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Because the analysis draws on large‑scale international cancer registries and established risk estimates for well‑studied exposures like tobacco and HPV, confidence is relatively high for major factors, though the authors acknowledge that uncertainties in exposure data—especially in lower‑income countries—mean that estimates for some settings carry wider margins of error [1][4].
  • Limitations and future work: The authors note that they did not assess all possible risk factors or account for every interaction between them, and that many countries still lack comprehensive cancer registries, so further research and better surveillance could refine or adjust the estimates and help track how prevention policies change the preventable fraction over time [1][3].

Historical Background

For decades, epidemiological studies have linked behaviours such as smoking and heavy alcohol consumption to increased cancer risk, and reductions in smoking in several high‑income countries have already led to declines in some tobacco‑related cancers [common]. Building on earlier regional analyses of preventable cancer fractions, the WHO’s International Agency for Research on Cancer has progressively expanded its global cancer observatory datasets, enabling this new worldwide assessment of modifiable risk factors by cancer type and country [1][4].

Q&A

Q: How did the researchers estimate exposure levels to different risk factors in countries with limited data?

A: The team combined available national and international datasets on behaviours and environmental exposures with established epidemiological risk models to approximate how common each risk factor is in different populations, acknowledging that data gaps in some countries introduce additional uncertainty into those estimates [1][4].

Q: Which infections besides HPV contribute substantially to preventable cancers in the analysis?

A: The study highlights several infection‑related contributors, including Helicobacter pylori for stomach cancer and hepatitis B and C viruses for liver cancer, alongside HPV for cervical and other anogenital cancers [2][3].

Q: In what ways do preventable cancer patterns differ between high‑income and low‑income regions?

A: High‑income countries tend to show larger preventable burdens from lifestyle‑related exposures like smoking and alcohol, while many low‑ and middle‑income regions carry a higher share of infection‑related cancers due to limited access to vaccination, screening and treatment for pathogens such as HPV and hepatitis viruses [3][4].

Action Items

  • Stay up to date on recommended cancer‑preventing vaccines: Check your country’s immunisation schedule for vaccines that reduce cancer risk, such as HPV or hepatitis B, and discuss any missed doses or catch‑up programmes with your healthcare provider [3][2].
  • Review personal exposure to key cancer risks: Use reputable public health resources to understand how factors like smoking, alcohol intake, body weight and sun exposure influence cancer risk, then set one realistic change—such as booking a stop‑smoking appointment or scheduling a skin check—to start lowering your own risk profile [3][2].
