Canada tables bill to turn defence procurement agency into Crown corporation

Category: Canada

Canada’s federal government introduced Bill C-40 on Oct. 6 to convert the Defence Investment Agency into the Canadian Corporation for Defence Investment, a Crown corporation with its own board and chief executive [3][2][4]. Ottawa says the arm’s-length corporation would gain authority to negotiate contracts, support defence production and invest in strategic sectors to speed military purchases and strengthen domestic industry [7][5]. The Canadian Armed Forces and Department of National Defence would continue to set military requirements, while the new corporation would handle procurement, production and investment. A new associate minister for defence procurement would oversee it. Officials say the corporation would be subject to ministerial direction, parliamentary scrutiny and annual audits by the auditor general [2][3][5]. The bill would also permit loans or payments of up to $1 billion with ministerial approval and sets conditions for non-competitive contracts [4]. Conservatives and a defence procurement expert have raised concerns about oversight and accountability under the arm’s-length structure. Parliament must pass the bill before the proposed structure takes effect [3][4].

Key Points

  • Oversight concerns: The new corporation would not fall under the procurement ombudsman’s mandate, and Secretary of State Stephen Fuhr said he had no answer on whether that mandate would be expanded [5].
  • Budget pressure: The Parliamentary Budget Officer projected that annual defence spending could reach $163.6 billion by 2035 to meet Canada’s NATO core spending pledge of 3.5% of GDP [4].
  • Procurement examples: The agency has advanced a competition to replace the navy’s submarine fleet and a contract with Telesat LEO for military satellite communications [4].

Perspectives

Federal government: Secretary of State for Defence Procurement Stephen Fuhr said the agency needs commercial expertise to negotiate complex contracts and manage relationships with large suppliers over decades [3].

Timeline

  • February 2026: Canada launched its Defence Industrial Strategy to support domestic defence firms [2][4]
  • 6 October 2026: Government tabled Bill C-40 to establish the agency as a Crown corporation [3][4]

Technical Details

  • Sole-source criteria: The bill lists circumstances for non-competitive procurement, including supporting a domestic economic sector or ensuring interoperability with Canadian military equipment or that of an associated government [4].
  • Financial powers: The corporation could make loans or payments of up to $1 billion from the consolidated revenue fund, subject to ministerial approval [4].

Industry Impact

  • Defence Industrial Strategy: The strategy includes nearly $7 billion to build Canada’s defence industrial base [3][5].
  • Defence suppliers: The government expects contracts to be structured more like private-sector purchases, which it says could offer taxpayers better value [2].

Historical Background

Former Royal Bank of Canada executive Doug Guzman was appointed to lead the agency as CEO [4]. The agency was initially structured as a departmental agency; in April 2026, Fuhr said that arrangement would preserve greater government control [4]. The Carney government has also established bodies such as the Major Projects Office to speed decisions on major investments [2]. The former federal Department of Defence Production handled military purchasing from its creation in 1951 until it was disbanded in 1969, although it was a department rather than an arm’s-length corporation [3].

Business Angle

  • Officials said the structure could make it easier to retain experts for specific procurements or draw on board members’ expertise [5].
  • The DIA says combining procurement, production and investment could reduce fragmentation and provide greater continuity and predictability across acquisitions [5].
  • Cabinet will decide where to base the corporation; the agency currently operates largely out of Ottawa, while its CEO frequently works from Toronto [4].

Q&A

Q: What oversight would remain over procurement decisions?

A: National Defence would still need to make Treasury Board submissions, and officials said Finance, the Treasury Board Secretariat and the Privy Council Office could scrutinize decisions [5].

Q: What would the corporation’s board look like?

A: The proposed structure includes a CEO and executive chairperson; the board would include a deputy minister and up to two other directors [5].
