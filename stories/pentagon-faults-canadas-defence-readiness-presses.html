Pentagon faults Canada’s defence readiness, presses Ottawa on spending and F-35s

Category: Canada

The U.S. Defense Department published a fact sheet Monday criticizing Canada’s military readiness and saying Ottawa has not made the decisions needed to meet NATO commitments. It cited capability gaps across air, land and sea forces and faulted Canada for reviewing its planned purchase of U.S.-made F-35 fighter jets [2][4][9]. The criticism followed a classified directive from U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth restricting some military officials’ attendance at Canadian think-tank events and social gatherings. Republican Sen. Mike Rounds said the directive would not affect military operations; Canadian Defence Minister David McGuinty said military, security and intelligence co-operation continued as usual [5][7]. The dispute also concerns Ottawa’s plan to meet NATO’s new defence-spending goal of 5% of gross domestic product by 2035. Canada reached the previous 2% target in 2025, but its latest budget provides few details on how it will reach the new goal [4][5]. The F-35 review remains unresolved, and Canada has also considered Swedish Gripen fighters [9][10].

Key Points

  • Readiness figures: The Pentagon said 41.9% of Canada’s air fleet and 53.9% of its naval fleet were serviceable in fiscal 2025-26, with army readiness at 48% [9][10].
  • Spending path: Canada’s budget projects defence and security spending of 4.1% of GDP in fiscal 2027-28, including 2.6% for core defence; the Pentagon accused Ottawa of postponing the largest increases until after the 2029 federal election [9][10].
  • Submarine fleet: Prime Minister Mark Carney has acknowledged that only one of Canada’s four submarines is seaworthy; Ottawa named Germany’s Thyssenkrupp Marine Systems as the preferred supplier for up to 12 replacements, pending contract talks [10].
  • Early-warning aircraft: The GlobalEye early-warning aircraft will require U.S. clearance for communications equipment and cryptological support to connect to NORAD’s command-and-control networks, giving Washington leverage over its integration and export [7].

Historical Background

NATO members, including Canada, made the 2% of GDP defence-spending pledge in 2006 and reaffirmed it in 2014 [4]. In 2024, then-Prime Minister Justin Trudeau argued that the 2% measure would not automatically make the country safer [4].
