Jurassic fossil traces mammal-like swallowing to early mammaliaforms

Category: Science

A fossil of Megacauda sungei, a semiaquatic mammaliaform from what is now Inner Mongolia, preserves an arrangement of throat bones that researchers say resembles those of living marsupials and placental mammals. The animal lived about 165 million years ago, and its remains provide evidence that this throat anatomy arose before modern mammals [1]. The researchers conclude that the common ancestors of docodonts and mammals had the structures involved in mammal-like swallowing. Their study, published online in Nature on October 7, also describes the fossil as evidence of an early mammaliaform adapted to swimming and foraging in water [1][3].

Key Points

  • Body size: Megacauda measured about 0.5 metres long and weighed one to two kilograms, making it the largest known Jurassic mammaliaform. Many of its contemporaries weighed only a few grams [1].
  • Anatomy: Megacauda had a broad, flattened tail, short hind legs with relatively long feet, and distinctive teeth capable of shearing [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Feeding adaptation: The soft palate and complex hyoid arrangement help mammals swallow while breathing and can keep milk from entering the nasal passage during suckling [1].
  • Comparative anatomy: Crocodiles move unchewed prey down the throat by raising their heads and using gravity; mammals’ complex hyoid structure lets them swallow against gravity [1].
