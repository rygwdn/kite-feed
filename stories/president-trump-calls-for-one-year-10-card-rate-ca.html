President Trump calls for one-year 10% card-rate cap

Category: Business

President Trump says he wants credit card interest rates capped at 10% for one year, with the limit intended to begin on January 20, 2026, as part of a broader affordability push aimed at curbing rates he described as “20 to 30%” [2][3][4][9][5]. The proposal drew encouragement from some lawmakers in both parties, while banks and other card issuers warned a hard cap could restrict the availability of credit, especially for riskier borrowers [3][6][7]. Several outlets noted the president did not lay out an enforcement mechanism and that congressional authorization may be required to implement a binding nationwide cap [2][9][5].

Key Points

  • Possible savings: One analysis cited the policy could save consumers tens of billions of dollars in interest costs over the year if implemented as described [7].
  • Industry split: The pushback is notable because major card issuers and financial firms have often aligned with President Trump on other policy priorities, highlighting a rare public rift with the banking industry [7].
  • Investor warning: Hedge fund manager Bill Ackman said a rate cap could prompt lenders to cancel cards for millions of consumers if they cannot price for losses and returns [5].
  • Issuer exposure: Market coverage flagged large card-focused issuers and networks as potential winners/losers depending on how any cap is structured and whether it applies broadly to banks, store cards, and fintech lenders [barrons.com#1][8].
  • Compliance questions: Reports emphasized the White House has not detailed how it would compel companies to comply by the stated start date, leaving open questions about rulemaking, supervision, and penalties [2][9].

Technical Details

  • APR (annual percentage rate): A credit card’s APR is the annualized interest rate charged on carried balances; caps would directly limit what issuers can charge for revolving credit [common].
  • Risk-based pricing: Card issuers typically charge higher APRs to riskier borrowers to compensate for expected losses; critics argue a low uniform cap could reduce approval rates or credit limits [5][6].
  • Implementation authority: Several reports noted uncertainty about whether a binding national cap can be imposed without Congress and how compliance would be enforced in practice [2][9][5].

Industry Impact

  • Consumer spending: If card interest costs fall for revolvers, households could have more cash flow for other purchases, though the size of the effect depends on whether credit remains widely available [7][6].
  • Fintech and alternatives: If mainstream issuers tighten approvals, demand could shift toward buy-now-pay-later products or personal loans—areas where pricing, disclosures, and regulation differ from traditional credit cards [6][common].
  • Retail co-brand cards: Store and co-branded card programs rely on interest income and issuer economics; a cap could alter revenue-sharing arrangements between retailers and issuing banks [barrons.com#1].

Historical Background

US policymakers have periodically debated interest-rate limits, including proposals to cap consumer lending rates, but modern credit card APRs have typically been set by issuers based on funding costs and borrower risk rather than a national ceiling [common]. During the 1970s–1980s, a series of court rulings and state-level changes helped shape today’s nationwide credit card market, including the ability for many issuers to export interest rates across state lines [common]. The current debate re-emerges as elevated borrowing costs and household budget pressure keep “affordability” at the center of US economic politics [3][5].

Q&A

Q: Would the proposed cap apply to existing balances, new purchases, or both?

A: The articles describe a one-year 10% cap but note the administration has not provided implementation specifics, leaving key scope questions unanswered [2][9].

Q: What enforcement tools could regulators use if a cap became policy?

A: Coverage emphasizes the lack of detail on compliance and suggests congressional authorization may be needed, implying enforcement mechanisms have not been laid out yet [9][5].

Q: How might issuers adjust rewards programs if interest income drops?

A: None of the reports specify changes to rewards, but issuers commonly adjust benefits and fees when economics shift, so consumers would need to watch for program updates from their card providers [common].

Action Items

  • Audit your card APR exposure: Check each statement or issuer app for the purchase APR on cards you carry balances on, then compare it with alternatives like a lower-APR card or a credit-union product before making big purchases [common].
  • Reduce interest while uncertainty plays out: If you revolve balances, consider paying above the minimum and prioritizing the highest-APR card first (“avalanche” method) to cut interest costs regardless of policy outcomes [common].
  • Protect credit access: If lenders tighten approvals as some banks warn, keep utilization low and avoid opening multiple new accounts at once to help maintain eligibility for credit products [6][common].
