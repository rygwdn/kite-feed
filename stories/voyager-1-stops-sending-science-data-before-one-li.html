Voyager 1 stops sending science data before one-light-day milestone

Category: Science

NASA’s Voyager 1 has stopped sending science and engineering data, though its carrier signal remains detectable, the agency said as it confirmed the spacecraft reached one light-day from Earth on Nov. 18. Launched in 1977, the probe is now more than 16 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) away in interstellar space; its signal took 24 hours to reach Earth [3][4]. The spacecraft has sent no data since Nov. 14. Engineers are investigating the fault. NASA said a similar interruption in 2023 was traced to an onboard computer, while the current issue may also involve the computer that collects and formats instrument readings [3][11]. The carrier signal shows the spacecraft is still transmitting and allowed engineers to verify its distance, but it does not provide the missing science readings. Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd said the team remains in contact with the probe. Because signals take a day each way, sending a command and receiving a response now takes about two days, slowing efforts to diagnose the fault and restore the data flow [6][8][11].

I’m confident that it won’t be too long, and it will send us data back.

Did you know: NASA expects the Voyagers’ gold-plated Golden Records to last more than one billion years [4].

Key Points

  • Remaining instruments: If data transmission resumes, Voyager 1’s two remaining science instruments are a magnetometer and a plasma-wave detector [11].
  • Power budget: The probes lose about 4 watts of electrical output each year as their plutonium power sources decay; engineers’ latest power-saving changes are intended to sustain current science operations through the probes’ 50th launch anniversaries next year [3][2].
  • Twin’s milestone: Voyager 2, whose route included Uranus and Neptune, is expected to reach one light-day from Earth in 2035 [4][8].

Perspectives

Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager: The team expects more anomalies as the aging spacecraft continue operating at great distances, Dodd said, describing the current glitch as part of the probes’ aging process.

Linda Spilker, Voyager project scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory: Scientists have yet to determine where the Sun’s influence ends and undisturbed interstellar space begins, Spilker said. Whether Voyager can operate long enough to locate that boundary also remains unresolved.

Sources: NASA

Nicky Fox, NASA associate administrator for the Science Mission Directorate: The probes are in a region no spacecraft had previously explored directly, Fox said, making every measurement they return valuable to the mission.

Sources: Cyprus Mail

Timeline

  • 5 September 1977: Voyager 1 launched to explore Jupiter and Saturn [3][5]
  • August 2012: Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space [3][8]
  • November 2023: A computer-related failure halted Voyager 1 science data [11][23]
  • June 2024: Engineers restored science data after the earlier computer fault [11][23]
  • 14 November 2026: Voyager 1 stopped returning science and engineering data [3][6]
  • 18 November 2026: Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth [3][4]

Technical Details

  • Carrier signal and frame sync: The carrier indicates that Voyager 1’s radio transmitter is active, but the team reported no frame synchronization—the signal structure needed to recover science and engineering telemetry [6].

Scientific Significance

  • Direct interstellar measurements: When data are flowing, Voyager 1 measures magnetic fields, plasma density and energetic particles beyond the heliosphere, observations that let researchers examine the changing boundary between the Sun’s environment and interstellar space [8][3].
  • Unresolved magnetic direction: After crossing the heliopause, Voyager’s magnetic-field direction did not rotate toward the interstellar orientation as scientists expected; researchers continue watching for a change [3][29].
  • Mission legacy: Voyager’s discoveries are informing later missions, including Europa Clipper and Dragonfly, which will explore worlds in the outer solar system [10].

Historical Background

The Voyager mission took advantage of a rare alignment of the outer planets: Voyager 1 studied Jupiter and Saturn, while Voyager 2 continued on to Uranus and Neptune [3].

Q&A

Q: Could Voyager 1’s current communications glitch be related to an earlier interruption?

A: The spacecraft had a separate communications interruption in October, possibly after entering safe mode following a thruster failover. Engineers are investigating whether that event is connected to the current fault [23].
