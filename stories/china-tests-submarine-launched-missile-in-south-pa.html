China tests submarine-launched missile in South Pacific

Category: World

China’s navy test-launched a long-range ballistic missile at 12:01 p.m. local time on Monday, July 6, from a strategic nuclear submarine into the South Pacific; Xinhua said it carried a dummy warhead and landed in designated waters [5][9][20][34]. The People’s Liberation Army Navy called the launch routine annual training, said it notified relevant countries in advance, and said the test complied with international law and was not directed at any country or target [5][6][34][45]. The U.S. State Department said it monitored an unarmed intercontinental-range ballistic missile that landed in the southern Pacific Ocean and called China’s “rapid and opaque nuclear weapons buildup” a concern for the region and the world [8][31][40]. Australia, New Zealand and Japan criticized the test; New Zealand said it had been informed only hours beforehand and noted the missile was fired into the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone, while Japan said it had urged China to reconsider before the launch [5][20][37][23].

Key Points

  • Flight path: A regional source told CNN the missile flew over the exclusive economic zones of the Federated States of Micronesia, Nauru, Kiribati and Tuvalu before landing near the Kiribati-Tuvalu maritime boundary [25][26].
  • Pacific pact: Australia and Fiji signed the Ocean of Peace alliance the same day. The pact includes mutual-defense obligations and a $1 billion Australian commitment, and is Fiji’s first such alliance [35][17][60].
  • Missile uncertainty: China did not identify the missile. Global Times cited a military expert saying it was likely the JL-3, a submarine-launched missile that a Pentagon report says could reach the continental United States from Chinese coastal waters [24][39].

Perspectives

Historical Background

China’s 2024 launch mirrored tests the United States conducts for its own ballistic missile fleet and was seen as an assertion of China’s growing superpower status [20]. The South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone was established by the 1986 Treaty of Rarotonga, and China ratified protocols in 1987 pledging not to test nuclear weapons in the zone or threaten their use against signatories with territory there [20][2]. Asian News Network reported that China’s last publicly disclosed submarine-based ICBM flight test was in September 1988, when a Type 09II submarine launched two JL-1 missiles from the Bohai Sea [46].

International Reactions

  • 🇺🇸 United States: The State Department said the United States monitored the launch, urged China to join arms-control talks, and reiterated U.S. defense commitments to allies [31][8].
  • 🇯🇵 Japan: Tokyo said China notified it around 11:30 a.m., Japan asked Beijing to reconsider, and Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara reported no damage to Japanese aircraft or vessels [37].
  • 🇸🇧 Solomon Islands: Prime Minister Matthew Wale said he did not want more countries to test ICBMs in the Pacific and described the incident as evidence for a new regional security pact [4].
