Researchers say sperm whales use bubbles during sleep

Category: Science

Researchers from the University of St Andrews and the Université de Neuchâtel reported that sperm whales release gas bubbles to regulate buoyancy while resting vertically just below the ocean surface [1][5]. The study examined a behavior seen during short underwater naps, when sperm whales rest head-up, close enough to the surface to breathe but below rougher surface water [3]. The team monitored sperm whales in Norway with suction-mounted devices that recorded bubble sounds, depth and body orientation, allowing researchers to identify when the whales were resting vertically [3][6]. The study, described in the Journal of Experimental Biology, links bubble release to the whales’ ability to remain suspended instead of drifting upward while asleep [3][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Sperm whales may use vowel-like elements in their communication, according to Popular Science’s summary of recent research on the species [3].

Key Points

  • Bubble frequency: Sleeping sperm whales released bubbles as many as 36 times while resting, according to Discover Magazine’s account of the study [4].
  • Expedition: The team collected data during a 2023 research trip to the Lofoten Islands, Norway, where researchers tagged sperm whales with suction-cup devices [3].
  • Tag recovery: Researchers attached recording devices with suction cups and retrieved them after they detached from the whales and floated to the surface [3].
  • Resting interval: Researchers theorize that sperm whales nap for about 10 to 15 minutes at a time while positioned vertically underwater [3].

Perspectives

Noémie Freymond: The study was part of Freymond’s master’s thesis at the University of St Andrews, focusing on sperm whale resting behavior and bubble release.

Sources: Popular Science

University of St Andrews and Université de Neuchâtel researchers: The team described the finding as the first evidence that sperm whales actively control their buoyancy to maintain a vertical sleeping position.

The Scotsman: The report quoted lead author Noémie Freymond as saying the bubbles help the whales ‘stay at a constant depth’ while sleeping.

Scientific Significance

  • Remaining uncertainty: Reports on the study describe it as supporting a buoyancy-regulation explanation, but they do not state whether researchers tested the mechanism across other sperm whale populations or ocean conditions [3][6].
