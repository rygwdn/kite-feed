Tiny Southeast Asian snakes use controlled cartwheels to escape predators

Category: Science

Researchers at Japan’s Toho University have documented how dwarf reed snakes roll rapidly to flee danger while keeping their heads pointed forward. In a study published Oct. 19 in Current Biology, video analyses showed that the snakes use alternating, precisely timed body movements rather than rotating like a wheel. They reached speeds of 1.7 to 3.7 meters per second, about four times their usual slithering speed [1][6][7]. Each cycle begins with the snake rolling its body against the ground and lifting its front end. It then swings its tail past its head and down to start another roll. The head stays relatively stable as the rest of the body rotates, allowing the snake to maintain its direction at speed [4][6]. The researchers recorded the behavior in three Pseudorabdion species, while six comparison snakes from the genus Calamaria did not cartwheel [6].

I find it especially amazing that an elongate animal like a snake can manage to roll, especially given that they do it in such an explosive way, which probably asks a lot of their muscles and bones.

— Jessica Tingle, biologist at Brown University (Science News)

Key Points

  • Test track: Researchers used a 2.2-meter track and found the snakes stayed on a path just 20 centimeters wide [6][2][7].
  • Size: The tested snakes were only about 11 to 28 centimeters long [6].
  • Seeking cover: After rolling, the snakes tended to dive beneath nearby objects for shelter [5].

Perspectives

Jessica Tingle, a biologist at Brown University, said the movement raises questions about whether dwarf reed snakes have distinctive anatomy, physiology or neural control, or whether other snakes have the same capabilities but do not use them [6].
