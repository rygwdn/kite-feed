UK and EU near post-Brexit deals on food, carbon and youth mobility

Category: World

Britain and the European Union are poised to announce agreements on food trade, carbon markets and youth mobility at a Brussels summit on Friday, as Prime Minister Andy Burnham seeks to reset ties a decade after the Brexit vote [4][1][10]. The food pact would remove many certificates and border checks on animal and plant products. The two sides also plan to link emissions-trading systems and create a scheme allowing young people to live, work or study across the Channel for up to three years [4][6]. Negotiators settled on a proposed annual cap of 100,000 young people in each direction. Britain would align with EU food standards and have the right to be consulted, but no vote, on future rules [6][32][1]. A compromise on university fees would give EU students a discount from current international rates, with details to be worked out within six months. EU implementation would depend on Britain introducing the fee change [6][1]. The agreements remain subject to final approval, and Burnham said his government would bring legislation to Parliament next week if a deal is secured [10][12][30]. He has committed to Labour’s manifesto pledges not to rejoin the single market or customs union or restore free movement during this parliamentary term. He plans to discuss longer-term options after the summit [16][10].

After the summit, we will initiate a discussion with our EU partners about options for our long-term relationship.

— Andy Burnham, British prime minister (France 24)

Key Points

  • Carbon-market benefit: The UK government estimates linking emissions-trading systems could add up to £3.8 billion a year and protect about £6.5 billion in exports from the EU’s carbon-border mechanism [19].
  • Food rules: Alignment could require Britain to follow EU rules on products such as gene-edited crops and on labelling [12].
  • Further cooperation: The two sides are also discussing a digital partnership for early 2027 covering secure connectivity, artificial intelligence and digital identities [19].
  • Youth eligibility: The proposed scheme would be open to 18- to 32-year-olds [1].
  • Economic estimate: Britain estimates removing food-trade paperwork could add up to £5.1 billion a year to its economy [16].

Perspectives

European Union diplomats: Some diplomats said Brussels wants Britain to clarify whether it seeks deeper integration before opening further negotiations. They warned that incremental, sector-by-sector agreements have reached their limits [11].

  • January 2030: The youth experience scheme is scheduled to begin operating [19].

Industry Impact

  • Steel and energy exports: British firms could continue paying EU carbon-border taxes until the linked emissions-trading systems take effect. Industry representatives warned that the delay could increase export costs and domestic electricity prices [1].

Q&A

Q: What could Britain have to change to qualify for the EU’s industrial support scheme?

A: The EU has linked possible British access to its “Made in Europe” rules to alignment with EU trade and investment measures, potentially including tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles [1].

Q: What parts of a deeper relationship remain excluded under current policy?

A: Burnham says he will set out options later this year, while any major shift is expected to wait until after the next general election, due by 2029 [16][21].
