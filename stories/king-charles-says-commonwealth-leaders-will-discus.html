King Charles says Commonwealth leaders will discuss slavery’s legacy at summit

Category: World

King Charles III said on Oct. 27 that Commonwealth leaders would discuss slavery’s legacy at their upcoming summit, after beginning a nine-day Caribbean tour in the Bahamas. At a youth rally in Nassau, he called it “only right” to acknowledge the suffering and injustices of the past, but did not apologize for Britain’s role in the slave trade [1][18][2]. Caribbean governments and campaigners are pressing Britain for a formal apology and reparations, while the British government has ruled out both. Guyana’s president, Mohamed Irfaan Ali, said an apology should follow the king’s acknowledgment. UK Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds called reparations an unreasonable request but said Britain should be able to discuss its history [1][10]. Charles is due to visit Guyana and then attend the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua and Barbuda with Queen Camilla. The Nov. 1-4 summit is expected to address reparations alongside climate resilience, trade and investment. Charles also noted that 90% of the Bahamas’ land lies below two metres [2][5].

Our world cries out for healing.

— King Charles III (France 24)

Key Points

  • Jamaica’s petition: Jamaica has asked King Charles to refer legal questions about slavery and restitution to the Privy Council; a hearing is provisionally scheduled for the week of March 8, 2027, and an opinion would not automatically require Britain to pay reparations [14].
  • Public opinion: A More in Common poll commissioned by the Repair Campaign found 60% of Britons aged 18-24 and 51% aged 25-34 supported a meeting on reparatory justice [6].
  • Trade history: European merchants abducted and sold at least 12.5 million Africans between the 15th and 19th centuries, including an estimated 3.2 million transported by Britain [7][10].
  • Island development: In the Bahamas, the Save Exuma Alliance is challenging a Sampson Cay project involving a large seawall and dredging of seagrass and coral reefs; the case is before the Bahamas Supreme Court [2].

Perspectives

Mohamed Irfaan Ali, President of Guyana: Ali called for accountability, saying the former empire had “exploited the region,” “destroyed lives” and “created under development” [1].

Sources: France 24

Jonathan Reynolds, UK Business Secretary: Reynolds argued that current generations should not be held responsible for past events [10].

Sources: CBC Barbados

Bahamas National Reparations Committee: The committee said the king’s remarks mischaracterized Bahamian culture and fell short of justice, which it said requires a formal apology and active steps to remedy harms committed in the Crown’s name [6].

Sources: The Guardian

Shirley Ayorkor Botchwey, Commonwealth secretary-general: Botchwey said the time had come to discuss reparations, including financial payments or other forms such as returning artefacts, and said an apology “wouldn’t hurt” [7][1].

Historical Background

Britain abolished slavery in 1834 and compensated slave owners, but not enslaved people; research cited by CNN puts the equivalent value of those payments at £17 billion ($22.5 billion) [10].
