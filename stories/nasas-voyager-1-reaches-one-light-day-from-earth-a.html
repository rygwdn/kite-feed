NASA’s Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth after 49 years

Category: Technology

NASA’s Voyager 1 reached a distance of one light-day from Earth at 5:16:07 a.m. Eastern on Nov. 18, becoming the first human-made object to reach that distance. A light-day is about 16.1 billion miles; radio commands now take roughly 24 hours to reach the probe, and replies take another day to return [21][26][32]. Launched in 1977 to study the outer planets, Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012 and still sends measurements from beyond the Sun’s protective bubble. Its remaining magnetometer and plasma-wave instrument record magnetic fields and charged particles, but the spacecraft’s plutonium power supply continues to weaken [12][18][33]. Engineers must wait roughly two days to check whether a command worked, and a recent loss of contact lasted two days. NASA’s small team continues to manage the aging craft remotely; Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, estimated its operational life could extend another three to five years [21][11][26].

Did you know: At Voyager 1’s current pace, it would take about 18,000 years to travel one light-year [17].

Key Points

  • Solar system passage: Because the Sun and its planets are moving through space, Voyager’s readings describe an interstellar region the solar system is expected to enter in about three years [11].
  • Past discoveries: Voyager 1 discovered volcanic activity on Jupiter’s moon Io and a thick atmosphere around Saturn’s moon Titan during its planetary flybys [25][32].
  • Last look home: Before its cameras were shut down for good, Voyager 1 captured a solar system “family portrait,” including the image of Earth later known as the “Pale Blue Dot” [21].
  • Planetary discoveries: Voyager 1’s earlier Jupiter observations revealed the planet’s rings and extreme atmospheric storms. Research using Voyager data also showed that material from Io’s volcanoes feeds Jupiter’s magnetosphere, creating a giant cloud of plasma around the planet [28][37].
  • Twin spacecraft: Voyager 2 is expected to reach the same one-light-day distance in November 2035, about nine years after Voyager 1 [19][21].
  • Power trade-offs: In September, engineers’ “Big Bang” power-saving switch conserved 10 watts and delayed the need to turn off another instrument until as early as next summer [11].

Technical Details

  • Radioisotope power: Voyager 1’s radioisotope thermoelectric generators convert heat from plutonium-238 decay into electricity; output has fallen from about 470 watts at launch to roughly 210 watts, a loss of about 4 watts per year [26].
  • Deep-space telemetry: The downlink is so faint that NASA relies on 230-foot-wide Deep Space Network antennas; Voyager’s transmitter sends data at roughly 40 bytes per second [18][16].

Scientific Significance

  • Unexplained pressure front: A persistent pressure front Voyager 1 encountered remains unexplained. Simulations have not reproduced the mix of shocks, swells and placid stretches the spacecraft observed [9].
  • Solar shielding: Measurements from beyond the heliosphere indicate the Sun’s particle-and-field bubble blocks roughly 75% to 85% of galactic cosmic rays, with the estimate varying alongside solar activity [9].
  • Direct interstellar measurements: Voyager 1 and its twin provide the only direct spacecraft measurements of the interstellar environment beyond the heliosphere, including magnetic fields, plasma and energetic particles [9][12].
  • Solar shielding: Voyager measurements indicate the heliosphere blocks about 75% to 85% of galactic cosmic rays, with the proportion varying according to solar activity [9].

Historical Background

The Voyager mission used the outer planets’ gravity to redirect the probes between worlds, enabling a multi-planet tour [31]. Voyager 2 launched first, on Aug. 20, 1977, and Voyager 1 followed on Sept. 5; their numbering reflected planned trajectories, not launch order [31]. Voyager 1 passed Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn and its moon Titan in 1980, then headed out of the plane of the planets [21][31].

Q&A

Q: What happens to Voyager 1 after its instruments stop working?

A: The spacecraft will continue moving through the galaxy even after its power supply can no longer support transmissions; it is expected to pass about 1.7 light-years from Gliese 445 in roughly 40,000 years [24][16].

Q: How much longer might Voyager 1 remain in contact with Earth?

A: NASA says recent power-saving measures could let the Voyager spacecraft stay in communication with Earth for another three to four years, though their remaining operating life is difficult to predict [47].
