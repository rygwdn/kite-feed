Animal blood colors reflect distinct oxygen-carrying proteins and adaptations

Category: Science

Animal blood can be blue, violet, green or white because species use different oxygen-transport proteins or pigments, according to a Knowable Magazine feature republished by Nautilus and ZME Science [1][2]. Octopuses, squid and crustaceans use copper-containing hemocyanin. Some marine worms and brachiopods use purple hemerythrin, while New Guinea lizards accumulate green biliverdin [1][2]. Antarctic blackfin icefish lack hemoglobin and red blood cells, relying instead on oxygen dissolved in their blood and enlarged hearts and capillaries [1][2]. Vampire bats are the only known mammals that live exclusively on blood; specialized kidneys and gut microbes help them handle the diet [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • High-altitude adaptation: Deer mice can live at altitudes as high as 14,000 feet, where their hemoglobin binds oxygen tightly. They compensate for the difficulty of releasing that oxygen by carrying more oxygen-releasing mitochondria in their muscle fibers [1][2].
  • Energy strategy: Icefish are patient ambush predators that lie in wait rather than darting after prey, conserving energy and reducing their oxygen needs [1][2].
  • Part-time blood-feeding: Female mosquitoes drink blood for protein that helps their eggs mature, while oxpeckers may lap blood from wounds after picking ticks from large mammals [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Evolutionary mechanism: The researchers propose that oxygen-rich waters in the Southern Ocean may have made hemoglobin unnecessary for icefish, but the evolutionary cause of its loss remains unresolved [1][2].
  • Possible medical relevance: The article links unusual hemoglobin traits in deer mice to possible hypoxia therapies and icefish biology to potential anemia research. These are research leads, not established treatments [1][2].
