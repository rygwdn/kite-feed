Chinese dinosaur fossils reveal predators and peacock-like tails

Category: Science

Researchers described two small feathered dinosaurs from Early Cretaceous China, adding evidence about how bird-like dinosaurs and early birds shared ecosystems about 120 million years ago. One new species, Jian changmaensis, came from a fossil bed in northwestern China that also contains hundreds of prehistoric bird remains, including clusters of shattered bones compressed into pellet-like masses; researchers said the four-winged, Velociraptor-related glider may have produced them [1][2]. Scientists separately identified a new genus and species, Changzhousaurus sinensis, that lived in what is now northern China during the same general period. It measured about 34 cm, placing it among the smallest known non-avian theropods [3][4]. Xing Xu of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology described it as an early-diverging member of the deinonychosaur lineage, a pennaraptoran group that includes dromaeosaurs and troodontids [3][4].

Key Points

  • Predation clue: Researchers had long suspected the Changma bird-bone clusters were evidence of predation; the discovery of Jian changmaensis at the same fossil site gave them a possible predator [2].
  • Microraptor classification: Jian changmaensis is a newly identified microraptorine, part of a dromaeosaur subgroup that includes other four-winged gliders, expanding the known diversity of these small predatory dinosaurs [1][2].
  • Tail display: Changzhousaurus sinensis had a fan of long feathers on its tail, giving it a peacock-like appearance that may have been used for courtship or social signaling [3][4].
  • Evolutionary record: Xu wrote that three decades of pennaraptoran discoveries have filled morphological gaps, documented early theropod flight and shown rapid Middle Jurassic diversification [3][4].

Perspectives

Jingmai O'Connor: O'Connor, senior author of the Jian changmaensis paper and associate curator of fossil reptiles at the Field Museum, described the new microraptor as the strongest available explanation for the broken bird-bone clusters.

Xing Xu: Xu said unresolved questions remain about pennaraptoran phylogeny, behavior and anatomy, even after recent fossils broadened the record of feathers, flight-related traits and ecological adaptations.

Sources: Sci.News

Technical Details

  • Pellet-like bone accumulations: The Changma site preserves clusters of shattered prehistoric bird bones compressed into masses comparable to modern owl pellets, making the fossil pattern a behavioral clue instead of only isolated skeletal material [2].
  • Pennaraptoran mosaic traits: The small skeleton of Changzhousaurus sinensis combines traits distributed across multiple bird-like dinosaur branches, complicating efforts to place early deinonychosaurs cleanly on the family tree [3][4].
  • Forelimb and shoulder diagnosis: Researchers identified Jian changmaensis partly by distinctive arm and shoulder-bone features, the same anatomical region relevant to reconstructing how four-winged microraptors moved [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty and confidence: Researchers used cautious language, saying Jian changmaensis 'may' be responsible and calling it the team's 'best guess,' because the sources do not report direct stomach contents or a fossilized attack linking the dinosaur to the bird remains [1][2].
  • Limitations and future work: For Changzhousaurus sinensis, Xu said unresolved issues remain in reconstructing pennaraptoran phylogeny, behavior and anatomy, so additional specimens and comparative analyses would be needed to stabilize its evolutionary placement [3][4].
