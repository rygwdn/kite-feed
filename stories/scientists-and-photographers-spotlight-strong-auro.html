Scientists and photographers spotlight strong auroras into 2026

Category: Science

Forecasters say 2026 could bring more chances to see the Northern Lights because the Sun remains in an active phase, keeping the odds of vivid auroral displays elevated into the year ahead [1]. Popular Science’s 2025 Northern Lights Photographer of the Year showcase highlights how intense the current solar cycle has been, with winning images captured across multiple countries and both hemispheres [2]. Together, the reporting points to a cheerful takeaway for skywatchers: strong geomagnetic activity can translate into more frequent, more dramatic nights of aurora—if clouds and local light pollution cooperate [1][2].

Key Points

  • Global winners: The 2025 awards collection features aurora photographs from places including Iceland, New Zealand, and Estonia, underscoring that aurora viewing is a worldwide pursuit rather than a strictly Arctic one [2].
  • Southern counterpart: Popular Science notes that auroras also appear in the Southern Hemisphere as the aurora australis, and one highlighted image was taken at Aoraki/Mt Cook National Park in New Zealand [2].
  • Glacier setting: One award-winning shot described by the photographer was planned around a changing glacier ice feature (a moulin/ice cave) monitored over an eight-month period before the aurora unexpectedly lit the sky during the shoot [2].
  • Activity after peak: Even though solar maximum has already peaked, Popular Science reports geomagnetic activity has stayed “exceptionally strong,” helping explain why auroras have remained impressive [2].
  • Year-ahead outlook: The BBC report frames 2026 as promising for sightings specifically because solar activity is still running high, which can increase the frequency of aurora opportunities compared with quieter solar periods [1].

Historical Background

Auroras brighten when charged particles from the Sun interact with Earth’s magnetic field and upper atmosphere, so displays tend to cluster around periods of heightened solar activity [common]. The Sun’s activity rises and falls in an approximately 11-year solar cycle, with “solar maximum” marking the peak in sunspots and related space-weather phenomena [common]. The current cycle has produced notably strong auroras in 2025, and both pieces connect that heightened space weather to continued viewing potential into 2026 [2][1].
