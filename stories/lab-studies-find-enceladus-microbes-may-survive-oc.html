Lab studies find Enceladus microbes may survive ocean conditions

Category: Science

Two studies published Sept. 25 in Science Advances examined whether Saturn’s moon Enceladus could support microbial life. In a laboratory experiment, researchers exposed an Earth microbe from deep-sea hydrothermal vents to brine designed to resemble the moon’s subsurface ocean and found that it survived, suggesting some Earth-like microbes could tolerate those conditions [1][7]. The findings do not show that life exists on Enceladus [8]. A separate study analyzed Cassini measurements of ice grains in Saturn’s E ring to better infer the composition of Enceladus’ ocean. Researchers identified more variation among salt-rich grains than earlier classifications had captured, providing additional information about the ocean from material ejected into space [2][4][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Test organism: The test archaeon was Methanothermococcus okinawensis, which lives near hydrothermal vents at Iheya Ridge off Japan [8][9].
  • Ocean chemistry: The experiment modeled dissolved organic carbon at 100 mmol per kilogram and tested different acidity levels. The microbes did not grow under highly acidic, low-carbon-dioxide conditions [8].
  • Cassini data: The grain analysis examined nearly 1,000 measurements from Cassini’s Cosmic Dust Analyzer, which recorded E-ring particle compositions from 2004 to 2017 [2].
  • Type 3 particles: This major compositional group in Enceladus’ plume and Saturn’s E ring is thought to consist of frozen, micrometer-sized droplets from the moon’s salty ocean [2].

Perspectives

Nozair Khawaja: Khawaja said the team did not expect the experiment to succeed so well and described the result as a surprise [8].

Sources: RTVI

Vanessa Helmbrecht: The study’s lead author said Enceladus’ geochemistry could create conditions more favorable to microbial life than researchers had previously assumed [9].

Sources: Onetz

Scientific Significance

  • Limits of remote sampling: The E-ring grain results infer ocean properties from particles ejected by Enceladus rather than from direct sampling of its subsurface ocean. The researchers said the ring data provide broader statistics than Cassini’s limited plume crossings [2].
