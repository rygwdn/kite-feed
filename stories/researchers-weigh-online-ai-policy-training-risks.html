Researchers weigh online AI policy training risks

Category: AI

A LessWrong analysis argues that training AI policies “online” (updating behavior during deployment) may help average-case behavior but creates new safety and governance challenges, especially around knowing what model is actually running and how it generalizes to untrained situations [1]. Separately, software creator David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH) posted that today’s AI models are “now good enough,” reflecting a more adoption-focused view of current capability [2]. A third piece highlights a learning-related concern: as AI systems get more capable, they may suffer a “curse of knowledge” that makes them worse at anticipating where human learners get stuck, potentially limiting their usefulness as tutors unless designs compensate for it [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: A common failure mode in explanations is skipping steps that feel “obvious” to an expert—an effect often described as the “curse of knowledge” [3].

Key Points

  • Audit gap: The LessWrong post argues online-trained policies can undermine pre-deployment evaluation, because the model can change after release in ways that were not fully audited beforehand [1].
  • Diffuse control: The author frames online training as part of “diffuse control” thinking—leaning on training-time feedback to keep average outcomes acceptable—while warning that worst-case generalization failures could still appear before training corrects them [1].
  • Capability optimism: DHH’s post signals a practical stance that models have reached a threshold where they can be relied on for real work, a view that tends to prioritize shipping and integration over waiting for further breakthroughs [2].
  • Teaching blind spots: The Decoder article links smarter systems to the “curse of knowledge,” suggesting more expert-like models may skip over beginner difficulties unless explicitly trained or prompted to surface intermediate steps and common misconceptions [3].

Technical Details

  • Training policies online (TPO): “Training policies online” refers to updating an AI policy during deployment based on live feedback, which can improve average-case behavior but complicates knowing exactly what system is currently running and whether it was audited in that form [1].
  • Generalization to untrained situations: The LessWrong post centers on how a model behaves in scenarios it was not trained on, warning that bad out-of-distribution behavior could appear before training feedback corrects it [1].
  • Curse of knowledge (in AI tutoring): The Decoder article uses “curse of knowledge” to describe how more expert-like systems may overlook novice stumbling blocks, which can make explanations less helpful unless the system is designed to reveal intermediate reasoning steps [3].

Industry Impact

  • AI governance tooling: If more products adopt online-updated policies, demand may grow for monitoring, versioning, and audit mechanisms that can track behavior changes after deployment, a gap the LessWrong post flags as a practical concern [1].
  • Developer adoption: Public statements like DHH’s that models are “good enough” can accelerate adoption pressure inside organizations, shifting competition toward product design and operational excellence rather than model novelty alone [2].
  • Edtech design: The “curse of knowledge” framing suggests tutoring products may need specialized evaluation and UX patterns—like prompting for step-by-step explanations—to meet learners where they are, not where the model is [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: Taken together, the pieces emphasize that “more capable” is not a single axis: systems can improve at task completion yet regress on human-facing skills like teaching clarity, and safety properties depend heavily on how learning continues after deployment [1][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The LessWrong and Decoder items present conceptual arguments rather than reporting controlled experimental results or quantified error margins, so their claims should be read as hypotheses and risk considerations, not settled measurements [1][3].
  • Limitations and future work: The LessWrong post implies a need for better methods to audit systems that change during use, while the education-focused critique implies future work on training/evaluation that measures instructional helpfulness for novices rather than only expert-level correctness [1][3].

Historical Background

Online learning and reinforcement learning systems have long raised a recurring tradeoff: adapting quickly to new conditions versus maintaining stable, auditable behavior once deployed [common]. As AI assistants expanded into consumer and enterprise workflows in the 2020s, evaluation and red-teaming before release became more common, but that model of assurance can get harder when behavior is updated continually after deployment [1].

Q&A

Q: What safeguards are suggested for systems that keep learning after deployment?

A: The LessWrong post points to the core challenge that you may not be able to audit the exact deployed model when policies are trained online, implying a need for stronger monitoring and audit approaches during and after deployment [1].

Q: Does the critique of online training apply equally to “monitors” and “policies”?

A: The LessWrong author notes the downsides discussed are “less relevant to online training of monitors” than to training policies online [1].

Q: What would make an AI tutor better for novices than a very “smart” general model?

A: The Decoder piece suggests smarter models can miss where learners struggle due to a “curse of knowledge,” so features that force explicit intermediate steps and learner-centered explanations can matter as much as raw capability [3].

Action Items

  • Add a model-version label in AI-powered apps: Display the active model/version (and last update time) in the UI so users can tell when behavior changes, addressing audit/awareness concerns raised about online-updated policies [1].
  • Stress-test tutoring prompts with beginners: When using AI for learning, ask for step-by-step reasoning and a quick check for common misconceptions to counter the “curse of knowledge” problem described for smarter models [3].
