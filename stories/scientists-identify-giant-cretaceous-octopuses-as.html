Scientists identify giant Cretaceous octopuses as apex predators

Category: Science

Researchers reporting in Science say fossilized jaw parts from ancient finned octopuses show that some octopuses living in the Cretaceous reached about 19 meters in length and sat at the top of marine food webs [6][1][3][7][8][12]. The findings challenge the long-standing idea that the era’s largest marine predators were only vertebrates, suggesting giant invertebrates also competed with marine reptiles in ancient seas [6][2][7][12]. Because octopuses have soft bodies that rarely fossilize, the study relied on 27 preserved jaw fossils from Japan and Vancouver Island in Canada, then used imaging and reconstruction methods to estimate body size [7][10]. The work also pushes back parts of the octopus fossil record and shows how new techniques can recover evidence from fragmentary remains [10][5][9].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Modern cirrate octopuses, the same broad group identified in the fossils, are often called dumbo octopuses [common].

Key Points

  • Fossil sample: The team analyzed 27 fossil jaws from cirrate, or finned, octopuses, a group that still survives in today’s oceans [7][10].
  • Record size: Several reports say the reconstructed animals were around 19 meters, or roughly 62 to 65 feet, long, making them candidates for the largest invertebrates yet identified [3][7][10][1].
  • Earlier origins: Popular Science reported that the fossils push back the record for giant finned octopuses by about 15 million years and the broader octopus timeline by about 5 million years [10].

Perspectives

Yasuhiro Iba and study co-authors: The researchers argue that the jaw fossils show early octopuses were giant apex predators, not small, marginal animals in Cretaceous seas.

Sources: Science

Science journalists covering the study: Multiple outlets say the result challenges the familiar picture of Cretaceous ocean food webs as being dominated only by vertebrate predators.

Sources: Scientific American

Paleontology coverage across outlets: Reporters also note that the claim rests on rare jaw fossils and reconstructions rather than complete bodies, highlighting both the novelty of the evidence and why ancient octopuses have remained hidden for so long.

Sources: BBC News

Timeline

  • 23 April 2026: Science and major outlets publish study coverage on giant octopus fossils [6][8]
  • 100 million years ago: Cretaceous giant octopuses are placed in reconstructions from around this time [10][8]
  • 72 million years ago: Some reports say giant octopuses lived more than 72 million years ago [1]

Technical Details

  • Fossil reconstruction: Popular Science says researchers repeatedly ground fossil samples into very fine layers and digitally reconstructed their internal structure, a destructive but high-detail method that can reveal tiny embedded features [10].
  • Jaw-based body scaling: Because no complete bodies were available, researchers inferred total size from fossil jaw dimensions by comparing them with living cephalopods and reconstructed anatomy [7][10].
  • Cirrate octopuses: The fossils were assigned to cirrate octopuses, the finned subgroup sometimes called dumbo octopuses, showing that this lineage once included much larger forms than those alive today [7][10].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The study appears to revise Cretaceous marine ecology by adding giant octopuses to the list of apex predators, broadening scientists’ view of who occupied the top trophic levels in ancient oceans [6][2][7].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Confidence rests on rare but direct fossil evidence from 27 jaws rather than complete skeletons, so the existence of large octopuses is supported, while exact body size and behavior remain reconstructions based on comparative methods [7][10][8].
  • Limitations and future work: The biggest open question is whether additional fossils from other regions can confirm the same anatomy, size range, and predator role, since soft-bodied animals remain underrepresented in the fossil record [8][5][common].

Historical Background

Octopuses are soft-bodied animals, so their deep fossil record has long been sparse compared with that of shelled cephalopods or vertebrates [common]. The Cretaceous Period lasted from about 145 million to 66 million years ago and is best known for large marine reptiles such as mosasaurs and plesiosaurs [common]. The new study matters because it places giant octopuses in that well-studied ecosystem much earlier than many researchers had documented before [10][6].
