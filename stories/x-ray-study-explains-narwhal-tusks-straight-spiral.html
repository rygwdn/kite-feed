X-ray study explains narwhal tusk’s straight spiral

Category: Science

An international research team reported in Nature Communications that the narwhal’s tusk, long known for its straight, spiraling form, is made of two structures that twist in opposite directions rather than a single spiral [7][9][8]. The work examined the dental tissues of the tusk, a modified canine tooth that in male narwhals grows through the jaw and upper lip and can exceed two meters in length [6][9][8]. The researchers used 3D X-ray methods, including synchrotron imaging at three European particle accelerators, to study the tusk from microscopic to molecular scales [8][17]. They found that the inner dentin forms a right-handed spiral while the outer cementum forms a left-handed spiral. The team said the counter-rotating arrangement helps explain how the tusk stays straight while displaying its distinctive left-handed external twist [6][8][17]. Henrik Birkedal, a chemist at Aarhus University and senior author of the study, said the consistent handedness pattern in narwhal tusks appears unusual among biological structures [7]. The tusk’s function remains debated, with proposed roles including sexual signaling, sensing water conditions and fighting, but Popular Science reported that evidence remains limited for several of those explanations [7].

The really special part is that these helices always have the same handedness—similar to if all [people] only had left hands

— Henrik Birkedal (Popular Science)

Key Points

  • Tissue layers: Unlike human teeth, the narwhal tusk has internal dentin and a thin external cementum layer, with no enamel outer covering described in the study coverage [7][8].
  • Growth timing: Ars Technica reported that the left upper canine pushes through the lip when males reach about two or three years of age [9].
  • Rare variants: Some male narwhals can develop two long spiral tusks, while rare individuals may have none [9].
  • Research team: The project brought together biologists, chemists and physicists to analyze the tooth’s structure [8].

Perspectives

Henrik Birkedal: Birkedal compared a male narwhal carrying the tusk while diving to riding a bicycle with a broom strapped to the head, emphasizing the structure’s mechanical burden.

Historical Background

Narwhal tusks were sold in medieval and early modern Europe as unicorn horns, and people credited them with detecting poison or curing diseases such as plague [8][10][12][13]. El País traced written unicorn lore to Pliny the Elder’s description of the monoceros in Natural History and reported that the link to a marine animal’s skull was not seriously questioned until the 17th century [10].
