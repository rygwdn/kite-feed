Anthropic AI proof clears formal checks, but human review remains

Category: AI

An AI-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge has passed formal checks by two groups: the Lean Focused Research Organization, which advances the Lean proof system, and the Mathlib Initiative, which supports its mathematics library. The groups said on Nov. 21 they had “full confidence” in the proof Alpöge developed with Anthropic’s Claude model [19][9][16]. The checks establish that the formalized code proves the Riemann hypothesis as defined in Mathlib, but do not provide mathematicians with a readable account of the argument. The proof consists of a Lean library of about 17 million lines, and Alpöge has said the argument is too large to present as a conventional paper at this stage [16][1][18]. The certification does not constitute formal acceptance of the problem as solved. The Clay Mathematics Institute requires a solution to appear in a peer-reviewed journal and withstand at least two years of scrutiny before it can consider awarding its $1 million prize [33][9].

Key Points

  • Human alternative: Stanford professor Ravi Vakil said postdoctoral researcher Alessandro Masullo had submitted an independent, AI-free draft concerning the generalized Riemann hypothesis; the manuscript still needs substantial revision and review [41][42].
  • Broader claim: Alpöge said his method also extends to the generalized Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions; that claim is separate from the certification of the Riemann-hypothesis proof [2][20].
  • Prize conditions: The Clay Mathematics Institute selected the hypothesis as one of seven Millennium Prize Problems and offers $1 million for an accepted solution [33][48].
  • Audit results: MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland reported that all 175,187 Lean modules compiled without errors; an axiom audit found that the five headline theorems relied only on Lean’s three standard axioms [9][16].
  • Independent checkers: Lean’s kernel and third-party checkers accepted the formal proof export, while the tools comparator and con-leche separately confirmed that the encoded theorem matches Mathlib’s Riemann-hypothesis statement [16][1].
  • Historical reach: Efforts to prove the hypothesis helped develop techniques used in algebraic geometry, probability theory, quantum physics and dynamical systems [48].
  • Model access: Alpöge said he worked with a non-public version of Claude for about six weeks, limiting outside researchers’ ability to reproduce the discovery process with the same model [8][19].
  • Parallel claim: Stanford professor Ravi Vakil said a postdoctoral researcher in his department had independently submitted a proof of the hypothesis [33].
  • Broader claim: Alpöge also said the approach proves the generalized Riemann hypothesis, which extends the conjecture to related L-functions [48][33][41].

Technical Details

  • Mathlib statement: The Riemann-hypothesis statement already existed in Mathlib and had been vetted by experts; a challenge file isolated it and imported only Mathlib for comparison [16].
  • Kernel verification: Lean’s kernel replay found no rejected modules, while independently developed checkers also accepted the exported proof [1].
  • Statement matching: Reviewers used comparator and con-leche to check that the code’s theorem matches the Riemann-hypothesis statement already defined in Mathlib [1].
  • Export safeguards: The proof was checked as an inert export, beyond the reach of code in the Lean source; con-leche and con-ron also have formal proofs, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof [16].
