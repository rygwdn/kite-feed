PLOS Biology study links penis size to attraction

Category: Science

A new study reported across several outlets suggests human penis size may have been shaped by two pressures: mate choice and competition between men [3][2][4][1]. Researchers used computer-generated male bodies that varied in height, body shape and penis size, asking women to rate attractiveness and men to rate how intimidating each figure seemed as a rival [2][4]. Across ratings, larger size tended to raise perceived attractiveness and threat—though women’s ratings showed diminishing returns at higher sizes, while men continued to see larger-endowed rivals as more threatening, especially as sexual competitors [2][4]. The findings are framed as one possible explanation for why humans, relative to many other primates, have larger penises for their body size, while also offering a more evidence-based way to talk about a topic often surrounded by stereotypes and misinformation [4][2].

Did you know: The study used both life-sized and scaled-to-size image presentations so researchers could check whether display scale affected how participants judged bodies [4].

Key Points

  • Study design: Participants viewed computer-generated male figures whose height, shoulder-to-hip ratio (a V-shaped torso cue), and penis size were systematically varied; women rated sexual attractiveness and men rated intimidation in fighting ability and as a sexual rival [2][4].
  • Body cues: Women’s attraction ratings also increased with taller figures and higher shoulder-to-hip ratios, suggesting genital size was evaluated alongside broader body-shape cues rather than in isolation [2][4].
  • Male overestimation: Men consistently judged ever-larger penis sizes as increasing sexual threat more than women’s attractiveness ratings did, which the authors interpret as possible overestimation of penis size’s importance in mate competition [2][4].
  • Publication venue: The work is reported as being published in PLOS Biology and led by Upama Aich at the University of Western Australia with colleagues [3][4].
  • Two pressures: Science coverage summarizes the evolutionary argument as involving both attraction (female choice) and intimidation of rivals (male-male competition) as the two main pressures discussed [1][2].

Perspectives

Study authors (UWA-led team): The researchers argue that patterns in women’s attraction ratings and men’s threat assessments are consistent with sexual selection pressures that could help explain unusually large human penis size relative to other primates [2].

Technical Details

  • Sexual selection: An evolutionary process where traits spread because they increase mating success through mate choice or competition, and the study interprets attractiveness and intimidation ratings through this lens [2][4].
  • Shoulder-to-hip ratio: A body-shape metric reflecting how V-shaped a torso appears; in the experiment, higher ratios were associated with higher attractiveness and greater perceived intimidation [2][4].
  • Diminishing returns: A pattern where increases in a trait yield smaller gains beyond a threshold; women’s attractiveness ratings rose with penis size only up to a point, after which further increases mattered less [2][4].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The study offers empirical evidence—via controlled, systematically varied stimuli—that both mate choice and rival assessment can be linked to genital size perceptions, supporting a dual-pressure account discussed in popular coverage [2][1].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Because the work relies on participant judgments of computer-generated bodies rather than real-world mating outcomes, it informs how people perceive cues (attraction/threat) but does not directly measure reproductive success or evolutionary change [2][4].
  • Limitations and future work: Future research would need to test whether similar perception patterns hold across different cultures and contexts, and whether these perceptions predict actual behavior in partner choice and competition rather than ratings alone [2][4].

Historical Background

Evolutionary biologists have long debated why human genital morphology differs from other primates, with hypotheses ranging from female choice to sperm competition and male-male rivalry [4]. Sexual selection theory, introduced by Charles Darwin, formalized the idea that traits can evolve because they increase mating success, even if they do not directly improve survival [common].

Q&A

Q: Did participants rate only penis size, or whole-body traits too?

A: They rated figures that varied simultaneously in height, shoulder-to-hip ratio, and penis size, allowing the study to examine combined effects of multiple cues [2][4].

Q: What does the study mean by men feeling threatened in two ways?

A: Men rated how intimidating figures seemed both as potential fighting opponents and as sexual rivals, and larger sizes increased both kinds of perceived threat in the reported results [2].

Q: Why might ratings differ between men and women at larger sizes?

A: The articles report that women’s attractiveness ratings showed a plateau at higher sizes, while men’s perceived sexual threat kept increasing, which the authors suggest may reflect men overestimating what matters to women [2][4].
