Indonesian cave hand stencil sets oldest rock art record

Category: Science

Researchers have dated a hand stencil in a limestone cave on Muna Island, off Sulawesi, Indonesia, to at least about 67,800 years old—making it the oldest known rock art reported so far [7][8][9][1][3][4][10][5]. The reddish, claw-like hand outline sits among younger paintings, suggesting people returned to (and kept decorating) the same cave spaces over long periods [12][5][4]. Scientists say the finding strengthens the case that early Homo sapiens in Island Southeast Asia had durable symbolic and artistic traditions and may help refine timelines for human movements toward Sahul (ancient Australia–New Guinea) [3][2][10][13]. It’s a hopeful reminder that creativity and the urge to leave a mark are deep, shared human traits—stretching far beyond a single continent’s story [6][9].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Some hand stencils in caves are engraved into rock rather than painted, showing the motif spans multiple techniques [5].

Key Points

  • How it dated: The team estimated a minimum age by uranium-series dating of tiny mineral crusts associated with the stencil, which can bracket when pigment was placed on the wall [5][7][8].
  • What it looks: Multiple outlets describe the image as a hand stencil with claw-like finger extensions, made with red ochre pigment [1][9][12].
  • Record context: Reports note the new age surpasses prior oldest-known rock-art claims, including earlier finds in the same broader region and older European hand-stencil records often discussed alongside Neanderthal-era cave art [2][10][6][1].
  • Migration clues: Several sources connect the Sulawesi/Muna discovery to debates about when and how people moved through Wallacea toward Australia, suggesting the artists could be linked to populations ancestral to Indigenous Australians [2][10][13].
  • Site preservation: The stencil was found in a tourist-visited limestone cave and had been overlooked amid more recent paintings, underscoring how much important evidence can remain hidden in well-known places [12].

Perspectives

Study authors (Nature paper): They present the hand stencil as rock art dating to at least 67,800 years ago, based on their reported dating analyses, and argue it extends the known timeline of symbolic behavior in the region.

Technical Details

  • Hand stencil technique: A hand stencil typically forms when an artist places a hand on rock and applies pigment around it (for example by blowing it through a tube), leaving a negative outline [5].
  • Uranium-series dating: Uranium-series methods can date calcium-carbonate mineral deposits that form on cave walls; by measuring uranium-to-thorium decay products in these deposits, researchers can estimate minimum ages for underlying artworks [5][8].
  • Minimum age framing: Because the dated material relates to deposits associated with the painting rather than the pigment itself, reports describe the result as a minimum age (‘at least’), not a guaranteed exact creation date [8][5].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The finding broadens the geographic center of gravity for early symbolic behavior by placing the oldest currently reported rock art in Wallacea (Island Southeast Asia), not Europe, and supports the idea that artistic traditions traveled with Homo sapiens across island networks [7][6][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Coverage consistently presents the age as a minimum (at least ~67,800 years) based on indirect dating of mineral accretions, a cautious framing that reflects how cave-art chronologies often rely on datable crusts rather than pigments [8][5].
  • Limitations and future work: Articles note the stencil appears fragmentary and sits among younger art, so additional dated examples from the region—and more sites surveyed carefully—will be important to test whether this is an isolated early case or part of a broader, older artistic landscape [5][12][8].

Historical Background

Hand stencils appear in caves across multiple continents and often form some of the most recognizable Ice Age-era motifs in rock art traditions [5]. In recent years, researchers have repeatedly pushed back the age of figurative and non-figurative rock art in Island Southeast Asia, making the region central to debates about where early symbolic expression flourished [3][8].

Q&A

Q: Why do researchers often date mineral crusts instead of the pigment itself?

A: Many cave pigments (like ochre) can lack enough datable carbon, so scientists date uranium-bearing carbonate deposits that formed on cave walls to bracket the artwork’s age [8][5].

Q: What does ‘Sahul’ mean in this migration discussion?

A: Sahul refers to the combined Ice Age landmass of Australia and New Guinea when sea levels were lower, a key destination in models of early human movement through Island Southeast Asia [common].

Q: Why might a tourist-visited cave still hide major discoveries?

A: The Guardian reports the oldest stencil went unnoticed among more recent paintings, illustrating how faint, fragmentary marks can be easy to miss without targeted documentation and dating [12].
