Hubble data identifies Milky Way merger 12 billion years ago

Category: Science

New observations from NASA's Hubble Space Telescope show definitive evidence that a dwarf galaxy known as LKH collided with and merged into the young Milky Way about 11.8 billion to 12 billion years ago, early in the Milky Way's evolution [2][1][4][5]. The result, published Monday in Nature Astronomy, extends researchers' direct reconstruction of the galaxy's history 1.8 billion years farther back than before [2][1][5]. The study used globular star clusters to trace the ancient collision. ESA said Hubble and Gaia data resolved a long-debated earlier merger that preceded the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus event about 10 billion years ago [2][5][6]. The Conversation reported that the team, led by Davide Massari of the Astrophysics and Space Science Observatory of Bologna, used exceptionally precise ages of dense star clusters to estimate when the merger happened, how massive the incoming galaxy was and how it evolved before becoming part of the Milky Way [6].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Galaxy growth: The Milky Way became a massive spiral galaxy by forming stars from gas clouds and collecting stars, gas and dark matter from smaller galaxies through mergers [5][6].
  • Disc structure: The Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger about 10 billion years ago greatly affected the structure of the Milky Way's disc of stars [5].
  • Difficult period: The Conversation described the Milky Way's first few billion years as among the most formative and hardest to reconstruct [6].

Perspectives

NASA Hubble mission team: NASA presented the finding as definitive evidence from Hubble observations of globular star clusters, not as a simulation-based inference about early Milky Way assembly.

Sources: NASA

ESA: ESA said Hubble and Gaia data resolved a merger whose existence had been suggested by observations and simulations but whose details had been heavily debated.

Sources: ESA

The Conversation: The Conversation described the method as galactic archaeology: stars pulled from an incoming galaxy can preserve clues to their origin billions of years after the merger.

Sources: The Conversation

Historical Background

The Big Bang occurred about 13.8 billion years ago, so a merger 11.8 billion years ago took place when the universe was about 2 billion years old [common][5]. The Milky Way's previously established early merger record included Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus about 10 billion years ago, while the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy merger began more than 6 billion years ago and is still unfolding [5].
