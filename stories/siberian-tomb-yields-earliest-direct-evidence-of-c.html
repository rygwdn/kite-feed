Siberian tomb yields earliest direct evidence of cannabis intoxication

Category: Science

Researchers have identified what they describe as the earliest direct archaeological evidence of cannabis use for its psychoactive effects in a 2,800-year-old burial mound in southern Siberia [21][1][7]. The study, published in Science, combines evidence from more than 1,400 cannabis seeds and chemical traces in human hair from the Tunnug 1 tomb. The findings predate previously known archaeological indications of cannabis use in China by about 300 years [14][2]. Radiocarbon dating placed the seeds in the early eighth century B.C. Researchers detected cannabinol, a breakdown product of THC, and weaker signals consistent with THC in preserved hair. Together, the findings support exposure to psychoactive cannabis, although the seeds alone could not establish how the plant was used [21][7][18]. The burial included extensive horse sacrifices and scattered human bones. The researchers suggest cannabis may have featured in funerary rites, but the idea that it was used to calm people facing sacrifice remains speculative [7][2]. Independent experts questioned whether hair testing can distinguish consumption from contact with smoke or plant material; another archaeologist defended the approach. The team has begun sequencing seed DNA to investigate the plant’s history and spread [10][11][14].

Key Points

  • Seed context: Cannabis accounted for almost all the prehistoric plant material recovered at Tunnug 1, with only a few other wild seed types identified [12].
  • Later evidence: At other Central Asian sites, cannabinoid residues on heated stones and in vessels provide clearer signs of inhalation, although this evidence is younger than the findings at Tunnug 1 [7].
  • Burial layout: The study examined six individuals buried in four wooden structures within the mound [3].
  • Plant origins: The seeds appear to come from wild cannabis, but their presence does not show whether the plants were cultivated or selected for stronger psychoactive effects [7][4].

Perspectives

Volker Auwaerter, forensic toxicologist: Auwaerter argued that the hair analysis did not adequately distinguish consumption from external contamination and said forensic toxicology expertise should have been used [10].

Sources: The Independent

Elisa Guerra Doce, archaeologist at the University of Valladolid: Guerra Doce said toxicological hair analysis has been used in archaeological research and credited the study with evidence that cannabis was consumed by people associated with the burial rite [11].

Sources: The Independent

Marga Sánchez Romero, prehistory professor at the University of Granada: Sánchez Romero said the cannabis could have been used medicinally for pain, rather than chiefly as a ritual intoxicant, and cautioned that grave goods can reflect everyday use [14].

Sources: elDiario.es

Technical Details

  • Hair residue analysis: High-resolution mass spectrometry detected cannabinol, which can form as THC oxidizes, alongside weaker signals matching THC. Hair chemistry alone does not establish the route or amount of exposure [7][18].
  • Radiocarbon dating: Radiocarbon measurements of six seeds, assessed alongside other dating evidence, place their burial in the early eighth century B.C. [7][4].

Historical Background

People cultivated cannabis in East Asia roughly 6,000 years ago for seed oil and other purposes [12][2]. In the fifth century B.C., Herodotus described Scythians inhaling fumes from heated hemp seeds [2][17].
