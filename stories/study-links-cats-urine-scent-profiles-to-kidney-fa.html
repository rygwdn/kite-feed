Study links cats' urine scent profiles to kidney fatty acids

Category: Science

A multinational team that included Iwate University researchers reported that domestic cats can identify other cats from urine scent profiles composed of 13 branched-chain fatty acids, according to findings published online in Current Biology and reported Aug. 19 or this week [1][2][3][4][5][7][8][9]. The researchers found that the mixtures and proportions of these fatty acids differ from cat to cat while remaining relatively stable within each animal, giving urine marks an individual chemical profile [1][2][3][4][5][7][8]. The team traced the compounds to lipid droplets in the outer region of the kidney, suggesting that a structure documented in cat kidneys for more than a century may help maintain stable urine scent signatures [1][3][4][5][7]. Researchers said the compounds evaporate slowly or remain detectable after deposition, helping preserve individual information in a scent mark after urine enters the environment [3][4][5][7][8].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Behavior tests: The team collected urine from 44 domestic cats and ran core recognition tests with seven male subjects [1][8].
  • Scent memory: Cats sniffed less after repeated exposure to the same donor’s urine, investigated again when a new donor sample appeared, and showed reduced responses to familiar urine after months [1][5].
  • Flehmen clue: Researchers used the flehmen response, an open-mouthed facial reaction to odors, as a behavioral clue as they separated urine components and tested which fractions triggered responses [4][7][8].
  • Other felines: The team found similar compounds in the urine of other felids, including lions, tigers and Iriomote cats, though fatty acid compositions differed by species [4][5][7].

Perspectives

Masao Miyazaki, Iwate University: Miyazaki said researchers still need to explain how branched-chain fatty acids stored in renal lipids are released into urine.

Technical Details

  • Branched-chain fatty acids: The study identified 13 BFAs in cat urine; Sci.News reported that researchers had not previously documented this class in mammalian urine or other bodily secretions [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Evidence base: The evidence combines urine chemistry from 44 cats and behavioral experiments in 27 cats. ZME Science noted that several core recognition tests used the same seven male subjects [1].
