Researchers shift AI consciousness debate toward agency

Category: AI

New articles and research papers published Aug. 20 and 21 examine whether current and future AI systems could be conscious, whether researchers can answer that question reliably, and whether policymakers should focus instead on agency, welfare-relevant states and accountability [6][7][8][9][12]. MIT Technology Review argued that public debate has been distorted by rhetoric about “runaway” AI, “rogue” agents and “autonomous” actors, while noting that leaders including Demis Hassabis, Dario Amodei and Sam Altman have called for regulation of advanced systems [6]. The academic work takes different positions on AI consciousness and uncertainty. Tom McClelland’s arXiv paper says assuming AI is not sentient risks serious harm to entities that might deserve moral standing, while assuming sentience risks spending resources on machines that are not conscious; he proposes shifting attention from consciousness itself to whether AI systems could have states that would count as valenced experiences if they were conscious [7]. A Cambridge overview takes a skeptical stance, listing topics such as the Turing Test, the Chinese Room, mimicry arguments, global workspace theories and higher-order theories, and says experts, society and individuals do not know whether AI is or will become conscious [8]. Other contributions address measurement and responsibility. In Quanta Magazine, computer scientist Melanie Mitchell describes AI as an “alien intelligence” that does not think or reason like humans and says researchers lack adequate methods for measuring machine cognition [9]. Willem Fourie’s arXiv paper proposes a three-dimensional typology of agency for advanced AI systems—nature, mode and locus—and discusses individual legal non-human agency without assuming that AI systems are moral agents [12].

Primary Source: View original article

Given deep uncertainty about the possibility of artificial consciousness, it is unclear how we should treat potentially sentient AI.

— Tom McClelland (arXiv)

Did you know: Quanta’s discussion invokes Clever Hans, a horse from the early 1900s, to illustrate how apparent intelligence can reflect cues in the testing setup rather than the ability being measured [9].

Key Points

  • Valence focus: McClelland’s paper is an eight-page contribution to the AISB 2026 Symposium on AI, Consciousness and Ethics [7].
  • Agency framework: Fourie classifies the eight possible agency instantiations as conventional, contested or controversial for policy discussions [12].
  • Measurement problem: Mitchell proposes adapting methods from developmental psychology and animal cognition research, and the Quanta discussion sets out six principles for assessing AI intelligence [9].
  • Skeptical scope: The Cambridge Element reviews materialism and functionalism alongside other theories, and includes a section titled 'Hills and Fog' on uncertainty [8].

Historical Background

Philosophers and computer scientists have long used the Turing Test and John Searle’s Chinese Room argument to debate whether machine behavior can show understanding or consciousness [common]. The in.gr article connects the current debate to Ridley Scott’s 1982 film Blade Runner, which depicted an artificial being with implanted memories confronting its non-human origin [11].
