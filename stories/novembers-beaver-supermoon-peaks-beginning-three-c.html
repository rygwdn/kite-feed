November's Beaver supermoon peaks, beginning three consecutive supermoons

Category: Science

November’s Beaver Moon reached its full phase at 9:53 a.m. Eastern time Tuesday, Nov. 24, beginning a run of three consecutive supermoons, NASA says [5][4][2]. The full moon was about 224,170 miles from Earth; NASA planetary scientist Noah Petro said it appeared about 17% larger and 30% brighter than the year’s smallest full moon [6][2][8]. Because the peak occurred during daylight in North America, evening viewing offered the clearest chance to see it [6][1]. A supermoon occurs when a full moon is near perigee, the moon’s closest point to Earth in its orbit [6][5]. The size difference can be subtle to the eye, while the moon illusion can make the moon seem much larger when it is near the horizon [6][4]. December’s Cold Moon is next, reaching its peak on Dec. 23 [2][4].

We're still exploring our closest neighbor in the universe, and so it's now especially exciting to look up at the moon.

— Kevin Lewis, vice chair and professor of Earth and planetary sciences at Johns Hopkins University (KSL.com)

Did you know: Beavers can store branches underwater at the bottom of ponds so food remains accessible after ice covers the surface [4].

Key Points

  • Meteor showers: The Leonids remain active through Nov. 30, offering a chance to see late meteors [2].
  • Viewing equipment: Binoculars or a telescope can reveal lunar craters and dark plains formed by ancient lava flows [2].
  • Tides: Full moons contribute to larger high tides because the moon’s gravitational pull affects Earth’s oceans, Johns Hopkins professor Kevin Lewis said [2][8].

Timeline

  • 13 December 2026: The Geminids are forecast to peak overnight Dec. 13-14 [4][8]
  • 21 December 2026: The Ursid meteor shower peaks into Dec. 22, near the solstice [4][8]
  • 23 December 2026: The Cold Moon, the final full moon of 2026, reaches peak illumination [2][4]
