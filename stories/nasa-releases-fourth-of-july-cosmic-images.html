NASA releases Fourth of July cosmic images

Category: Science

NASA marked the United States’ 250th anniversary with astronomy releases featuring red, white and blue celestial imagery, including a Hubble Space Telescope view of NGC 6426, a globular cluster in the Milky Way’s outer halo, and a Scientific American feature on Chandra X-ray Observatory images presented as an astronomical fireworks show with sound effects [4][2][3][1]. The Hubble image shows NGC 6426, a globular cluster estimated to be about 13 billion years old, nearly as old as the universe, and NASA described it as one of about 150 known globular clusters in the Milky Way [4][2][3]. Other Hubble releases highlighted Messier 3, which contains more than 500,000 stars, and LH 95, a star-forming region in the Large Magellanic Cloud where observations identified about 2,500 young pre-main-sequence stars [6][7][8].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Image colors: The red, white and blue appearance in the Hubble images comes from telescope filters: blue represents shorter visible wavelengths, while red includes longer visible wavelengths and some near-infrared light [3][8].
  • Cluster dating: Because stars in globular clusters usually form from the same collapsing gas cloud, their similar ages let astronomers use clusters such as NGC 6426 to study early Milky Way history [4][2][3].
  • Distance markers: Messier 3 contains more than 240 RR Lyrae variable stars, the most known in any Milky Way globular cluster, giving astronomers predictable brightness changes for distance measurements [6].
  • Stellar nursery: In LH 95, the brightest blue stars have at least three times the Sun’s mass and use ultraviolet radiation and stellar winds to heat hydrogen gas and sculpt the surrounding nebula [7][8][10].

Scientific Significance

  • Early-universe record: Located in the Milky Way’s outer halo about 35,000 light-years away, NGC 6426 is one of the oldest known globular clusters; its ancient, metal-poor stars preserve a chemical record of how the first massive stars exploded and seeded the cosmos with heavier elements [2][3].
  • Star-formation duration: LH 95 observations indicate that young stars can continue accreting gas and dust from surrounding disks for several million years, longer than some models described by BB.LV assume [7][10].
