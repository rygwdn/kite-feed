Grammarly disables AI ‘Expert Review’ amid class action

Category: AI

Grammarly has disabled its AI “Expert Review” feature after it drew criticism and became the target of a proposed class action lawsuit. The suit alleges the tool used the names of journalists, authors, and academics without consent, presenting editing suggestions as if they came from those experts [2][3]. Multiple outlets said the feature framed suggestions as “inspired by” — or effectively coming from — “leading professionals,” even as some named individuals said they never agreed to take part [2][4][3]. Turning the feature off is a quick, concrete step that should reduce confusion for users. It also creates an opening for a clearer attribution model — one where experts can opt in, set boundaries, or opt out entirely [4]. More broadly, the episode highlights a recurring AI design challenge: making tools feel helpful and human without blurring lines around identity, authorship, and permission [2][4].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Feature access: Futurism reported “Expert Review” was available beyond a free trial through Grammarly’s $12-a-month Pro subscription [4].
  • CEO apology: Shishir Mehrotra, CEO of Grammarly’s owner Superhuman, said the company is disabling the feature while it is “reimagined.” He added that Grammarly “fell short” and apologized in a LinkedIn post [4].
  • Named individuals: Futurism reported tech journalist Kara Swisher objected publicly after the tool claimed to offer advice in her voice, highlighting how personal — and sensitive — AI “voice” claims can be for living writers [4].
  • Timing: WIRED noted Grammarly shut the feature down Wednesday as the controversy grew and the proposed class action took shape [2].
  • Consent claims: Gizmodo reported the lawsuit alleges Grammarly “misappropriated” the names of journalists and other literary figures by including them without consulting them first [3].

Perspectives

Grammarly leadership (via Superhuman CEO Shishir Mehrotra): Mehrotra said Grammarly received “valid critical feedback” that the agent misrepresented experts’ voices, and that the company will give experts “real control” over how they are represented as the feature is rebuilt [4].

Sources: Futurism

Plaintiffs in proposed class action: The suit described by WIRED argues the feature presented suggestions as if they came from established authors and academics without their consent, putting the use of those names at the center of the complaint [2].

Sources: WIRED

Skeptical editors/testing: Futurism reported copy editor Benjamin Dreyer tested the feature with “lorem ipsum” placeholder text, adding to criticism that the “expert” framing could give undue authority to feedback that may be generic or misapplied [4].

Sources: Futurism

Action Items

  • Treat named ‘expert’ suggestions as non-authoritative: If you previously relied on the feature, cross-check key edits against primary style guides or human editors rather than assuming endorsement by the named person [2][common].
