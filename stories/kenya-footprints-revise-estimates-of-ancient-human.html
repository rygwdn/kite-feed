Kenya footprints revise estimates of ancient human relative’s size

Category: Science

Researchers said fossilized footprints on an ancient Lake Turkana shoreline in northern Kenya provide new evidence on the body size and social behavior of Paranthropus boisei, an extinct human relative that lived in East Africa about 1.4 million years ago [5][3][8]. The study, published Monday in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, examined 21 footprints attributed to eight individuals and dated the tracks to roughly 1.43 million years ago [6][2]. The track sizes led researchers to conclude that some P. boisei individuals may have stood up to 6 feet tall and weighed about 165 pounds, dimensions previously associated with Homo erectus rather than this species [6][2]. The same footprints also suggested that a group made mostly of adult males moved together across muddy lakeshore terrain, providing behavioral evidence that skeletal remains alone had not shown [6][5][3]. P. boisei is best known from skulls, jaws and large molars, including cheek teeth described as up to four times the size of modern human molars [7][8]. The findings add locomotion and group-movement data for a species whose anatomy has often been reconstructed from cranial and dental fossils [3][7].

Key Points

  • Shared habitat: The footprint surface also preserved tracks from large animals, including hippopotamuses and antelope, placing the hominins on a busy lakeshore landscape [3].
  • Species overlap: P. boisei lived alongside early Homo species such as Homo erectus in East Africa, but followed a separate evolutionary path that ended in extinction [8][6].
  • Tool ability: Researchers now view P. boisei as having enough manual ability to use tools with a very strong grip, not simply as a less-developed side branch [3].

Perspectives

Kevin Hatala, Chatham University: Hatala said the clustered footprints provide rare direct evidence of group movement, suggesting a social structure not previously documented for P. boisei.

Sources: Kenya Star

Max Planck-led research team: The international team, including researchers from Germany, the United States and Kenya, said the footprints offer direct evidence for the lifestyle and social behavior of P. boisei, rather than relying only on inferences from skulls and teeth.

Sources: Asharq Al-Awsat

Technical Details

  • Preservation: Lakeshore mud preserved the footprints and later turned to rock, capturing fine details of foot shape [5].
  • Species attribution: Researchers identified the footprints as P. boisei based on their wide, robust shape and the absence of a modern human-like arch, distinguishing them from Homo erectus tracks [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Revised size parity: The findings suggest that P. boisei may have been similar in body size to Homo erectus, challenging earlier views that it was a smaller species and implying potential overlap in ecological niches [6][3].
  • Rare behavioral snapshot: The trackway provides one of the few known examples of fossilized hominin group movement, offering evidence of early human relatives’ social behavior that bones alone do not provide [6][5].
  • Limits of inference: The study’s social interpretation rests on a single track surface with 21 footprints from eight individuals, so it documents one preserved movement event rather than a complete picture of the species’ social life [6][2].

Historical Background

Paranthropus boisei lived in East Africa from roughly 2.3 million to 1.4 million years ago and had a bony sagittal crest, flared cheekbones and oversized molars [7]. Those cranial traits earned it the nickname “Nutcracker Man,” reflecting early attention to its chewing anatomy rather than its full body proportions or behavior [7].
