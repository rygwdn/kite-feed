Canada’s budget funds major projects as labour and insurance plans advance

Category: Canada

Canada’s 2026 federal budget commits more than $50 billion over 10 years to Indigenous programs, including $32 billion for First Nations housing and on-reserve infrastructure, while offering new support for major infrastructure projects [1]. It also earmarks $187 million to begin work on the proposed 800-kilometre Mackenzie Valley Highway; Ottawa has proposed paying the estimated $3.5-billion construction cost [2]. Alberta is expected to face a construction-worker shortfall of more than 5,000 by the decade’s end [4]. The budget also proposes a federal earthquake-insurance backstop of up to $50 billion; legislation could be introduced as soon as spring 2027, with insurer levies potentially starting in 2028 [5].

Primary Source: View original article

We need to treat workforce planning as part of project planning. If projects are accelerated without a clear view of the people required, we need to proceed with caution.

— Lance Mortlock, managing partner for the energy segment at Ernst and Young (Canada’s National Observer)

Key Points

  • Investment incentives: The budget’s Productivity Mega Deduction, estimated to cost more than $36 billion over five years, would let businesses immediately write off about 65% of new capital investment [3].
  • Skills pressure: Alberta’s oil and gas industry could need about 69,400 hires by 2035, while 88% of the province’s construction workforce is outside unions and Red Seal programs [4].
  • Debt costs: Federal debt-service costs are forecast to rise from $60.2 billion in 2026-27 to $85.5 billion in 2030-31, according to the budget analysis [3].

Perspectives

Bill Lomax, president and CEO of First Nations Bank of Canada: Lomax argues First Nations should hold equity and ownership stakes in infrastructure and energy projects, rather than being limited to consultation, jobs and contracts [1].

Sources: APTN News

R.J. Simpson, premier of the Northwest Territories: Simpson says the territory must move quickly to build enabling infrastructure before political support fades, and says regulatory delays add weeks or months to project timelines [2].

Sources: The Globe and Mail

Aaron Pete, chief of Chawathil First Nation: Pete says Ottawa’s approval requirements can constrain how communities spend funds and argues for more regional decision-making and support for vulnerable First Nations [1].

Sources: APTN News

Danielle Smith, premier of Alberta: Smith renewed her call for a Quebec-style provincial immigration system, saying employers should be able to identify needed tradespeople such as welders, pipefitters and plumbers [4].

Sources: Canada’s National Observer

Industry Impact

  • Earthquake insurance: The facility aims to prevent multiple insurers from failing at once, which could disrupt access to auto, home and commercial insurance; projected industry levies total about $154 million over three years [5].
