Chad study finds eagles may prey on 26-kilogram baboons

Category: Science

Researchers have found marks on bones and skulls suggesting Verreaux’s eagles preyed on adult male olive baboons in Chad’s Ennedi Plateau, including one estimated to have weighed 26.2 kilograms. The study, published Nov. 18 in the American Journal of Biological Anthropology, challenges a longstanding rule of thumb that primates above about 13 kilograms were effectively beyond avian predation. Previously, no primate heavier than that had been confirmed killed by a raptor [2][1][4]. The team documented punctured eye sockets and talon-like scrapes on shoulder blades among the baboon remains. It did not witness a kill, so the researchers describe eagle predation as a likely explanation rather than a directly observed event [2][3]. Some early hominins were in a similar weight range, but the findings do not establish that eagles hunted adult hominins or influenced their defensive behavior. The researchers plan further study of the isolated baboon population and its behavior [1][5].

At first, the idea of eagles hunting adult male baboons seemed to exist on the thin edge of possibility, until it didn’t. Our skepticism slowly gave way to acceptance. The evidence was convincing.

— Nathaniel Dominy, senior author and an anthropologist and evolutionary biologist at Dartmouth College (Gizmodo)

Key Points

  • Sample details: The initial sample contained nine baboons, including eight adult males; researchers estimated a mean body mass of 22.2 kilograms [2][5].
  • Bone evidence: Two males had punctured eye sockets, and researchers identified a scapula with scrapes in a pattern associated with eagle feeding [2][3].
  • Possible defense: Researchers observed baboons sounding alarm calls and moving to safety when Verreaux’s eagles flew overhead; primates may also mob aerial predators by charging them [3][5].

Perspectives

Robert Simmons, a researcher at the FitzPatrick Institute of African Ornithology, called the finding rare and said limited prey in the dry desert could lead Verreaux’s eagles to hunt larger, riskier animals [4].

Sources: Live Science

W. Scott McGraw, an anthropology professor at Ohio State University, said early hominins, particularly infants and juveniles, may have been vulnerable to raptors and could have relied on group living, warning calls and safer habitats [4].

  • March 2025: The initial expedition recovered the remains of nine olive baboons [2][5]
  • June 2026: Researchers found another male baboon skeleton with talon-like marks [2][5]
  • 18 November 2026: The American Journal of Biological Anthropology published the study on suspected eagle predation [2][1]

Technical Details

  • Body-mass estimation: Researchers estimated baboon mass from the superoinferior breadth of femoral heads, applying established regression equations for cercopithecid primates; one associated male’s estimate was 21.8 kilograms [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Possible hunting mechanism: Verreaux’s eagles may have killed baboons by driving them off cliffs, which would not require carrying such large prey [3].

Historical Background

Researchers previously argued that the Taung Child, an Australopithecus fossil, showed evidence of a raptor-related death, but that interpretation was disputed [2][4].

Q&A

Q: How could an eagle kill prey too large to carry?

A: One possibility raised in reporting on the study is that eagles could drive baboons off cliffs without needing to carry them away. No attack was directly observed [3].
