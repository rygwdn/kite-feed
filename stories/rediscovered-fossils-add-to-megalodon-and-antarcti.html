Rediscovered fossils add to megalodon and Antarctic dinosaur records

Category: Science

Two fossil rediscoveries in museum and research collections have added details to records of prehistoric animals. In Denmark, Bent Erik Kramer Lindow, a vertebrate paleontologist and curator at the Natural History Museum of Denmark, recognized about 20 megalodon vertebrae in 2017 that had been collected in 1978 from clay pits near Gram in Jutland. The material had long been considered lost and was damaged in 1989 [3]. The specimen, dated to about 10.8 million years ago, supports an estimated megalodon body length of up to 24.3 meters [3]. Separately, researchers have identified a small fossil collected on an island off the Antarctic Peninsula on Dec. 9, 1985, as a tail vertebra from a titanosaur sauropod that lived in Antarctica about 83 million years ago during the Late Cretaceous Campanian age [2][4]. The specimen had remained in the British Antarctic Survey geology collection in Cambridge before researchers confirmed it as a dinosaur bone [2][4].

Key Points

  • Collection rediscovery: Lindow found the Danish megalodon material in a box of mixed fragments in 2017; the specimen had not been on public display [3][1].
  • Antarctic record: The Antarctic fossil may represent the first dinosaur fossil found on the continent, based on its 1985 discovery date and later identification as a titanosaur vertebra [4].
  • Field note: Thomson’s notebook sketched the Antarctic fossil, dated it Dec. 9, 1985, and recorded a width of about 10 centimeters [4].

Perspectives

Mark Evans: Evans recognized the British Antarctic Survey fossil among thousands of Antarctic expedition specimens and inferred that the original team probably thought it belonged to a marine reptile.

Paul Barrett: Barrett examined the bone to confirm the find and said it was clearly from a titanosaur.

Scientific Significance

  • Evidence: The megalodon vertebrae provide a rare body-size reference for an animal often reconstructed from teeth, while the Antarctic vertebra adds evidence for titanosaur presence in a region with limited dinosaur remains [3][4].
  • Uncertainty: The Antarctic identification rests on anatomical reassessment of a single tail vertebra, so it supports presence but does not by itself identify a species, population size or broader ecosystem abundance [2][4].
