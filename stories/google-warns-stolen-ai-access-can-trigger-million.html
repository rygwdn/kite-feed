Google warns stolen AI access can trigger million-dollar cloud bills

Category: AI

Google’s Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant say attackers are hijacking companies’ access to large language models (LLMs), running up unauthorized cloud charges of more than $100,000 in a day and up to $1 million before access is cut off. Their report, based on incidents investigated in 2026, describes criminals using stolen cloud credentials and AI API keys to run models or resell access, leaving affected organizations with the bills [1][2][8][6]. The attacks have affected organizations in financial services, retail, technology and transportation across North America, South America and Asia-Pacific. Google says attackers often gain access through exposed or stolen identity tokens, then sell that access through illicit routing services known as LLM Mixers. One such service, Jet Night, advertised unlimited access for about $60 a month [2][7][8]. Google recommends tighter access controls, centralized management of secrets and monitoring for unauthorized AI use and unusual spending [1][7].

Primary Source: View original article

Whenever possible, threat actors want to use this technology to gain a scale advantage over defenders. They achieve that with agentic tools and by stealing tokens from unwitting third parties.

— John Hultquist, chief analyst at the Google Threat Intelligence Group (SecurityBrief Australia)

Key Points

  • Rapid activity: In one case, an attacker made more than 500,000 model API calls in less than eight hours, repeatedly creating and deleting short-lived service accounts to evade limits [1][7][12].
  • Services switched on: In several incidents, AI services had been disabled before the compromise; attackers used stolen access keys to activate them and rapidly run up usage charges [8].
  • Victim infrastructure abused: Some attackers used compromised cloud infrastructure to host unauthorized proxy endpoints and agentic workloads, including custom Node.js bundles with endpoints compatible with multiple frontier models [1].
  • Usage charges, not final losses: The reported sums are unauthorized usage charges observed during incidents, not necessarily the final amounts companies paid. Mandiant did not disclose how many incidents it investigated or how it calculated the figures [6][5].

Perspectives

Nick Tausek, lead security automation architect at Swimlane, warned that checking only for unusual consumption may miss an earlier compromise: Resellers have an incentive to keep stolen access working quietly before usage surges [11].

Timeline

  • 6 May 2024: Sysdig published research naming attacks that stole access to cloud LLMs [5].
  • 18 September 2024: Sysdig described stolen credentials used to enable models and estimated potential daily costs [5].
  • 2 November 2026: Google and Mandiant published their analysis of investigated LLMjacking incidents [5][9].

Technical Details

  • LiteLLM vulnerability: Google said attackers exploited CVE-2026-40217 through the custom-code guardrail testing endpoint in LiteLLM, a model-routing gateway. The vulnerability can allow arbitrary code execution on the server [1].
  • Token validation: Some attackers used `max_tokens=1` requests to check whether exposed API keys were active while keeping resource use per request very low [5][9].

Historical Background

Sysdig's earlier cost figures were projections based on assumed model use, pricing and quotas, not bills verified during incident response. Mandiant's later figures reflected charges observed during its investigations, though it has not disclosed how many cases it reviewed or how it calculated the amounts [5].

Q&A

Q: Why can the reported million-dollar bills accumulate so quickly?

A: LLM charges are based on usage. In several intrusions, attackers first activated AI services that had been disabled before the compromise, then generated large volumes of inference requests [8].

Q: Does a stolen token always bypass multifactor authentication?

A: In an incident described by Google, attackers harvested active session tokens through a fake portal, allowing them to reuse authenticated sessions without a fresh password or multifactor check [2][8].

Action Items

  • Scan and secure code: Automatically scan repositories for exposed credentials, remove leaked keys, and patch model-routing gateways such as LiteLLM [11][9].
  • Watch for intrusion signals: Enable cloud audit and inference logs, and investigate suspicious credential checks, unexpected AI-service activations, and attempts to disable logging [1].
