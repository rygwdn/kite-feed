Researchers use birds and fossils to probe dinosaurs

Category: Science

A set of science reports this week shows how paleontologists are drawing fresh insight from very old remains, from mammal fossils on the Pacific coast to bird skulls that may shed light on dinosaur behavior [1][3]. The common thread is that fossils, along with living descendants, can still help researchers explore how ancient ecosystems worked and how some lineages made it through major planetary change [1][3]. One report says a hamster-sized mammal was already living on the Pacific coast before the non-avian dinosaurs disappeared [1]. Another says scientists found a 240-million-year-old giant reptile fossil inside a 5-meter marine predator [2]. Separately, researchers told The Guardian that birds’ skulls may offer clues to the inner lives of extinct dinosaurs, building on evidence that some modern birds use tools, plan ahead, and show basic forms of empathy [3].

  • Pacific survivor: Phys.org reports that a small mammal lived on the Pacific coast before the dinosaur extinction [1].
  • Predator evidence: The Daily Galaxy reports that the marine fossil preserved one giant reptile inside another and dates to roughly 240 million years ago [2].
  • Living reference: The Guardian notes that experiments on birds have found tool use, future planning, and basic empathy-like responses, making birds a practical comparison point for studies of dinosaur cognition [3].

Perspectives

Bird-behavior researchers: Researchers cited by The Guardian say that studying living birds can offer clues to dinosaur behavior, and that modern birds already show sophisticated behaviors in experiments.

Technical Details

  • Taphonomic rarity: A fossil preserved inside a larger predator is especially valuable because it can capture a feeding event in situ, which is rarer and more direct than reconstructing diet from isolated bite marks or associated bones [2][common].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: Taken together, these reports show how paleontology combines fossil evidence with comparisons to living animals to ask broader questions about behavior, survival, and ecosystem structure [1][2][3].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: Claims about dinosaurs’ inner lives remain inferential because they rely on analogies between bird skulls, bird behavior, and extinct species [3].

Historical Background

Birds are the only surviving dinosaur lineage after the end-Cretaceous mass extinction about 66 million years ago [common]. Paleontologists have long used skeletal similarities between birds and non-avian theropods to study dinosaur evolution [common]. The newer work highlighted here pushes that comparison further, using bird anatomy and behavior to ask how extinct dinosaurs may have sensed and responded to the world around them [3].
