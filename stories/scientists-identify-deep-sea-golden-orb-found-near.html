Scientists identify deep-sea golden orb found near Alaska

Category: Science

Scientists have identified the mysterious “golden orb” recovered from the deep seafloor in the Gulf of Alaska during a 2023 NOAA expedition as the remains of an anemone, ending a two-and-a-half-year puzzle that drew broad public curiosity [1][2][4][5]. The object was attached to a rock more than two miles underwater, and researchers said solving the case took a mix of morphology, genetic analysis, deep-sea expertise, and bioinformatics because the specimen was unusual and hard to classify [2][5].

Primary Source: View original article

This turned into a special case that required focused efforts and expertise of several different individuals.

— Allen Collins (Futurism)

Did you know: The orb was found more than two miles underwater, in a part of the ocean so remote that even seasoned survey teams could not identify it on sight [2].

Key Points

  • A tough identification: When the team first collected the orb, expedition members could tell only that it was biological, showing how even experienced deep-sea surveys can be stumped by incomplete specimens [2].
  • Experts joined forces: NOAA zoologist Allen Collins said the case became “a special case” that needed several specialists, reflecting the cross-disciplinary approach now common in difficult species identifications [2].
  • A rare glimpse: Sci.News reported that the remains likely came from a rarely seen species of anemone, underscoring how few direct observations scientists still have for many deep-sea animals [5].

Technical Details

  • Morphological analysis: Researchers compared the orb’s physical structure with known animal forms, a standard approach that becomes much harder when a deep-sea specimen is damaged or incomplete [2].
  • Genetic analysis: The team used genetic evidence to help classify the specimen, showing how DNA-based methods can narrow down an identification when appearance alone is ambiguous [2].
  • Bioinformatics: Collins said bioinformatics helped solve the case, indicating that computational analysis played a role in classifying the unusual sample [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Uncertainty and confidence: Researchers appear to have gained confidence in the identification by combining several lines of evidence, rather than relying on appearance alone, since they said the case required morphological, genetic, and bioinformatics expertise to solve [2].
  • Limitations and future work: Because the object was a remnant rather than a whole, easily recognized organism, the case highlights a continuing challenge in deep-sea biology: scientists often work from sparse, damaged, or rarely observed material, which makes follow-up sampling especially important [2][5].

Historical Background

Deep-sea expeditions have repeatedly turned up organisms that scientists could not immediately classify, because large parts of the ocean are still sparsely sampled [common]. Sea anemones belong to the phylum Cnidaria, an ancient animal lineage that also includes corals and jellyfish [common].
