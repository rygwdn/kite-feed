January 2026 Wolf supermoon delights skywatchers worldwide

Category: Science

Skywatchers around the world photographed January’s full “Wolf Moon” over the weekend, with multiple galleries highlighting the lunar rise from different locations [1][2]. Several outlets described it as a “Wolf Supermoon,” noting it appeared unusually large and bright in the sky [1][2]. NASA’s January skywatching guide also points stargazers to more easy-to-spot highlights later this month, including Jupiter’s peak visibility of the year and a close-looking pairing of the Moon with Saturn [3].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: NASA says Jupiter can be found in the constellation Gemini during its best viewing period this month [3].

Key Points

  • Jupiter milestone: NASA says Jupiter reaches “opposition” on Jan. 10, when Earth sits between Jupiter and the Sun, making the planet look bigger and brighter than at any other time this year [3].
  • Where to look: NASA notes Jupiter will be visible in the constellation Gemini and should stand out as one of the brightest objects in the night sky, with only the Moon and Venus brighter [3].
  • Moon meets Saturn: NASA says a Moon–Saturn conjunction occurs Jan. 23, when the two appear close together in the sky even though they are far apart in space [3].
  • Beehive cluster: NASA highlights the Beehive Cluster (Messier 44) as a January target, describing it as an open star cluster with at least 1,000 stars [3].

Perspectives

NASA skywatching guide: NASA emphasizes January as a strong month for casual observing, spotlighting Jupiter at opposition, a Moon–Saturn conjunction, and the Beehive Cluster as accessible targets.

Timeline

  • Saturday (Jan. 3): First full moon of 2026 photographed worldwide [1].
  • Jan. 10: Jupiter reaches opposition and its brightest view of the year [3].
  • Jan. 23: Moon and Saturn appear close together during conjunction [3].

Technical Details

  • Opposition: Opposition happens when Earth lies between the Sun and an outer planet; NASA says this geometry makes Jupiter look biggest and brightest for the year [3].
  • Conjunction: A conjunction is when two objects appear close together in the sky from our viewpoint even though they are far apart in space, as NASA describes for the Moon and Saturn on Jan. 23 [3].
  • Open star cluster (M44): NASA describes the Beehive Cluster (Messier 44) as an open star cluster containing at least 1,000 stars, visible during January [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Why it looks brighter: NASA’s guide links Jupiter’s standout brightness this month to orbital geometry—at opposition, Jupiter is favorably placed for viewing from Earth, improving its apparent size and brightness for observers [3].
  • Perspective effects: The Moon–Saturn conjunction is a useful reminder that the sky’s “close pairs” often reflect line-of-sight alignment rather than true proximity, which NASA explicitly notes when explaining conjunctions [3].

Historical Background

People have long used seasonal nicknames for full moons as a way to track time through the year, and “Wolf Moon” is a traditional name for January’s full moon [common]. Separately, “supermoon” is a modern popular term commonly used when a full moon occurs near perigee (the Moon’s closest point to Earth in its orbit), which can make it appear somewhat larger and brighter to observers [common].

Q&A

Q: Why are some full moons labeled “supermoons” in popular coverage?

A: “Supermoon” is a commonly used public term for a full moon occurring near perigee (the Moon’s closest orbital point to Earth), which can make it appear somewhat larger and brighter [common].

Q: What simple equipment helps with these January targets?

A: Binoculars can make features like star clusters easier to spot than with the naked eye, while still offering a wide field of view that’s beginner-friendly [common].

Q: Why does a planet sometimes look brighter in some months than others?

A: A planet’s apparent brightness changes with its distance from Earth and viewing geometry; for outer planets, opposition is a key alignment that improves visibility [common].

Action Items

  • Plan a Jupiter viewing night: On Jan. 10, look east and watch through the evening for Jupiter at opposition, when NASA says it will be biggest and brightest this year [3].
  • Catch the Moon–Saturn pairing: On Jan. 23, look west to spot Saturn just below the Moon during their conjunction, as NASA describes [3].
  • Try for the Beehive Cluster: Throughout January, search for the Beehive Cluster (M44), which NASA highlights as a visible target for the month [3].
