Canada's budget due Thursday as First Nations seek $376 billion plan

Category: Canada

Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne is scheduled to present Canada’s 2026 federal budget on Thursday, Nov. 5, at 4 p.m. The government is expected to emphasize productivity and investment in major infrastructure, including energy, trade corridors, ports, rail and electricity, as it seeks to change the country’s growth model [5][1][4]. The budget is also expected to include the government’s annual immigration plan [8]. The Assembly of First Nations is urging Ottawa to commit to a funded plan, developed with First Nations, to close an infrastructure shortfall it estimates at more than $376 billion over 10 years [2][3]. The Parliamentary Budget Officer projects that the government could miss its commitment to balance the operating budget by 2027-28 by a year, while the government says it is on track to meet the target earlier [6].

Primary Source: View original article

Budgets are not just math. They are instruments that governments use to deliver on their obligations to the rights of all citizens.

— Cindy Woodhouse Nepinak, National Chief of the Assembly of First Nations (CBC News)

Key Points

  • School repairs: An Assembly of First Nations-commissioned assessment found that 77 on-reserve schools need immediate replacement and 227 need additions [3].
  • Other Indigenous priorities: Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami is seeking a $1-billion, five-year Build Inuit Nunangat Fund, while the Métis National Council proposes $2.14 billion over five years for health infrastructure and extended benefits [2].
  • Deficit outlook: The Parliamentary Budget Officer projects a $63.3-billion deficit for 2026-27 and $67.8 billion by 2030-31, compared with the government’s spring projection of $65.3 billion and $53.2 billion, respectively [6].
  • Household relief: The Bloc Québécois proposes a $300 payment to families facing higher living costs [4].

Perspectives

Assembly of First Nations: National Chief Cindy Woodhouse Nepinak said new funding announced at a First Nations-First Ministers meeting—including $630 million for policing and $2.5 billion for emergency preparedness—would not close widening service and infrastructure gaps. She also called for sustained education funding and resources for First Nations to pursue specific claims [3].

Sources: CBC News

Judy Wilson, co-chair of the B.C. Specific Claims Working Group: Wilson criticized the budget’s “Masters of Our Destiny” title, arguing that fast-tracking projects without proper consultation or consent conflicts with Indigenous self-determination and unresolved Crown obligations [3].

Sources: CBC News

Randall Bartlett, deputy chief economist at Desjardins: Bartlett said higher oil prices may temporarily reduce deficits but warned that the revenue boost will fade. He also criticized the government’s distinction between capital and operating spending as unclear and self-interested [6].

Sources: Canada's National Observer

Conservative Party of Canada: Leader Pierre Poilievre is pressing to extend the federal fuel excise-tax holiday until Canada Day, temporarily suspend the GST on fuel and repeal the Clean Fuel Regulations [1][4].

Industry Impact

  • First Nations infrastructure: The Assembly of First Nations says closing the infrastructure gap could create hundreds of thousands of jobs and return $1.82 for every dollar invested, based on its research with the Conference Board of Canada [2].
  • Cultural industries: The federal government previously promised $600 million to offset lost revenue for Canada’s cultural industry. The budget is expected to clarify whether that funding will be allocated [8].

Historical Background

The budget’s title, “Masters of Our Destiny,” echoes a slogan from Jean Lesage’s 1962 Quebec election campaign, which Prime Minister Mark Carney has also invoked since entering politics [4]. The previous federal budget was tabled in November 2025 [5].

Business Angle

  • Consider whether the budget addresses trust and the regulatory and investment conditions that have caused projects to stall, rather than relying on incentives alone [1].
  • C.D. Howe’s Colin Busby called the previously announced $60-billion, five-year savings target timid [6].
  • Assess the productivity tax deduction against evidence of additional business investment, not simply its announced scale [1][6].

Q&A

Q: What could the immigration plan signal?

A: Prime Minister Mark Carney and the immigration minister have said immigration has stabilized and raised the possibility of resuming a controlled increase. The plan may indicate when Ottawa expects that increase to resume [8].

Q: What is the First Nations infrastructure shortfall affecting?

A: The Assembly of First Nations says it spans housing, water and wastewater systems, schools, roads, health facilities and community buildings on reserves [2].
