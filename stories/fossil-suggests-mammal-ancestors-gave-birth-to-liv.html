Fossil suggests mammal ancestors gave birth to live young earlier

Category: Science

Paleontologists studying Chiniquodon theotonicus, a carnivorous cynodont that lived about 236 million years ago in what is now northwestern Argentina, reported fossil evidence suggesting at least one mammal ancestor gave birth to live young rather than laying eggs [3][5][6][4]. The researchers examined thin sections of fossilized femur and ulna and identified a distinct growth transition near the center of each bone, which they described as a neonatal line marking birth [5][4]. The finding would push the origin of viviparity in the mammalian lineage back by about 90 million to 95 million years, according to the study descriptions [3][5][6].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Live birth has evolved independently more than 150 times among vertebrates and defines reproduction in most living mammals today [5].

Key Points

  • Lineage link: Chiniquodon theotonicus has long been placed among cynodonts, a lineage linking earlier synapsids with the first true mammals [6][3].
  • Comparative test: Researchers compared estimated young and adult body weights with thousands of mammals, reptiles and birds to test whether the growth pattern fit live birth [6].
  • Reproductive contrast: Sci.News quoted the authors as saying viviparity contrasts with oviparity, the ancestral vertebrate condition, while monotremes still lay eggs [5].

Timeline

  • 260000000 years ago: Cynodonts first appeared in the Late Permian [3].
  • 252000000 years ago: End-Permian extinction preceded cynodont expansion in the Triassic [3].
  • 236000000 years ago: Chiniquodon theotonicus lived in present-day northwestern Argentina [5].
  • 13 August 2026: Researchers reported the findings [3].

Scientific Significance

  • Evolutionary meaning: The study authors said viviparity is considered an important acquisition in mammalian evolution, characteristic of modern therians and thought to have played a key role in their evolutionary success [5].
  • Evidence strength: The study rests on a neonatal line identified in fossilized femur and ulna tissue, a birth-associated marker documented in living mammals but not previously reported from such an ancient cynodont [5][4].
  • Comparative support: Researchers estimated Chiniquodon theotonicus weighed about 1.7 kilograms at birth and about 12 kilograms when it died, a young-to-adult mass ratio they said resembled placental mammals more than egg-laying reptiles or birds [6].

Historical Background

Researchers long believed cynodonts, the group that includes Chiniquodon theotonicus, laid eggs because they link earlier synapsids with the first true mammals [6]. True mammals evolved later from cynodont relatives, while monotremes retained egg-laying, unlike therian mammals [5].
