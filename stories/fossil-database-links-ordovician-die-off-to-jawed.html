Fossil database links Ordovician die-off to jawed fish rise

Category: Science

A new analysis drawing on fossil database evidence argues that the Late Ordovician mass extinction—about 445 million years ago—reshaped marine ecosystems in a way that helped jawed vertebrates rise to dominance, setting the stage for what later became known as the “Age of Fishes” [4][3][1]. The articles describe how rapid cooling, glaciation over Gondwana, and major shifts in ocean chemistry triggered widespread losses in marine life, clearing ecological space that surviving lineages could diversify into [4][3]. Researchers quoted in coverage say the work connects fossil patterns with ecology and biogeography, offering a clearer map of how catastrophe can redirect evolution’s long arc—ultimately contributing to the rich vertebrate diversity we see today [3].

Historical Background

The Late Ordovician mass extinction is widely recognized as one of Earth’s earliest major mass extinctions, occurring well before dinosaurs and land-vertebrate dominance [common]. The new coverage places it near the end of the Ordovician Period and describes how climate cooling and changing ocean chemistry coincided with large losses of marine biodiversity and a subsequent evolutionary reshuffling [4][3].
