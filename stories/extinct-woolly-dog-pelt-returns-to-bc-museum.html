Extinct woolly dog pelt returns to B.C. museum

Category: Canada

The only known surviving pelt of a woolly dog has returned to British Columbia after roughly 160 years in the Smithsonian Institution’s collection and is now on display at the University of British Columbia’s Museum of Anthropology in Vancouver [1][4][5][3]. The pelt belonged to Mutton, a dog associated with American naturalist George Gibbs, and is on loan from the Smithsonian until August 2027 [4][5]. Museum curator Jordan Wilson said Mutton is thought to have come from the Chilliwack area, in Stó:lō territory in B.C.’s Lower Mainland [1][4]. Coast Salish peoples bred woolly dogs for thousands of years; sources say Makah peoples also raised them for fur used in blankets, ceremonial textiles and other weaving [1][4][5]. The exhibit opened after local Indigenous community members were given time to visit the returning canine ancestor [5].

Key Points

  • Weaving practice: Musqueam weaver Debra Sparrow said weavers spun the dogs’ hair after mixing it with mountain goat wool, and that her grandfather told her every village used to have the dogs [4].
  • Visual record: The woolly dog appears in rare historical photographs, sketches by artist Paul Kane and woven Skokomish/Twana baskets [5].

Perspectives

Atheana Picha: The Coast Salish artist said touching the thick white pelt made her think about how her ancestors might have used the fur, possibly for a blanket or headband.

Historical Background

George Gibbs worked on the Northwest Boundary Survey, a four-year expedition in the late 1850s to map the international land boundary between the American Pacific Northwest and British Columbia [4][5]. The woolly dog breed disappeared sometime around the late 1800s, while Coast Salish oral traditions preserved stories of the white, wool-bearing dogs for more than a century [5].
