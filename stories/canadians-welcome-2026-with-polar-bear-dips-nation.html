Canadians welcome 2026 with polar bear dips nationwide

Category: Canada

On New Year’s Day, Canadians in multiple cities welcomed 2026 by running or wading into frigid lakes and oceans in “polar bear” dips, a tradition that drew both first-timers and long-time regulars [2][6][5]. In Vancouver, the annual Polar Bear Swim at English Bay marked its 106th year and again attracted large crowds, with many participants describing the plunge as a ritual and a personal reset [4]. Ottawa’s New Year’s Day plunge in the Ottawa River also saw participation jump compared with last year, as some swimmers framed it as a motivating challenge to start the year [7].

Primary Source: View original article

It’s better than Halloween, better than Christmas. It’s the highlight of my year.

— Terry Saik (Vancouver Sun)

Did you know: One Vancouver participant said he rebuilt his collection of past Polar Bear Swim buttons by searching Value Village after losing the first few years’ pins [4].

Key Points

  • Vancouver veterans: Vancouver’s English Bay event includes “Polar Bear Veteran’s club” participants, with membership requiring at least 20 dips, and one long-time swimmer described adapting his approach (including water booties and only wading in) to keep participating safely [4].
  • First-time visitors: A visitor to Nova Scotia from Mexico said she was familiar with cold dips at home in an ice-filled tub, but had never done a full polar plunge before trying it in Canada [6].
  • Ottawa turnout: About 250 people joined Ottawa’s plunge, and organizers noted the turnout was more than double last year’s participation [7].
  • Nationwide tradition: Reports described similar New Year’s dips happening across the country, including events in Charlottetown and Sarnia, highlighting how the tradition has become a shared cross-Canada way to kick off the year [1].

Perspectives

Long-time participants (Vancouver): Some returning swimmers describe the English Bay plunge as a defining annual ritual and note the event has evolved over decades with added amenities and more organized safety features.

New participants and visitors: First-timers, including out-of-province and international visitors, framed the dip as a fun way to try something new and share a communal start to the year.

Challenge-seekers (Ottawa): Some Ottawa plungers said they joined specifically to begin the year with a personal test in the Ottawa River’s cold water.

Technical Details

  • Cold-water shock: Sudden immersion in very cold water can trigger an involuntary gasp and rapid breathing, which is why some participants choose to wade rather than fully submerge and why organized events often emphasize controlled entry and safety awareness [4][common].
  • Gradual immersion: Some swimmers reduce risk by entering slowly and limiting how deep they go—an approach described by a Vancouver participant who waded to belly height due to a medical concern [4].
  • Protective footwear: Water booties can help protect feet from cold and sharp objects, and at least one veteran Vancouver swimmer said he uses them to keep participating comfortably [4].

Industry Impact

  • Local events economy: Large, recurring dips can support seasonal business for nearby vendors and event services—food trucks and merchandise were among the features described around Vancouver’s swim [4].
  • Community fundraising potential: While not all dips are charity events, the broad participation and spectator appeal described across cities show why many communities use polar plunges as a ready-made format for local fundraising and volunteer organizing [2][common].

Historical Background

New Year’s Day “polar bear” swims have a long history in parts of Canada, often framed as a communal way to mark a fresh start at the beginning of the calendar year [common]. In Vancouver, the English Bay Polar Bear Swim has run for more than a century and reached its 106th edition in 2026, underscoring how the tradition has been maintained across generations even as the event’s setup has modernized [4].

Q&A

Q: What does it take to be recognized as a long-term participant in Vancouver’s event?

A: The Vancouver Sun reported that the Polar Bear Veteran’s club requires at least 20 dips for membership [4].

Q: What kinds of amenities have been added to Vancouver’s swim over time?

A: A long-time participant said earlier years lacked things like warming tents, merch tables, food trucks and a DJ, which are now part of the event atmosphere [4].

Q: Where did at least one Toronto-area polar dip take place this year?

A: The Globe and Mail photo caption identified a New Year’s Day polar bear dip at Sunnyside beach in Toronto [6].

Action Items

  • If you try a polar dip, plan a controlled entry: Consider wading in gradually and setting a personal limit on depth or time in the water, as some experienced participants do to manage cold exposure [4].
  • Dress for a quick warm-up: Bring warm, dry clothing and easy-to-change layers so you can get out of wet gear quickly after the plunge (a practical step for cold-water comfort) [common].
