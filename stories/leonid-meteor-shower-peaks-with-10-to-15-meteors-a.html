Leonid meteor shower peaks with 10 to 15 meteors an hour

Category: Science

The annual Leonid meteor shower is peaking overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, Nov. 17-18, with observers under dark skies able to see about 10 to 15 meteors an hour [6][2][1]. The meteors come from debris shed by comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, which orbits the Sun roughly every 33 years; scientists do not expect a meteor storm this year [6][3]. The best viewing window is after midnight and before dawn, when the constellation Leo—the shower’s apparent point of origin—rises higher in the sky. The Moon sets around midnight, reducing moonlight during those hours [6][9]. Viewing conditions will vary by region: far northern Scotland is forecast to have more breaks in the clouds, while clouds could hinder viewing in western Washington [2][9].

Key Points

  • Meteor speed: Leonid particles enter Earth’s atmosphere at up to 44 miles (71 kilometers) per second, making them among the fastest meteors visible [2][4].
  • Historic storms: Observers reported as many as 40 Leonids per second at the 1966 storm’s peak; the 1833 storm has been estimated at up to 150,000 an hour [1][6].
  • Nearby planets: Bright Jupiter and Mars appear near the Leonids’ radiant this year and can help skywatchers locate that region of the sky [6][7].
  • Shower activity: Earth passes through the Leonid meteor stream from Oct. 28 through Dec. 7, so meteors may be visible outside the peak night as well [6].

Historical Background

The comet’s returns in 1899 and 1932 went unobserved, and no major meteor storm followed in those years. Its 1998 return produced Leonid showers from 1999 through 2001 [6].

Action Items

  • Choose a dark viewing spot and watch after midnight: Find an open area away from streetlights and give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt; no telescope or binoculars are needed [2][4].
