NASA's Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth, setting distance record

Category: Science

NASA’s Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth on Wednesday, becoming the first human-made object to reach that distance after nearly 50 years in flight [11][17][23]. The probe is now about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers) away; a light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours [19]. Voyager 1 remains in interstellar space and continues sending measurements of magnetic fields and plasma waves from its two remaining operational science instruments as its power declines [11][24]. Its measurements provide direct observations of the region beyond the Sun’s protective bubble, including the effects of solar particles reaching into interstellar space [1]. Radio signals now take 24 hours to travel each way: controllers must wait about two days for a command to arrive and a response to return [11][19]. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory plans a discussion with Voyager team members on Nov. 19 about the probes’ operations, scientific observations and engineering challenges [9].

I feel like this mission is one of the great things that people have ever done, that humans have ever done.

Did you know: At Voyager 1’s present pace, it would take roughly 18,000 years to travel one light-year from Earth [3].

Key Points

  • Dwindling power: Voyager 1’s generator output has fallen from about 470 watts at launch to a little over 210 watts, and the spacecraft loses roughly 4 watts of available power annually [11][13].
  • Signal strength: Voyager’s 12-foot dish transmits using less power than a typical 40-watt refrigerator bulb, and NASA’s 230-foot Deep Space Network antennas collect the faint signal [11].
  • Twin trajectories: Voyager 1 moves outward at about 3.5 astronomical units a year, compared with Voyager 2’s 3.1, after NASA selected a faster route focused on Jupiter and Saturn [8].
  • Power-saving maneuver: In September, controllers completed a coordinated operation dubbed the “Big Bang,” switching off three devices and substituting lower-power alternatives [17].
  • Public event: The Planetary Society scheduled a Nov. 18 livestream featuring Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd, mission manager Kareem Badaruddin and project scientist Linda Spilker [10].

Perspectives

Alan Cummings, Caltech senior research scientist and former Voyager instrument team member: He described the crossing as an engineering milestone rather than a scientific one, crediting generations of engineers with keeping the spacecraft operational [6].

Linda Spilker, Voyager project scientist: She said the team did not expect Voyager to keep operating for nearly 50 years; after the 1989 Neptune flyby, she thought the spacecraft might last only a few more years [16].

Sources: Denver7

Deana Weibel, an anthropologist who studies space exploration: She said it was remarkable that Voyager took more than half a typical human lifespan to reach the milestone [1].

Timeline

  • 5 September 1977: Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral on its outer-planet mission [19].
  • August 2012: Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause and entered interstellar space [19].
  • 20 October 2026: Communications resumed after missed sessions and the activation of backup thrusters [20].
  • 18 November 2026: Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth [11][17].

Technical Details

  • Low-rate uplink: Commands travel to Voyager at 16 bits per second, limiting the amount of command data NASA can send across the one-day signal delay [4].
  • Radioisotope generators: Voyager’s radioisotope thermoelectric generators convert heat from decaying plutonium-238 into electricity [11][13].

Scientific Significance

  • Planetary discoveries: During their outer-planet flybys, the Voyagers found active volcanoes on Jupiter’s moon Io and evidence of a cracked water-ice crust on Europa, which is now thought to hide a potentially habitable ocean [1].
  • Pressure front: An incoming pressure front that began around 2020 caused magnetic fields around the Voyagers to rise sharply before dropping six years later; researchers are investigating the cause [1].

Historical Background

In 1965, doctoral student Gary Flandro identified a rare alignment of the outer planets that would let spacecraft use successive planetary flybys to travel outward [4]. The alignment, which recurs about every 175 years, inspired NASA’s Grand Tour concept, but the four-probe plan was canceled after budget cuts in the early 1970s [4]. NASA then reworked the proposal around the less costly Mariner spacecraft design, producing the Voyager missions [4].

Q&A

Q: Does crossing one light-day mark a new boundary in space?

A: No. It is a distance milestone, not a change in the spacecraft’s surroundings or a new boundary [17].

Q: When will Voyager 2 reach the same distance?

A: NASA expects Voyager 2 to reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035 [19].
