Only 35% of announced US AI data center capacity may materialize

Category: Business

Only about 35% of announced U.S. data-center capacity is likely to be built, according to a Bernstein Research analysis of thousands of planned projects cited by Business Insider. Power shortages and speculative proposals are complicating the artificial-intelligence construction boom [1]. Some developers have announced vast facilities without securing electricity, permits or customers. Texas grid-connection requests for data centers exceed five times the state’s existing electricity supply [1]. Business Insider contrasted these proposals with Amazon, which it said is struggling to secure power for its AI equipment [1]. The buildout also faces voter resistance ahead of U.S. midterm elections. A CNBC survey found 63% of voters oppose a data center near their home, and 52% favor pausing advanced AI development until Congress passes safety rules [3]. Candidates have proposed making developers pay for electricity and grid upgrades. The House has approved the bipartisan Ratepayer Protection Act, while President Donald Trump has urged developers to generate their own power and pay host communities a dividend [4][3].

Key Points

  • Texas review: Texas Gov. Greg Abbott halted data-center permits pending reviews of grid and water impacts and called for ending state tax incentives [3].
  • Ohio campaign: The National Republican Senatorial Committee warned that data-center opposition could threaten Sen. Jon Husted’s reelection, while Husted promoted legislation requiring large facilities to cover grid and generation costs [3].

Perspectives

Brad Carson, a former Democratic congressman and co-founder of Public First Action PAC, said communities want reasonable safeguards for AI, making local opposition difficult for politicians to overcome.

Business Angle

  • Developers may submit multiple electricity-connection requests hoping at least one location works out, so raw request totals can overstate the committed project pipeline [1].
  • Some large announced facilities come from companies with little experience building data centers [1].

Q&A

Q: What concerns do residents raise beyond electricity costs?

A: Opponents have cited water demand, noise, land use and taxpayer subsidies, as well as fears that communities bear the costs while technology companies receive the benefits [3].
