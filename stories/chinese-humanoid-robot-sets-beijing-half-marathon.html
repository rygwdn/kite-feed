Chinese humanoid robot sets Beijing half-marathon record

Category: Technology

A humanoid robot built by Chinese company Honor won Beijing's half-marathon for robots after finishing the 21-kilometer course in 50 minutes and 26 seconds on April 20. The event's scoring favored autonomous performance over remote control, so independence mattered as much as speed [1][3][4][5][6][7][8]. Multiple reports said that time was faster than the human half-marathon world record of 57 minutes and 20 seconds, recently set by Jacob Kiplimo in Lisbon, making the race a vivid sign of how quickly legged robotics is advancing [3][4][5][6][7]. The event in Beijing's E-Town technology hub was both a public spectacle and a field test for humanoid systems, with roughly 40% of robots running autonomously and the rest controlled remotely [1][2][7]. Coverage across outlets described a sharp leap from last year's inaugural race, when the fastest robot took two hours and 40 minutes, suggesting that gains in balance, cooling, gait design, and autonomy are pushing these machines beyond demo-stage novelty and closer to practical real-world performance [1][2][6][7][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Did you know: Mashable reported that Honor also showed a "Robot Phone" at Mobile World Congress, with a robotic eye and arm that extends from the handset to take photos and respond to requests [8].

Key Points

  • Big field: More than 100 humanoid robots took part alongside about 12,000 human runners, and organizers used separate tracks to reduce collision risks during the event [7].
  • Honor sweep: Honor took the top three places, and Digitimes reported that the company placed six units among the top ranks, underscoring how aggressively it has expanded into robotics from its smartphone engineering base [8][9].
  • Accessibility demo: At the same event, Amap introduced its quadruped robot Tutu, which the company said can guide visually impaired users through open environments without preset routes or remote control [10].

Perspectives

TechRepublic: The outlet framed the race as more than a publicity stunt, calling it a stress test that offered a stronger measure of humanoid progress than last year's more heavily remote-controlled field.

Technical Details

  • Weighted autonomy scoring: The competition did not simply reward the fastest elapsed time. An Honor robot ran 48 minutes and 19 seconds under remote control, while the 50:26 robot won because the event's scoring system gave more weight to autonomous operation [1][6].
  • Leg-length tuning: Honor engineers said the winning robot used legs approximately 95 centimeters long, a geometry choice meant to mirror elite human runners and improve stride efficiency at speed [5].
  • Liquid cooling: The robot incorporated a custom liquid-cooling system derived from smartphone technology, a design choice meant to manage heat during sustained high-output movement and one engineers said could translate to industrial uses [5].

Industry Impact

  • Consumer electronics crossover: Ars Technica and Digitimes both highlighted that Honor's robot program draws on smartphone-derived engineering, suggesting existing electronics firms may be more credible humanoid competitors than many expected [5][9].
  • Autonomy benchmarking: TechRepublic said this year's less remote-controlled field made the race a better real-world benchmark for humanoid progress than the prior event, which could shape how companies demonstrate capability to customers and investors [2].
  • Assistive robotics: Amap used the marathon to showcase a guidance robot for visually impaired users, signaling that mobility-assistance applications are emerging alongside entertainment and athletics demos [10].

Historical Background

Last year's inaugural Beijing robot half-marathon produced a winning robot time of two hours and 40 minutes, far behind this year's leaders [1][6][7]. Several reports said this year's field also relied less on remote control, with about 40% of entries operating autonomously, making the 2026 race a more meaningful test of onboard locomotion and navigation [1][2][7]. More broadly, modern humanoid robotics has accelerated in the 2020s as improvements in batteries, actuators, sensing, and control software made longer untethered demonstrations increasingly feasible [common].

Action Items

  • Watch for real-world pilot programs: Track whether companies highlighted at the race announce logistics, industrial, or accessibility pilots, because those deployments will show whether the technology is moving beyond demonstrations [10][2].
  • Compare autonomy claims carefully: When assessing future robot announcements, distinguish between fully autonomous performance and remote-controlled demos, since the Beijing race scored them differently [1][6].
