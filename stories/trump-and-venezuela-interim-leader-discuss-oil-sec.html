Trump and Venezuela interim leader discuss oil, security

Category: World

President Donald Trump says he held a lengthy phone call with Venezuela’s interim president Delcy Rodríguez on Wednesday, describing the conversation as positive and his first known direct contact with her since Nicolás Maduro’s ouster [1][2][5][7][4]. Both sides said the exchange was courteous and productive, and Trump said the two discussed oil, minerals, trade and national security [1][2][4]. The call signals a diplomatic opening after recent upheaval in Venezuela, even as questions remain over who will lead the country’s political transition and how quickly conditions will improve for Venezuelans [11][8].

Primary Source: View original article

We just had a great conversation today, and she’s a terrific person

— President Donald Trump (South China Morning Post)

Did you know: Euronews notes Rodríguez has been closely associated with Venezuela’s oil management portfolio in recent years [9].

Key Points

  • White House meetings: Trump is scheduled to receive Venezuelan opposition leader María Corina Machado at the White House on Thursday, adding a second track to Washington’s engagement with Venezuela’s new power structure [6][8].
  • Prisoner releases: Rodríguez said her government will continue releasing people detained under Maduro, presenting the move as part of a shift toward a more open political moment [3][9][10].
  • NGO dispute: Rodríguez accused NGOs of misrepresenting the prisoner-release process, while rights groups cited by reports estimate close to 1,000 political prisoners remain in Venezuela [10].
  • Rodríguez profile: Rodríguez previously served as Maduro’s vice president and has been linked in reporting to oversight of intelligence and management of the oil sector—background that shapes how both supporters and critics interpret her promises of a “new era” [9][11].
  • US strategy focus: One account says the Trump administration has framed control over Venezuelan oil production and exports as a central pillar of its approach, putting energy policy at the heart of the new relationship [4].

Perspectives

US President Donald Trump: Trump said the call was “great,” praised Rodríguez personally, and said they discussed oil, minerals, trade and national security as areas for progress between the two countries.

Sources: Deutsche Welle

Venezuela interim president Delcy Rodríguez: Rodríguez described the call as “productive and courteous,” and has pledged to continue releasing detainees from the Maduro period while portraying it as the start of a new political moment.

Sources: Al Jazeera

Venezuelan NGOs and rights groups: Rights groups cited in reporting say releases have been slow and opaque and estimate that close to 1,000 political prisoners remain despite official claims of a new era.

Sources: The Guardian

Venezuelan opposition (María Corina Machado): Machado is seeking support from Washington as Trump prepares to meet her, highlighting a parallel contest for international backing during Venezuela’s transition.

Sources: El País

Timeline

  • January 3: A US raid is reported after Maduro’s removal [4].
  • Wednesday: Trump and Rodríguez hold their first known call [1][2].
  • Thursday: Trump is set to meet opposition leader Machado [6][8].

Industry Impact

  • Oil and shipping: If US policy truly centers on Venezuelan oil production and exports, that could ripple into tanker routing, export enforcement and energy-trade compliance planning for firms dealing with Venezuelan crude [4].
  • Human-rights monitoring: Public commitments to prisoner releases could raise demand for independent verification and legal support services, especially amid disputes between the government and NGOs over transparency [10][9].

Historical Background

Venezuela has faced years of political confrontation between the Maduro-aligned state and opposition movements, with political detentions becoming a major international human-rights concern [10]. In early January 2026, reporting describes a dramatic shift in power after Maduro was removed from office, creating a fast-moving transition in which outside recognition—especially from Washington—has become a key lever [1][2][11].
