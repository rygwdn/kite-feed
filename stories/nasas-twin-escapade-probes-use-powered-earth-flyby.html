NASA's twin ESCAPADE probes use powered Earth flybys to head for Mars

Category: Science

NASA’s twin ESCAPADE spacecraft completed powered flybys of Earth on Nov. 7 and 9, using the planet’s gravity and their own engines to set course for Mars. This was the first time NASA used a powered Earth flyby to propel a spacecraft to Mars [2][1]. The probes passed about 1,600 kilometers and 1,050 kilometers above Earth, respectively [2]. The spacecraft are expected to reach Mars in September 2027, where each will fire its main engine for about 11 minutes to enter orbit. Their paired measurements will track how the solar wind interacts with Mars’ magnetic environment and atmosphere. Science operations are planned to begin in June 2028 [3][6]. A rocket delay caused the mission to miss the October 2024 launch opportunity. After launching in November 2025, the probes spent nearly a year in a looping orbit before returning to Earth [1][2].

We were able to capture the magnetic field structure of that distant tail, how it flaps, how it moves, how the plasma flows within it

Key Points

  • Earth observations: En route, the probes sampled Earth’s magnetotail about 1.19 million miles away and later observed it alongside two ARTEMIS-THEMIS spacecraft near the Moon [1].
  • Paired measurements: The spacecraft will initially follow the same Mars orbit, separated by as much as 30 minutes. Later, one will measure the incoming solar wind while the other measures Mars’ response [2][4].
  • Program context: Two earlier CubeSat-class missions in NASA’s Small Innovative Missions for Planetary Exploration (SIMPLEx) program, LunaH-Map and Q-PACE, also failed after launch [2].

Perspectives

Jeff Parker, Advanced Space: Parker said the trajectory allows launches outside the Mars launch windows that occur roughly every two years, using Earth’s gravity to boost spacecraft toward Mars [2].

Timeline

  • October 2024: ESCAPADE was scheduled to launch during a favorable Mars alignment window [1][5].
  • November 2025: The twin spacecraft launched aboard Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket [1][4].
  • 7 November 2026: The first probe passed Earth and fired its engine during the flyby [2][3].
  • 9 November 2026: The second probe completed its powered Earth flyby, following its twin by two days [2][3].
  • September 2027: Both spacecraft are scheduled to reach Mars and conduct orbit-insertion burns [2][3].
  • June 2028: The mission plans to begin scientific observations after Mars-orbit adjustments [3][6].

Industry Impact

  • NASA, citing budget pressures, has not solicited proposals for new SIMPLEx missions and has not stated plans to do so for the foreseeable future [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Atmospheric loss processes: The mission will investigate ion escape, in which charged particles leave Mars, and sputtering, in which energetic particles knock atmospheric material into space [4].
  • Ionospheric effects: The instruments will also characterize Mars’ ionosphere, where variations can affect the passage of communication and navigation signals [4].

Historical Background

ESCAPADE was selected in 2019 as one of three missions in NASA’s Small Innovative Missions for Planetary Exploration program [2]. NASA canceled the Janus asteroid mission in 2023 after launch delays disrupted its planned rideshare, while the Lunar Trailblazer spacecraft was lost after its February 2025 launch and the agency ended that mission five months later [2].
