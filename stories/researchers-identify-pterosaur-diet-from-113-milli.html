Researchers identify pterosaur diet from 113-million-year fossil

Category: Science

Researchers studying a 113-million-year-old pterosaur wing bone from northeastern Brazil said it preserved rare soft-tissue traces, organic molecules and chemical signals linked to diet, according to a study published in iScience [1][2][4]. The fossil, a hollow wing phalanx from the Romualdo Formation in the Araripe Basin, was encased in a limestone or carbonate concretion that preserved it in three dimensions rather than flattening it under sediment pressure [1][2][4]. The team used high-resolution CT scanning, micro-mineral imaging and organic geochemical analysis to examine the bone without destroying it, identifying collagen-like fibers, mineralized soft tissues and steroid biomarkers [1][2][3]. Carbon-isotope measurements of cholesterol-derived compounds indicated the anhanguerid pterosaur likely hunted above Cretaceous seas and fed heavily on fish and cephalopods such as squid or nautilus relatives [2][3][4].

Did you know: Microbial activity during decay can drive mineral reactions that later lock fossil tissues and molecules in place [1][3].

Key Points

  • Preservation pathway: Scans showed mineral layers of different densities inside the hollow bone, indicating a multi-stage sequence of chemical events after burial [1][2].
  • Specimen origin: Researchers examined a partial left wing found at the Sítio Baixa Grande locality in the northwestern Araripe Basin [2].
  • Study authorship: Curtin University Professor Kliti Grice led the work with colleagues, and the paper carries the DOI 10.1016/j.isci.2026.116199 [2].

Perspectives

Professor Kliti Grice said, 'Our findings open a new window into fossil formation.'

Sources: Sci.News

Scientific Significance

  • Limits of scope: The finding comes from a single partial wing, so it identifies chemical signals in one anhanguerid individual rather than measuring variation across pterosaur species or life stages [2].

Historical Background

Pterosaurs were flying reptiles that lived alongside dinosaurs and were among the first vertebrates to achieve powered flight [4]. Some pterosaurs reached wingspans of up to 12 meters, while their thin, hollow bones reduced their weight but usually made detailed fossil preservation less likely [1][4]. The Romualdo Formation has produced exceptionally preserved fish, turtles, crocodile relatives and pterosaurs, making it one of Brazil’s best-known Cretaceous fossil deposits [1][2].
