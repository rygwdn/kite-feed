Spanish rail unions challenge human-error blame as crash probes widen

Category: World

Spanish rail unions are challenging the official focus on human error after a high-speed collision near Urda killed 69 people, the country’s second deadly crash on the Madrid-Seville line this year [3][27]. A judge and a government commission are investigating the Oct. 28 crash, which happened after the LZB train-protection system failed and a Renfe train struck a stationary Iryo service [3][13]. Transport Minister Óscar Puente has called the controller’s authorization for the Renfe train to resume at normal speed “inexplicable.” The traffic controllers’ union says understaffing and deteriorating working conditions made the job harder, while train drivers say the aging LZB system should have been replaced [3][48][17]. Investigators are examining the controller’s actions and the safety systems in use. The train drivers’ union SEMAF went ahead with a previously scheduled safety strike on Oct. 29, and Spain’s two main parties suspended campaign events ahead of the Nov. 29 election [25][27][1].

Key Points

  • Maintenance spending: Spain devoted about 16% of its high-speed rail spending to maintenance, renewals and upgrades from 2018 to 2022, compared with 34% to 39% in France, Germany and Italy [8].
  • Strike disruption: The pre-planned Oct. 29 strike saw 26.6% of the Renfe staff covered by minimum-service requirements join; Madrid’s commuter network canceled 81% of scheduled services [25].
  • Safety warnings: Train drivers had warned of increased vibrations and rolling instability on several rail corridors and called for lower speed limits or stronger maintenance [24].
  • Independent review: Opposition leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo called for an independent review of the railway network [8][1].

Technical Details

  • LZB failure procedure: The LZB system monitors train positions and can trigger automatic braking; when it fails, drivers are generally instructed to proceed on sight at no more than 40 km/h, allowing them to stop for obstacles [3][13].
  • Signalling transition: The EU’s rail network has more than 25 non-interoperable signalling systems; Spain’s Madrid-Andalusia line still uses LZB while the European Rail Traffic Management System is intended to replace national systems across the bloc by 2050 [13].

Industry Impact

  • Passenger rail services: The Madrid-Seville high-speed route was closed after the crash, leaving thousands of passengers seeking alternative transport; the strike also disrupted commuter services [29][25].

Historical Background

A 2013 derailment near Santiago de Compostela killed 80 people. In 2016, the European Union Agency for Railways found that Spain’s investigation of that crash did not meet required independence standards and recommended a new inquiry; the European Commission later opened infringement proceedings over wider railway safety oversight shortcomings [8].

Humanitarian Impact

Authorities said 42 foreign citizens from seven countries were involved; one was killed and at least seven were injured [27]. The Civil Guard opened offices in Madrid, Toledo, Córdoba and Seville for relatives to provide DNA samples to help identify victims, and blood donation points were established in Castilla-La Mancha and Madrid [27][45].
