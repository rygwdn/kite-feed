Ancient DNA reclassifies North American ‘American cheetah’

Category: Science

A study led by University of California, Santa Cruz researchers found that Miracinonyx trumani, the extinct North American predator commonly called the “American cheetah,” was more closely related to modern pumas than to African cheetahs [2][6][5]. The team used ancient DNA from four specimens and stable isotope evidence from fossils, including three from Canada’s Yukon and one from Natural Trap Cave in Wyoming, to reassess the animal’s ancestry, range and diet [4][5][6]. The findings revise a decades-old view that the cat filled a cheetah-like ecological role on North America’s Pleistocene grasslands [5][4]. Researchers reported that Yukon fossils previously assigned to another big cat were also M. trumani, placing the species as much as 20 degrees of latitude farther north than scientists had assumed [4]. Chemical evidence also indicated that northern individuals ate fish in Arctic environments rather than specializing only in fast-running prey [3][6][8].

Key Points

  • Anatomy: The cat’s long forelimbs, large nasal cavities and slender body had led paleontologists to compare it with fast-running animals such as modern cheetahs and horses [4][5].
  • Pronghorn clue: Some fossils appeared near pronghorn remains, contributing to the earlier view that M. trumani hunted like African cheetahs on open terrain [4][5].
  • Body size: Fossil estimates put M. trumani at about 90 centimeters tall, 2.5 meters long to the tail tip and roughly 70 kilograms on average [5].
  • Genetic adaptation: The researchers found loss-of-function mutations in genes tied to circadian rhythms and linked them to extreme seasonal light cycles at northern latitudes [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Evolutionary classification: The genome evidence places the animal closer to the puma lineage than to modern cheetahs and indicates that similar body shapes can evolve in unrelated cats under comparable ecological pressures [2][6][5].
  • Evidence limits: The genetic conclusions rest on four of the most complete M. trumani DNA sets available, so the study strengthens the case but still relies on a small fossil sample for a species that ranged across North America [4][5].

Historical Background

The species lived during the Pleistocene epoch, about 2.5 million to 13,000 years ago [4][5]. During parts of that epoch, exposed land in Beringia connected northeastern Asia and northwestern North America, allowing large mammals such as woolly mammoths to move between continents [4].
