Researchers identify gaps in AI safety testing

Category: AI

Several AI research papers and engineering reports published in August 2026 examined weaknesses in how organizations test AI systems before deployment, with examples spanning coding agents, aviation collision avoidance, military command and control, and prediction-based certification [1][2][3][6][4]. The articles examined whether current evaluation practices support safety claims when AI systems behave non-deterministically, operate inside defined deployment domains, or produce correct outputs for the wrong reasons [2][3][6][4]. The aviation paper proposed a process for assessing whether AI/ML operational design domain data are sufficiently representative for safety assurance, citing EASA learning-assurance expectations and demonstrating the method on prior HCAS and VCAS collision-avoidance simulation data [2]. A separate military command-and-control paper reviewed 240 documented testing and evaluation practices and found that agentic properties weaken assumptions about system specifiability, stability, composability and supervisability [3]. Another arXiv paper argued that accuracy, calibration and conformal coverage cannot by themselves certify trustworthiness because models can match on prediction-side certificates while diverging in explanation fidelity and deployment behavior [6]. Two engineering posts described applied testing results. Towards Data Science described 28 debugging experiments and said AI coding harnesses struggled more with missing information than with task complexity [1]. PagerDuty reported that an A/B test of AI agent tooling found no quality gain from splitting standards into domain-specific skills when the right skill loaded [4]. A Google News listing also included a report describing an IQ-testing-derived method that found cracks in AI safety benchmarks, but the provided source text did not include methodological details [5].

For ordinary code, that evidence standard wouldn’t survive review. We’d ask for tests. We’d ask what happens on the unhappy path. Agent tooling deserves the same skepticism, and arguably more, precisely because of the non-determinism that makes a single good run mean so little.

— Sarah Martinelli Benedetti (PagerDuty)

Key Points

  • C2 limits: The military C2 study derived ten assurance claims for three assumption clusters and mapped operational consequences through five command-and-control scenarios [3].
  • Hidden failures: PagerDuty’s experiment found that domain-specific skill splitting introduced a silent failure mode in which the agent sometimes selected no skill and worked without standards [4].
  • Mechanism evidence: The competence-envelope paper said failures invisible in prediction behavior require evidence about the model’s decision mechanism, not only its outputs [6].

Perspectives

Trustworthy AI researchers: Nataliya Shakhovska, Ivan Izonin and Stergios-Aristoteles Mitoulis introduced the competence envelope as an operational framework to capture decision-mechanism properties that prediction-side certificates miss.

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: The papers examine AI evaluation methods for deployment-domain coverage, run-to-run behavior and decision mechanisms, rather than aggregate performance alone [2][3][6].
  • Uncertainty and confidence: The sources include structured reviews, proofs and case demonstrations, but the provided abstracts and descriptions do not report external replication of the proposed aviation method, competence envelope or C2 assurance framework [2][3][6].
  • Limitations and future work: The military C2 paper did not assess supervisability because that evidence depends on system stability results and human-factors testing beyond its scope [3].

Historical Background

Conformal prediction, calibration and accuracy have become common prediction-side tools for evaluating model reliability, but they assess output behavior rather than internal reasoning or causal decision paths [common]. Aviation certification has long required explicit safety cases for systems whose failures can endanger life, and EASA’s AI work extends that assurance logic to learning-enabled components [2]. Military command-and-control systems also use formal testing and evaluation practices, but the reviewed paper argues that agentic AI changes the assumptions those practices rely on [3].
