JWST cluster models offer route to early supermassive black holes

Category: Science

Two studies propose that dense star clusters in the early universe could have produced massive black-hole seeds, potentially explaining how supermassive black holes grew so quickly [2][3]. The James Webb Space Telescope has observed candidate young clusters, but researchers have not directly detected black holes forming in them [3][4]. One model finds that repeated stellar mergers could build stars more than 200 times the Sun’s mass, while another estimates that clusters in the Cosmic Gems arc could produce black holes with masses of 1,600 to 2,700 times the Sun’s [2][3]. The studies also suggest that dense gas around black holes could help explain the early universe’s “Little Red Dots” [2]. Cluster structure, rotation and the initial mass function remain uncertain [3][4].

Key Points

  • Different clusters: The separate model estimates smaller seed black holes of 180 to 810 solar masses in the less compact Firefly Sparkle clusters [3][4].
  • Collision threshold: In simulations by a team led by Claire Williams, a University of California, Los Angeles doctoral student, clusters with densities above roughly 100,000 solar masses per cubic parsec formed stars more than 200 times the Sun’s mass across nearly all tested low-mass systems [2].
  • Model checks: Researchers used a runaway-collision model that accounts for mass loss through stellar winds to estimate black-hole masses in Cosmic Gems and Firefly Sparkle, then checked the results against direct N-body simulations of compact clusters [3].

Technical Details

  • Metallicity and stellar winds: The Cosmic Gems clusters’ low metallicity is expected to limit mass loss through stellar winds, helping merger-built stars retain enough mass to form larger black-hole seeds [3][4].
  • Dynamical friction: In the Williams team’s model, merger products sink toward a cluster’s center through dynamical friction, increasing the chance of further collisions [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Evidence gap: Observational evidence for intermediate-mass black holes remains limited, though candidates include Omega Centauri and a 142-solar-mass remnant inferred from gravitational waves [3].
