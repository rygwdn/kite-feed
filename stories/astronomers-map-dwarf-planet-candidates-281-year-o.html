Astronomers map dwarf planet candidate's 281-year orbit, seek size confirmation

Category: Science

Astronomers have mapped a 281-year orbit for 2026 ED138, an icy body beyond Pluto that they are investigating as a possible dwarf planet. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile detected it, and its estimated orbit takes it between 36.86 and 49.04 astronomical units from the Sun [4]. Estimates of ED138’s diameter range from 600 to 1,500 kilometers because reflected sunlight alone cannot distinguish a small, bright object from a larger, darker one. To qualify as a dwarf planet, it must have enough mass for its gravity to make it nearly round. Measuring its physical size is central to deciding whether it belongs among the five dwarf planets currently recognized by the International Astronomical Union [2][15]. The object’s orbit is tilted about 25 degrees to the solar system’s plane. Astronomers found it against the star-dense Milky Way after earlier surveys missed it. They are seeking follow-up observations, including measurements of heat emitted at longer wavelengths. Matthew Holman, the astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard & Smithsonian who identified it, said determining its physical size could take a few months [6][13][15].

The main thing we need to do is to establish the physical size of 2026 ED138.

Did you know: Archival Dark Energy Camera images also captured ED138 in 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2023 [4].

Key Points

  • Survey cadence: Rubin’s decade-long Legacy Survey is designed to image the southern sky every few days, allowing researchers to identify moving objects by comparing repeated observations [6][14].
  • Future naming: If ED138 is confirmed, astronomers would assign it a permanent number before giving it a formal name; Matthew Holman said some suggested names he has received are already in use for other objects [13].

Perspectives

Matthew Holman, astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard & Smithsonian: Holman said ED138’s discovery neither supports nor rules out the existence of Planet Nine, but shows that undiscovered objects may remain in the outer solar system [1].

Sources: Futurism

Michele Bannister, associate professor at the University of Canterbury: Bannister said that if a spacecraft ever visited ED138, it might find a nitrogen glacier like Pluto’s, though its physical characteristics remain unknown [13].

Sources: Channel 3000

Mario Jurić, University of Washington professor and Rubin Solar System team leader: Jurić described finding an object of this size as an event that may happen once in a generation and said it is the largest trans-Neptunian object found in about 20 years [3].

Sources: Discover Magazine

Timeline

  • 2016: Archival Dark Energy Camera images later revealed ED138 [4][6]
  • March 2026: Rubin observations of ED138 began appearing in its image records [3][19]
  • 13 October 2026: Matthew Holman’s search software flagged the moving object [3][6]
  • 27 October 2026: The observatory and Minor Planet Center announced the candidate [22][2]

Technical Details

  • Additional measurement methods: If ED138 passes in front of a distant star, timing how long it blocks the starlight could help determine its dimensions; finding a moon could help researchers estimate its mass [5].
  • Orbital dynamics: Researchers classify ED138 as a hot-classical trans-Neptunian object; its unusual orbit may reflect gravitational encounters with Neptune during the solar system’s early evolution [6][5].

Historical Background

The International Astronomical Union adopted its formal definition of a planet in 2006, reclassifying Pluto because it had not cleared its orbital neighborhood [2][5]. Its list of recognized dwarf planets expanded to five with Haumea’s classification in 2008 [5].

Q&A

Q: Why did earlier sky surveys miss an object this large?

A: Its position in the southern sky limited access for many northern observatories, and fewer telescopes were searching the southern sky [13][9].
