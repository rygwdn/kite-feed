Update: Trump extends Iran ceasefire as Hormuz attacks mount

Category: World

In the latest turn, President Trump said the United States would extend its ceasefire with Iran to allow more time for diplomacy after Pakistan asked for a pause and planned talks in Islamabad failed to come together [1][5][17][19][16]. Trump said the truce would continue until Iran submits a “unified proposal” and talks conclude, while the US blockade around Iranian ports and the Strait of Hormuz remains in place [7][15][21][6]. The extension, despite harsher rhetoric earlier in the day, lowers the immediate risk of renewed US strikes for now and keeps a narrow diplomatic opening alive [8][19][1]. At the same time, security in the Strait of Hormuz worsened. Maritime monitors and multiple news outlets said three vessels came under fire, while Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said they seized two ships and moved them toward the Iranian coast; crews were reported safe, though at least one ship sustained bridge damage [2][35][32][31][43]. Iran has not clearly endorsed Trump’s ceasefire extension, and Iranian officials said they would join new talks only if negotiations could deliver results and pressure on Iranian shipping eased. That leaves the diplomatic path uncertain even as both sides avoid an immediate return to full-scale fighting [10][13][20][25].

Key Points

  • Pakistan mediation: Pakistan welcomed the extension after Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Field Marshal Asim Munir asked Washington to hold off, positioning Islamabad as the main intermediary for a second round of talks [16][27][28].
  • Oil jitters: Energy markets stayed under pressure as Brent crude climbed back toward or above $100 a barrel in some reports, while traders and executives warned that prolonged disruption in Hormuz could strain oil and gas supplies worldwide [29][4][33].
  • Troop buildup: The US is sending additional forces to the region even as it extends the truce, underscoring that Washington is pairing diplomacy with military readiness [1][47].
  • Iran leverage: Several analyses said the status of the Strait of Hormuz is now a central point of leverage in the standoff, with Tehran and Washington each seeking an advantage around one of the world’s key shipping chokepoints [5][43][7].

Technical Details

  • Strait chokepoint: The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow maritime passage linking the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea; its geography makes naval interdiction, escort operations, and insurance risk especially consequential for global energy flows [common].
  • Naval blockade: The current US posture combines a ceasefire on direct attacks with continued blockade operations around Iranian ports and the strait, so commercial access remains constrained even without renewed large-scale bombing [21][6][7]
  • UKMTO reporting: UKMTO incident notices track vessel positions, attack types, and damage reports in near real time, which is why its alerts became a primary operational reference when ships reported gunfire and rocket-propelled grenade damage near Oman [35][48]

Industry Impact

  • Oil markets: Brent crude rose back above $100 a barrel in Financial Times reporting as the latest attacks renewed concern about supply disruptions through Hormuz [29].
  • European gas storage: European countries that normally refill gas reserves during warmer months are finding it harder to do so because summer prices have risen to winter levels [4].
  • Shipping and insurance: Container ships reported gunfire and rocket-propelled grenade damage, a mix likely to raise war-risk premiums and make carriers more cautious about using the route [35][48].

Historical Background

The Strait of Hormuz has long been one of the world’s most strategically important maritime chokepoints because a large share of global oil shipments passes through it [common]. The current crisis follows a two-month war involving Iran, the United States, and Israel, after which a Pakistan-mediated ceasefire held for two weeks before Trump announced a further extension on April 22, 2026 [10][8][15]. Since then, control of the waterway has remained a recurring bargaining point in efforts to turn a temporary truce into broader de-escalation [15][7].
