Lancet research urges regulation of digital platforms used by youth

Category: Canada

An international research series published in The Lancet urges governments to treat young people’s digital-media use as a public-health issue and address risks in platform design. University of Toronto researchers contributed to the work, which reviewed 2,790 studies involving more than nine million participants. Among children 10 and younger, heavier use was linked to less sleep and effects on cognitive, emotional and social development. For people aged 10 to 24, studies associated use with mental-health problems, reduced physical activity, substance use, self-harm and suicidal thoughts [2][8]. Researchers say parents cannot manage the risks alone and want technology companies to share user data to help assess the scale of young people’s exposure and potential harm. They also call for coordinated public-health action on digital environments and commercial design practices [2][6][8].

Key Points

  • Unequal risks: Young people with existing mental-health vulnerabilities, less offline support or lower digital literacy may face greater risks, according to the research [9].
  • Potential benefits: The review also describes benefits when digital media is used in moderation or for a specific purpose, including opportunities for learning, identity exploration and social connection [2][9].
  • Design features: Researchers and clinicians identified autoplay, push notifications, gamification and daily-use rewards as features that can encourage continued engagement [2].
  • Platform measures: TikTok’s default one-hour daily limit for users aged 13 to 17 can be changed or switched off, while Snap’s chief executive has said the company is open to teen time limits [2].

Perspectives

Rachel Mitchell, Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre: Mitchell said children under five and adolescents are especially vulnerable because their brains are still developing. She noted that digital platforms generally were not designed with young users in mind.

Sources: CBC News

Alex Beattie, Victoria University of Wellington: Beattie argued that New Zealand should be skeptical of an under-16 social-media restriction as an effective solution. He instead emphasized safer-by-design platforms, independent data access, privacy protections and youth participation.

Sources: Scoop

Barbara Prainsack, University of Vienna: Prainsack said age checks should protect privacy without collecting additional sensitive information about young people or forcing every user to identify themselves.

Sources: Die Presse

Industry Impact

  • Meta, parent company of Facebook and Instagram, agreed to implement age limits in the U.S. as part of an $18-billion settlement of a multistate class-action lawsuit [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Evidence limitations: Many studies relied on young people’s self-reported screen use and found associations rather than establishing that digital-media use caused the outcomes. Researchers say studies used inconsistent measures [9][5].
  • Population estimates: The analysis estimated that excessive screen use was associated with about 20% of depression cases and 15% of obesity cases among young people. Those figures describe associations, not proven causal shares [9].
