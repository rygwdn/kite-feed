Astronomers report Jupiter-mass exosatellite candidate around brown dwarf

Category: Science

Astronomers reported evidence for a planetary-mass exosatellite candidate in the CD-35 2722 system, a nearby stellar system about 72 to 73 light-years from Earth [1][2][6]. The system's main star is roughly half the Sun's mass and is orbited by a brown dwarf of about 37 Jupiter masses; the newly reported object appears to orbit the brown dwarf rather than the star [1][2][6]. The candidate has a minimum mass of about 0.9 Jupiter masses and an orbital period of roughly 170 days, making it far larger than the small rocky or icy moons in the Solar System [1][5][8]. The work appeared in Nature, and researchers described the object as an exosatellite because it does not fit cleanly into the usual categories of planet or moon [2][5][6]. Researchers inferred the object from a radial-velocity wobble in the brown dwarf's motion, not from a direct image [5][7][8]. SpaceDaily reported that the paper presents evidence for a candidate that still needs further observation, while Futurism cited the researchers as saying no exomoon has yet been confidently detected despite more than 6,000 exoplanet discoveries [8][2].

Did you know: The Solar System has 891 known natural satellites, but astronomers have confirmed thousands of exoplanets without a universally accepted exomoon detection [7][5].

Key Points

  • Telescope role: ESO's Very Large Telescope contributed to the discovery, Sci.News reported [1].
  • Detection method: Radial-velocity analysis measures small shifts in motion caused by gravity, the same broad technique CNET says astronomers used in the first exoplanet detections [5].
  • Host object: A brown dwarf is more massive than the largest gas giants but not massive enough to sustain hydrogen fusion like a star [2][6][7].
  • Object size: CNET identified the potential moon-like object as CD-35 2722 B and said it is larger than Saturn [5].

Perspectives
