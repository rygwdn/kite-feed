Astronomers trace disappearing exoplanet to an unseen planetary neighbor

Category: Science

Astronomers say the giant exoplanet TOI-2535 b stopped transiting its star because an unseen companion’s gravity caused its orbit to tilt and wobble, taking it out of Earth’s line of sight. The planet, about 1,100 light-years away, still orbits its star; researchers tracked its transits for seven years before they vanished in March 2026 [1][4][7]. Astronomers have not observed the companion directly, but changes in the timing and shape of TOI-2535 b’s transits point to a second giant planet, possibly at least as massive as Saturn. The pair’s orbital interaction is unusually strong, and the cause of their tilted orbits remains unclear. Researchers expect TOI-2535 b’s transits to become visible again in 30 to 50 years, while the companion could appear sooner [2][1][6].

Key Points

  • Timing shifts: TOI-2535 b’s transits arrived as much as six hours early or late and grew shorter and fainter before disappearing [2][6][7].
  • Orbit angles: The planets’ orbits are tilted at least five degrees relative to each other, a larger tilt than in most known multi-planet systems [1][4][5].
  • Detection yield: The transit method has identified about 4,800 of nearly 6,500 confirmed exoplanets by measuring dips in starlight rather than imaging planets directly [1][4][6].
  • Orbital period: TOI-2535 b completes an orbit in just over eight days, compared with Mercury’s 88-day orbit around the Sun [1][4][7].

Timeline

  • 2019: TESS observations first revealed TOI-2535 b’s transits [1][5]
  • March 2026: The planet’s transits moved out of view from Earth [4][6]
  • 9 November 2026: The study appeared in The Astrophysical Journal Letters [7][3]

Technical Details

  • Transit timing variations: Researchers combined changes in the timing of TOI-2535 b’s transits with measurements of the star’s motion to infer the presence of a companion exerting a gravitational pull [2][6].
  • Long-term stability models: Reports of the simulations differ: ScienceAlert describes a million-year model in which orbital resonance may stabilize the pair, while Folha reports that two-thirds of 100 tested solutions became unstable over that span [1][3].

Scientific Significance

  • First observed case: Astronomers observed a confirmed exoplanet’s transits disappear for the first time; researchers had expected such a change to take decades or centuries [2][6].
