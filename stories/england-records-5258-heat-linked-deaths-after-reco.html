England records 5,258 heat-linked deaths after record-hot summer

Category: Science

England recorded an estimated 5,258 deaths associated with extreme heat between May and September 2026, the highest total since the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) began monitoring in 2016 and nearly twice the previous record of 2,985 in 2022 [6][8]. The figures are provisional because death-registration data for the period are incomplete [6]. The agency counted seven heat episodes lasting a total of 41 days, both records since monitoring began [5][8][6]. The deadliest stretch was June 21-28, when heat was associated with an estimated 2,401 deaths; more than 3,000 deaths were linked to episodes in May and June combined [5][6]. Heat can trigger heatstroke and dehydration or worsen cardiovascular and respiratory conditions [5]. Dr Ross Thompson, UKHSA’s principal environmental health scientist, said periods of extreme heat are expected to become longer, more frequent and more intense as the climate warms [6].

The numbers are a stark reminder of the human impact of climate change, and that is why I have asked the NHS to now prepare for summer as seriously as we do for winter.

— Health Secretary Yvette Cooper (BBC)

Key Points

  • European toll: Researchers estimated more than 50,000 heat-related deaths in urban areas across 30 European countries this summer, the highest total in their data since 2000 [5][7].
  • Health alert: England issued its second-ever red heat-health alert during the June heatwave [6].
  • Other impacts: England recorded its driest July, with 7mm of rain. Drought was declared across all of Wales and more than two-thirds of England, and wildfires caused damage [6][8].
  • 2050 projection: Emma Howard Boyd, chair of the National Heat Risk Commission, contrasted the 2026 toll with a Climate Change Committee projection of up to 10,000 heat-associated deaths annually across the UK by 2050 [6].

Perspectives

UK Health Security Agency: The agency said heat precautions should begin at the start of summer because people and public services may be less prepared for early-season heat, and urged longer-term adaptation of homes, neighbourhoods and health and care settings [6].

Sources: The Guardian

Greenpeace UK: Climate campaigner Maja Darlington called the deaths a policy choice and urged the government to make fossil fuel companies pay for adaptation, while warning against approving Rosebank drilling, weakening electric-vehicle targets and expanding Heathrow [6].

Sources: The Guardian

National Heat Risk Commission: Chair Emma Howard Boyd said the figures show an urgent public-health risk and argued that the necessary responses are already known [6].
