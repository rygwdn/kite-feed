Tooth proteins suggest Homo naledi remains came from females

Category: Science

Researchers analyzing ancient proteins from Homo naledi teeth found no male-specific protein markers in fossils from South Africa’s Rising Star cave system, suggesting the sampled remains may all be female [1][2][3][4][8][11]. The study, published in Cell, examined peptides from 23 fossil teeth representing about 20 individuals and focused on dental enamel, which can preserve protein fragments in ancient remains [1][3][8][11]. Homo naledi, an extinct relative of Homo sapiens, was first described after discoveries in the Rising Star cave system from 2013 to 2015 and lived in what is now South Africa roughly 335,000 to 241,000 years ago [1][10][11]. Researchers said the result could help explain why fossils in the Dinaledi Chamber showed little of the male-female physical variation normally expected in a group. They also said it adds evidence to claims that the remains were deliberately placed in the cave rather than accumulating there by chance [1][3][4][8].

Key Points

  • Sample range: The tested teeth came from individuals ranging from infants to older adults, including specimens with heavy tooth wear [8].
  • Statistical rarity: Lee Berger said the chance of sampling 20 individuals from one sex was “quite literally one in a million” [8].
  • Body mosaic: Researchers describe Homo naledi as having a small brain and an upper body more like those of earlier australopithecines, along with a more human-like face, hands and lower limbs [2][9].
  • Research scale: The University of York said the fossils represent the largest extinct hominin population yet examined through protein analysis [11].

Perspectives

Lee Berger: Berger told Live Science the result surprised the team and said Homo naledi “has always been an enigmatic discovery.”

Sources: Live Science

ScienceAlert: ScienceAlert highlighted the main caveat: the absence of Amelogenin-Y does not prove every Rising Star individual lacked a Y chromosome.

Timeline

  • 2013: Rising Star project began finding Homo naledi fossils [2][9]
  • 2015: Researchers published the new species description [10]
  • 2023: Team proposed possible fire use and deliberate burials [2][9]
  • 24 June 2026: Cell study reported no male-specific tooth proteins [1]

Technical Details

  • Amelogenin markers: Researchers searched dental enamel for Amelogenin-X, found in both biological sexes, and Amelogenin-Y, a Y-chromosome-linked peptide associated with male biological sex. None of the tested samples contained the Amelogenin-Y signature [3][11].
  • Enamel proteomics: Researchers used a minimally destructive acid-etching technique to extract microscopic peptide fragments from teeth, allowing protein analysis while limiting damage to rare fossils [1][11].

Historical Background

The Rising Star cave system, a UNESCO World Heritage site in South Africa's Cradle of Humankind, lies about 30 meters underground and can be reached only through narrow passages that required specially trained cavers [9][8]. Initial excavations yielded hominin fossils from at least 15 individuals, making it one of the richest early human sites ever found [10][3]. The site is central to debates about early hominin behavior; some researchers have proposed that the remains were intentionally placed in the remote chamber [2][9].
