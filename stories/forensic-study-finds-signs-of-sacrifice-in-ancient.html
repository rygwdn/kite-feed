Forensic study finds signs of sacrifice in ancient Egyptian royal burials

Category: Science

A forensic re-examination of 46 skulls excavated near First Dynasty royal tombs at Umm el-Qaab, Egypt, identified potentially fatal injuries in 18, strengthening the case that some people buried there were killed as retainers after a ruler’s death [2][4][8]. The skulls came from burials around the tombs of King Djer, King Djet and Queen Merneith; many graves were associated with royal officials and artisans [4][12]. British Egyptologist Flinders Petrie excavated the graves in the 1920s and suspected retainer sacrifice, but could not identify fatal injuries. Roselyn Campbell of Purdue University interpreted the newly identified trauma as evidence that at least some people died violently, but stressed that the sample is small and cannot establish what happened to everyone buried there [4][10]. Petrie reburied most of the remains, and Campbell said she would like to locate and analyze them [6][2].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Sharp-force injuries: Some skulls also showed sharp-force injuries, including a penetrating wound to the side of the head and a chopping injury to the eye [2].
  • Burial evidence: Some subsidiary graves appear to have been closed in clusters rather than as separate interments over time, a pattern Petrie considered suspicious [10][8].
  • Ritual change: The subsidiary burials appear to have dwindled and disappeared near the start of the Second Dynasty, when statuettes took the place of human attendants [4][12].
  • Unresolved cases: The remaining skulls showed no clear cranial trauma, but researchers said some people could have died naturally or by methods that leave no skeletal marks [6][8].

Perspectives

Roselyn Campbell, Purdue University: Campbell cautioned against imposing modern assumptions about violence on ancient societies and said researchers must account for the legacy of European and colonial archaeology when interpreting old collections [10].

Sources: Ars Technica

Salima Ikram, American University in Cairo: Ikram said the study applies rigorous current methods to remains that have rarely been examined directly, and suggested some retainers may have viewed service after death as an honor while others may have feared or resisted it [4].

Sources: The Guardian

Dawn McCormack, Samford University: McCormack said similar sacrifices occurred in other cultures and warned that not everyone involved at the Egyptian site may have accepted the practice [4].

Sources: The Guardian

Timeline

  • c. 3078–2900 BCE: The First Dynasty spanned this approximate period [2]
  • 1920s: Flinders Petrie excavated the royal cemetery’s subsidiary graves [4]
  • 5 October 2026: Campbell’s forensic findings appeared in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology [2][4]

Technical Details

  • Perimortem trauma: Researchers distinguished damage to fresh bone from later breaks to identify fractures that occurred around the time of death. This distinction helped them reinterpret injuries Petrie had regarded as natural damage [8][10].
  • Weapon inference: Researchers inferred that linear fractures were consistent with impact from a flat, wide object, while a mace would be expected to leave depressed fractures. The study does not identify a specific weapon [4][8].

Scientific Significance

  • Limits and further work: Assessing biological sex from skulls alone is uncertain; pelvic bones would be needed for more conclusive identifications [10].
