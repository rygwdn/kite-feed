New fossils and trackways sharpen view of dinosaur evolution

Category: Science

Three new paleontology reports are giving scientists a sharper look at how dinosaurs evolved and moved—especially on the smaller end of the size spectrum. In Patagonia, researchers describe a nearly complete skeleton of the tiny alvarezsaur Alnashetri cerropoliciensis, and they’re using it to rethink how this bird-like group evolved. Their analysis suggests miniaturization came before some later specializations [3]. A newly described small herbivore, Foskeia, is also pushing researchers to revisit parts of the dinosaur family tree and the relationships among early plant-eaters [1]. Footprints add a behavioral angle. In Colorado, scientists analyzed a rare looping trackway dated to about 150 million years ago that suggests a large sauropod repeatedly walked in circles. They also report that the footprint pattern may point to a limp [2]. Taken together, the studies show how fresh specimens and trace fossils can steadily refine big evolutionary stories—sometimes from surprisingly small clues [3][1][2].

Key Points

  • Rare anatomy window: A nearly complete Alnashetri cerropoliciensis skeleton from Patagonia gives researchers unusually detailed anatomy for this alvarezsaur lineage [3].
  • Trait timing focus: Using the new fossil, the team maps hallmark alvarezsaur traits onto an evolutionary timeline instead of treating them as an all-at-once package [3].
  • Trackway geometry: In Colorado, scientists examined an unusual looping set of sauropod footprints—rarely preserved in this kind of repeated pattern—rather than a single straight crossing [2].
  • Injury clues from traces: The Colorado study argues the footprint pattern fits a limp, a reminder that trackways can hint at injuries that bones don’t always capture [2].
  • Early herbivore relationships: Foskeia is described as a small herbivorous dinosaur that could reshape how some early plant-eaters are grouped on the dinosaur family tree [1].

Scientific Significance

  • Scientific meaning: Because the Patagonia specimen is nearly complete, it offers a stronger anatomical baseline for alvarezsaurs and helps researchers test competing ideas about when distinctive features emerged in the lineage [3].
  • Scientific meaning: The Colorado sauropod footprints add locomotion evidence that body fossils alone can’t provide, giving researchers another way to evaluate movement and gait in giant dinosaurs [2].
  • Limitations and future work: The sources treat the behavior and limp interpretation as suggestive; these kinds of inferences usually get stronger with more tracksites and broader comparative analysis [2].
