President Trump threatens to block Exxon from Venezuela

Category: Business

President Trump said he is “inclined” to keep Exxon Mobil out of Venezuela-related oil opportunities after a White House meeting in which he disliked the company’s response to his initiative encouraging US firms to invest there [2][4][8]. The comments followed Exxon CEO Darren Woods expressing skepticism, calling Venezuela “uninvestable” in its current condition [2][3][1]. The episode underscores how political risk and corporate risk discipline are colliding as Washington looks at a post-Maduro Venezuela and potential rebuilding of its petroleum industry [5][8].

Primary Source: View original article

They're playing too cute

— President Donald Trump (CNBC)

Did you know: “Uninvestable” is a term executives sometimes use to indicate that risks (legal, operational, or political) cannot be priced to an acceptable return, rather than simply that expected profits look low [common].

Key Points

  • CEO skepticism: Darren Woods’ stance that Venezuela is “uninvestable” reflects concerns about rule-of-law and operating conditions that can override resource potential in oil investment decisions [2][3][1].
  • White House tone: President Trump portrayed Exxon as insufficiently enthusiastic, saying the company was “playing too cute” as he discussed Venezuela plans [3].
  • Rebuild push: Trump framed his approach as a push for US oil majors to help rebuild Venezuela’s petroleum industry after Maduro’s ouster, tying market access to participation in that effort [8][5].
  • Policy uncertainty: The threat to “exclude” a specific company from potential deals highlights policy-driven uncertainty that can affect which firms pursue country re-entry and on what terms [4][8].
  • High stakes framing: Trump’s comments referenced a “$100 billion plan” for Venezuela in describing the scale of what he wants companies to consider [2].

Technical Details

  • Country political risk: For oil projects, political risk includes contract sanctity, sanctions exposure, and regulatory stability—factors implied by Exxon’s “uninvestable” assessment [2][1].
  • Upstream investment cycle: Large oil developments require multi-year, capital-intensive commitments, so executives often demand durable legal and fiscal terms before re-entering high-risk jurisdictions [common].
  • Market access via policy: Trump’s threat to exclude a firm shows how governments can shape participation in resource projects through licensing, approvals, and diplomatic leverage [8][4].

Industry Impact

  • Oil majors’ strategy: Public pressure tied to market access may prompt large producers to clarify how they weigh political stability versus resource opportunity when evaluating frontier re-entry [1][8].
  • Dealmaking dynamics: If participation is conditioned on government preferences, smaller or less-exposed firms could gain negotiating room versus incumbents that are more sensitive to US political scrutiny [4][8].
  • Venezuela services chain: Any rebuilding push would likely lift demand for drilling, field services, and infrastructure work—though timing depends on investment decisions by majors and policy clarity [8][5].

Historical Background

Venezuela holds some of the world’s largest proven oil reserves, but years of sanctions, underinvestment, and operational decline have constrained output and deterred many international oil companies [common]. US policy toward Venezuelan oil has often shifted alongside political developments in Caracas, creating a high premium on stability and legal protections for long-lived energy projects [common]. The current reporting situates Trump’s investment push in the wake of the toppling of former President Nicolás Maduro, which outlets describe as reshaping the country’s investment outlook—though companies disagree on how quickly risks can be priced and managed [5][8].
