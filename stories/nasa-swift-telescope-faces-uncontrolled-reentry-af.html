NASA Swift telescope faces uncontrolled reentry after failed orbit rescue

Category: Science

NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory is expected to reenter Earth’s atmosphere in the coming days after a failed attempt to raise its orbit. Forecasts place reentry roughly between Nov. 3 and Nov. 6, but the timing and location remain uncertain [2][3][5]. The U.S. Space Force is tracking the 1.5-ton spacecraft. Most of it is expected to burn up, although some dense tungsten components and mirror parts could survive. The possible reentry corridor spans a broad band near the equator, much of it over ocean [9][3]. Solar activity increased atmospheric drag and accelerated the telescope’s orbital decline. Swift, launched in 2004 for a two-year mission, detected more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and 1,400 exploding stars and rapidly alerted other observatories to transient events [1][9]. It continued observations after the rescue attempt was abandoned, but NASA shut down its systems in late October when its low orbit made stable operations impossible [5][8].

Primary Source: View original article

We’ve known this day would come since before Swift was launched. At least in low-Earth orbit, what goes up must come down. And that end is catastrophic

— John Nousek, Pennsylvania State University astronomer who led Swift’s control team (Associated Press)

Key Points

  • Rescue attempt: NASA hired Arizona-based Katalyst Space for the effort. The company’s Link robotic craft launched in July, encountered control problems and reentered in September [3][9].
  • Orbit corridor: Swift’s orbit is tilted about 21 degrees from the equator, limiting possible reentry to a latitude band that includes parts of Hawaii, Puerto Rico and southern Mexico but consists mostly of ocean and desert [3].
  • Debris assessment: Aerospace engineer Dean Sladen said no detailed Swift-specific public risk assessment appeared to have been released. He noted that NASA’s orbital-debris rules set a human-casualty risk limit of one in 10,000 [2].
  • More reentries: Space-debris specialist Darren McKnight said the number of objects reentering annually is now 10 times higher than five years ago, driven by low-Earth-orbit constellations and technology replacement cycles [2].

Perspectives

John Nousek, Swift control-team leader: Experience from the mission could help future space missions make discoveries more quickly, Nousek said [8].

Darren McKnight, LeoLabs senior technical fellow: McKnight said Swift would likely disintegrate enough during reentry to pose little risk of casualties on the ground. He said he was more concerned about the rapid abandonment of large objects above 400 miles [2].

Timeline

  • 20 November 2004: Swift launched on a mission originally designed to last two years [9][1]
  • July 2026: Katalyst Space launched Link to attempt an orbital rescue [3][9]
  • September 2026: Link reentered after the rescue effort was abandoned [9][5]
  • 20 October 2026: Swift was at 171 miles altitude and could no longer offset drag [2][3]
  • October 2026: NASA ended operations after the observatory became too low for stable observations [8][5]
  • 3 November 2026: One forecast window begins for Swift’s uncontrolled atmospheric reentry [3]

Technical Details

  • Atmospheric breakup: Aerospace engineer Dean Sladen said that once Swift falls to about 120 kilometers, the end usually comes within a few minutes; air in front of it heats enough for it to glow, and the main body breaks apart around 78 kilometers [2].
  • Likely surviving hardware: Sladen identified Swift’s six heavy reaction wheels and tougher telescope components, such as lenses and mirrors, as hardware that could survive reentry. He said the aluminum body, wiring and electronics are more likely to melt [2].

Scientific Significance

  • Research reach: Swift’s observations contributed to thousands of scientific papers [3].
  • Beyond explosions: Swift also observed comets, asteroids and black holes tearing stars apart [9][5].

Q&A

Q: Why could the reentry forecast still change?

A: Swift’s orbit is decaying rapidly, and unpredictable changes in upper-atmosphere density can add drag; the reentry time and location may not become clear until the final hours [3].
