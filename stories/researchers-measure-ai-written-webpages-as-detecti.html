Researchers measure AI-written webpages as detection tools emerge

Category: AI

Researchers and analysts published measurements and tools for tracking AI-generated writing online. One academic project found that by mid-2025 roughly 35% of newly published websites in its sample were classified as AI-generated or AI-assisted, up from zero before ChatGPT’s public launch in late 2022 [3]. A separate Pew Research Center analysis examined signs of AI authorship in nearly half a million English-language webpages from the Common Crawl archive [4]. The academic project, led by Jonas Dolezal, Sawood Alam, Mark Graham and Maty Bohacek, reported evidence that rising AI-generated text correlates with lower semantic diversity and more positive sentiment. It found no statistically significant evidence that AI-generated text reduces factual accuracy or stylistic diversity [3]. Pew said the upward trend began after OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022 and continued as chatbots including Claude and Gemini followed; Pew also reported that around half of U.S. adults use AI chatbots, including 24% who use them daily [4]. Developers have built consumer-facing detection tools, including the open-source Stop the Slop browser extension, which analyzes YouTube transcripts and flags likely AI-generated scripts or robotic narration with thumbnail badges, in-player indicators, sentence breakdowns and local or edge caching [1]. Separate essays by Arnold Kling and Benedict Evans argued that AI’s effect should be assessed against earlier computing shifts and the practical realities of enterprise software adoption, rather than by model capability alone [2][5].

Key Points

  • Detection method: Pew used Open Pangram, a machine-learning detector that checks language patterns, words, phrases and linguistic quirks more common in AI-written text [4].
  • Archive sampling: The academic study used the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine to collect a representative sample of websites published between 2022 and 2025 [3].
  • YouTube focus: Stop the Slop targets YouTube scripts specifically, showing AI probability ratings on thumbnails before users click and a verdict popover inside the player [1].
  • Enterprise limits: Evans argued large companies often have hundreds or thousands of software systems and may not know exactly what they use or pay for, complicating claims that AI will replace existing workflows [5].
  • Web comparison: Kling rated AI a 6 on a 1-to-10 disruption scale, below the 1990s Web at 8 and the Industrial Revolution at 10 [2].

Technical Details

  • Open Pangram detector: Pew ran webpage text through Open Pangram, which classifies likely AI authorship by identifying language patterns, words, phrases and quirks associated with AI systems [4].
  • Transcript-level signals: Stop the Slop breaks YouTube transcripts into sentences and highlights passages that triggered repetitive phrasing or AI markers, rather than only showing a single page-level score [1].
  • Caching design: The extension uses local and edge caching so previously evaluated videos can return verdicts without redundant network requests [1].

Industry Impact

  • Online video: Stop the Slop brings AI-script detection into YouTube feeds and video players, targeting creators’ transcripts and narration rather than conventional webpages [1].
  • Enterprise software: Evans described large American companies as running hundreds or thousands of software tools, a condition that could slow attempts to replace existing systems with AI-generated workflows [5].

Scientific Significance

  • Measured prevalence: The academic estimate of roughly 35% AI-generated or AI-assisted newly published websites by mid-2025 and Pew’s analysis of sampled English webpages in July 2026 differ in scope, archive source and sampling design, so they should not be treated as interchangeable measures [3][4].
  • Observed discourse shifts: The academic study said its results diverged from public perception, which often assumes AI degrades factual accuracy and diversity, while the data showed only reduced semantic diversity and increased positive sentiment [3].
  • Detection limits: Both projects rely on classifiers to infer AI authorship from text patterns, so their results depend on model accuracy, sampling choices and how well detectors generalize across domains and writing styles [3][4].

Business Angle

  • Widespread AI-written content may commoditize low-end web publishing, making distribution, trusted brands and first-party audience relationships more important to publishers [4][common].
  • AI could shift enterprise software from static tools to dynamic, on-demand creation, but organizational inertia may still slow adoption [5].
  • Stop the Slop shows user demand for AI detection inside platforms like YouTube, suggesting a market for built-in verification features [1].

Action Items
