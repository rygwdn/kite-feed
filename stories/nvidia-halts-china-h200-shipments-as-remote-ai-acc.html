Nvidia halts China H200 shipments as remote AI access faces curbs

Category: AI

Nvidia has stopped shipping licensed Hopper data-center chips to China, interim chief executive Colette Kress said Tuesday. U.S. rules issued this week also target Chinese companies’ remote access to advanced chips in overseas data centers [18][29][5]. Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper products to China in the third quarter, but its guidance no longer includes those sales [18]. The restrictions have also affected plans to build AI-computing capacity in Southeast Asia. Singapore cloud provider Aolani Cloud’s proposed $480 million loan was put on hold. The money was intended to buy Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs) for a Malaysian data center that TikTok owner ByteDance planned to access remotely. Five banks discussed withdrawing or delaying participation over potential violations of U.S. rules; DBS Group was arranging the loan [46]. Chinese AI developers are also using another method to build domestic systems: A Reuters review of more than 300 documents found industrial use of “distillation,” in which U.S. models generate material to train smaller Chinese models. The technique is legal, but cannot reproduce a leading model’s full capabilities or eliminate the need for advanced computing [13][71]. Congress is expected to consider three export-control bills as part of the annual defense authorization package in December [6].

Key Points

  • Manufacturing push: Beijing is encouraging distillation to make AI cheaper and easier to deploy across China’s manufacturing sector [13][71].
  • Congressional bills: The AI Overwatch Act, MATCH Act and Chip Security Act were among measures previously attached to the Senate defense bill; lawmakers are pushing to include them in the package expected in December [6].
  • Nvidia oversight: Nvidia formed a board committee on national security and export controls, chaired by director Suzanne Nora Johnson, a former Goldman Sachs executive [26].

Perspectives

Ryan Fedasiuk, a fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation, described the remote-access measure as a long-overdue step to address Chinese use of large computing hubs in Southeast Asia. He said it reflected the administration’s attention to security risks from access to computing power [5].

Technical Details

  • Aggregate total processing performance (TPP) threshold: Remote access requires a license above 12 million aggregate TPP; capacity across interconnected data centers may be combined [38][66].
  • Customer ownership checks: The controls cover firms headquartered in restricted countries and entities whose ultimate parent is based there, extending checks beyond where a customer is registered [38][66].

Historical Background

U.S. export controls have restricted Chinese firms’ direct access to advanced AI hardware since 2022. Renting capacity at data centers in third countries gave some companies a way to use U.S. processors remotely [62][5].

International Reactions

  • 🇨🇳 China: China’s Commerce Ministry threatened countermeasures and said the U.S. rules undermine semiconductor supply-chain stability [40][5].
  • 🇸🇬 Singapore: The government said it was consulting affected businesses and U.S. authorities on how the restrictions will be implemented and how legitimate firms can retain trusted chip access [53].

Q&A

Q: Does the rule cover ordinary online recommendation systems?

A: Some systems used exclusively for commercial recommendations, including e-commerce or social-media recommendations, can be exempt under specified conditions; generative AI systems are not covered by that exemption [66][48].

Q: Which countries face the separate licensing requirement for advanced-chip shipments?

A: The rule adds U.S. licensing requirements for advanced-chip shipments to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil [5].
