Northern Taurids to peak before November Leonids

Category: Science

The Northern Taurid meteor shower is expected to peak overnight Nov. 11-12, giving skywatchers another chance to see bright fireballs after the Southern Taurids’ recent peak. Under ideal conditions, the Northern Taurids may produce about five meteors an hour. The moon is forecast to be only about 5% illuminated, compared with about 22% during the Southern Taurid peak, leaving darker skies [7][5]. The new moon falls Nov. 9 [14]. The Leonids are forecast to peak overnight Nov. 17-18, with a theoretical rate of about 15 meteors an hour under ideal conditions. Observers in North America should watch in the early hours of both Nov. 17 and Nov. 18 [3][14]. The moon is expected to set before the best early-morning viewing period [14]. Mars and Jupiter will also appear close together before sunrise around Nov. 15-17 [14][6].

It doesn’t have lots and lots of meteors, but sometimes it gets some of the bigger and slower and more interesting ones.

Key Points

  • Leonid speed: Leonid meteors cross the atmosphere at about 44 miles (71 kilometers) per second, making them among the fastest meteors [3].
  • Viewing conditions: Observers can see meteors without a telescope or binoculars. They should choose a dark location and allow their eyes about 30 minutes to adjust [3][5].
  • Four planets: Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter are forecast to align in the southeast, with the best views 45-60 minutes before sunrise in mid-to-late November [6].
  • Full moon: November’s full moon is expected on Nov. 24, the first of three consecutive supermoons extending through December and January [5][10].

Timeline

  • 4 November 2026: Southern Taurids began their predicted fireball-peak period [15]
  • 9 November 2026: New moon brings the month’s darkest skies [14]
  • 11 November 2026: Northern Taurids reach their expected overnight peak [7][5]
  • 17 November 2026: Leonids reach peak activity overnight into Nov. 18 [3][14]

Historical Background

The Leonids come from debris left by comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, which completes an orbit around the sun about every 33 years [3]. In 1966, a Leonid storm produced one of the most extraordinary meteor displays on record; some accounts described rates of 40 meteors per second [3].

Q&A

Q: Can observers see Leonids from the Southern Hemisphere?

A: The shower is best placed for Northern Hemisphere viewers but is also visible from many Southern Hemisphere locations [3].
