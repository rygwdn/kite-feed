Annular solar eclipse draws ‘ring of fire’ over Antarctica

Category: Science

On Feb. 17, 2026, an annular solar eclipse—often called a “ring of fire”—swept across Antarctica and nearby Southern Ocean waters, with partial phases visible from parts of the Southern Hemisphere [3][4][8]. The effect happens when the Moon passes in front of the Sun but looks slightly smaller in the sky, leaving a bright ring of sunlight around the Moon’s silhouette [7][5].

Primary Source: View original article

Key Points

  • Peak duration: Along the centerline of the eclipse track, the annular phase lasted up to about 2 minutes and 20 seconds [8].
  • Where it reached: EclipseWise traced annularity over Antarctica as well as the Southern Ocean and South Indian Ocean, with partial eclipse visibility including parts of Africa, Argentina, and Chile [8].
  • Timing context: Scientific American described the Antarctica event as 2026’s first solar eclipse [3].

Perspectives

Scientific American: The outlet called it the first solar eclipse of 2026 and said only a “lucky few” in the Southern Hemisphere could see the ring of fire directly, while pointing readers to the next eclipse opportunities.

Sources: Scientific American

Space.com: Space.com focused on the eclipse geometry and what viewers could expect, explaining how annular eclipses leave a bright solar ring instead of the Sun disappearing completely.

Sources: Space.com

Historical Background

EclipseWise described the Feb. 17, 2026 event as an annular eclipse visible from Antarctica for the first time since 2014 [8].
